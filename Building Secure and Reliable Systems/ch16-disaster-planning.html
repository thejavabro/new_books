Disaster Planning

You can't plan for every disaster, but you can plan for the response. Know your risks, name your team, prestage your tools, and rehearse until the plan works.

1

Disasters start as smoke, then unfold in phases

You rarely meet a disaster fully ablaze; you first notice smoke. Plans that are flexible, and practiced until they are second nature, adapt to whatever the fire turns out to be. Split the response into phases, and define exit criteria early so everyone knows when the incident is over.

2

Name the team, and remove single points of failure

Whether the incident response team is dedicated, a virtual cross-functional team or a mix, roles must be clear: incident commander, SREs, security engineers, legal, communications. Incidents ignore vacations and time zones. Tap people to send them on holiday and look for gaps in coverage.

Charter and moraleA team charter starts with a one-sentence mission (what incidents the team handles), then scope and how to engage it. Keep workload realistic: overworked responders make mistakes and leave.
3

Severity is fixed; priority moves

Use two related scales, say 0 (worst) to 4. Severity is how bad the impact is and normally stays fixed once understood. Priority is how fast to work and changes over the incident. Shared models keep every team at the same tempo. Pick an incident and move through time.

4

Write the plans, then prestage everything

Response plans guide responders through reporting, triage, SLOs, roles, communication and escalation. Playbooks give step-by-step instructions for specific tasks. Store them somewhere that still works when your servers don't. Then prestage systems and people. How ready are you?

5

Test the plans, the systems, and the people

An untested plan is a hypothesis. Tap each testing approach, then run a short tabletop exercise.
