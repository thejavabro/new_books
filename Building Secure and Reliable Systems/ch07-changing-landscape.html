Design for a Changing Landscape

Threats, dependencies and requirements never stop moving. Design so that a change can be made quickly, safely and in small steps, because you will have to make many.

Changes come at different speeds

New vulnerabilities, new regulations, new user expectations: systems must change often and quickly while staying reliable. The chapter sorts security changes by timeline, and each needs a different playbook. Pick one.

2

Six properties of a safe change

Security changes follow the same release engineering rules as any other change. Tick the properties your change has and see how risky the rollout looks. The chapter's advice is "slow and steady": the trade of speed for safety is usually worth it.

3

Architectures that make change easier

Four habits make both routine improvements and emergency fixes cheaper. Tap each.

A frontend layer as a single point of change

tap a fix

Google Front End terminates TLS and absorbs attacks in front of most services. A fix applied there protects everything behind it, and backends that aren't ready (say, for IPv6) don't block the rollout.

4

Short term: patch the fleet before the exploit spreads

When Shellshock, a trivially exploitable bash bug, was published in 2014, exploits appeared the same day. Google triaged by risk: the huge standardized server fleet was low risk and easy to patch automatically; workstations were higher risk but also quick; a small set of nonstandard systems needed manual work from their owners. Run the response and choose your strategy.

Before chasing the zero-dayThe newest bug is rarely the one most exploited. Make sure the well-known "top hits" are patched first.
5

Medium term: replacing one-time codes with security keys

One-time passcodes can be phished. From 2011 Google evaluated stronger second factors, chose hardware security keys (helping design the FIDO standard), began rolling them out in 2013 and turned off the old codes in 2015. Step through how a medium-term change rolls out.

6

Long term: moving the web to HTTPS

Some changes take years and depend on people outside your organization. Chrome, Let's Encrypt and others pushed the web to HTTPS through research, gradual browser warnings, outreach in many languages and business arguments (new web features required HTTPS). Chrome users went from spending as little as 37% (Android) or 70% (Windows) of browsing time on HTTPS to over 90% everywhere. Drag the timeline.

7

When the embargo breaks

Embargoed vulnerabilities are kept secret until a coordinated date. Heartbleed (2014) let anyone read 64 KB of a server's memory, including private keys. Google had quietly patched key TLS-facing systems before the date, but the bug went public early with a name, a logo and a website, exploit code followed fast, and everyone else had to patch and rotate keys in a hurry. Choose how prepared you were.
