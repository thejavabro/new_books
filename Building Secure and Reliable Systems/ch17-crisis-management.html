Crisis Management

When the incident is real and possibly an attack, structure beats heroics. An incident commander, clear roles and careful communication turn chaos into a plan.

1

Is it a crisis or not?

Most escalations are not crises. Triage asks: is this a false positive, an easily fixed problem, or a complex, damaging one such as a targeted compromise? The answer depends on your defenses. The chapter imagines the same ransomware alert in three organizations. Pick one.

Vulnerabilities are incidents tooA newly reported critical bug may need the same machinery as an intrusion, plus coordinated vulnerability disclosure: fix fast enough to protect users, while keeping the details quiet until a fix is available.
2

Don't panic. Declare, and take command

Like firefighters, responders are trained not to run at the scene. Under Google's incident management model (IMAG, based on the Incident Command System), the person declaring an incident becomes the incident commander (IC) or explicitly hands the role to someone who explicitly accepts. Click roles to build the team.

3

Operational security: don't tip off the attacker

If the attacker can see your response, they may go quiet (hiding footholds you'll miss) or destroy what they can on the way out. Decide your OpSec rules before any incident. Sort these actions.

When to give up secrecyIf the risk is imminent and severe, act openly. In early 2019 Apple cut off access to its FaceTime servers for about a day after an easily exploitable privacy bug was disclosed, until fixes shipped, a decision widely seen as right. Calls like that belong with leadership, which is why the team needs a management liaison.
4

Backtrack the attack, in parallel tracks

Investigators reconstruct each step of the attack: forensic images of disks (read-only, checksummed), memory, logs, and timelines across systems. With enough people, shard the work into parallel tracks and let the IC keep the whole picture.

5

Handovers, morale, and food

Crises last longer than anyone can stay sharp. Plan shifts and handovers (follow the sun if you can), watch morale, and feed people. Drag the clock and watch the team.

6

Communication is the hardest part

Misunderstandings, hedging and too many (or too few) meetings sink responses. Be explicit, repeat back assignments, and say how confident you are without burying the answer. Compare.

hedged

"I mean, it's possible the servers might be mostly OK, probably, but there could be some stuff we haven't looked at, so I wouldn't want to say for sure…"

clear

"We're confident about servers, NAS, email and file shares. We're not confident about hosted systems: we have less log visibility there."
