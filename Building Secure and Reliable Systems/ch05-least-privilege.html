Design for Least Privilege

Give every person and every program the smallest set of permissions, for the shortest time, that still lets the job get done. Then make that cheap enough that people actually do it.

1

Least privilege: smallest scope, shortest time

Least privilege means every person and program gets only the access needed for the task at hand, and only while they need it. It guards against attackers with stolen credentials, malicious insiders, and the far more common honest mistake. Adjust a role's access and watch the blast radius of one compromised account.

2

Not all access is equal

Protecting everything to the maximum is unaffordable and unusable. Classify data and actions by impact, then match the controls. Here is a three-tier example similar to the chapter's. Tap a cell.

Reliability's view vs. security's viewReliability engineers worry first about who can change infrastructure (stop jobs, edit ACLs). Security engineers point out that merely reading sensitive data, or the keys, can be just as damaging. A good classification covers both.
3

Small functional APIs make everything easier

The single best move for least privilege is to replace broad administrative access (a shell) with small APIs that each do one job. A tiny API can be authorized precisely, audited meaningfully and approved by someone who knows exactly what they're approving. Compare ways for automation to push a web server's config file.

4

When yes/no isn't enough

Between "allow" and "deny" lies a useful "maybe, if...": a second approver, a ticket number, an approval on a separate hardened phone, or access that expires. Pick the controls and try three different requests against a user-data API.

Usability is part of securityIf finding an approver is painful, people paste generic justifications or rubber-stamp each other. Approval prompts must show exactly who is doing what, and the friction must be low enough that nobody routes around it.
5

Breakglass, auditing and denials

Every strict system needs an emergency override, a breakglass, that bypasses normal authorization. It must be rare, loud and reviewed. And audit logs are only useful when the actions they record are meaningful. Compare two logs of the same change.

6

Help people when access is denied

With fine-grained policy in many layers, "permission denied" becomes a mystery and people demand broad access just to get unstuck. Give them a tool that explains the denial and the shortest legitimate way forward.
