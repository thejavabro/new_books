The Architect Elevator

Large organizations stack many floors between the people who set strategy and the people who build systems. Architects earn their keep by riding between them, playing several roles at once, and caring most about how fast things can change.

1

From the penthouse to the engine room

Picture a company as a tall building. The penthouse is where business strategy gets set. The engine room is where systems are built and run. In between sit floors of management that relay messages up and down, and every floor rewrites the message a little, like a game of telephone. The worst outcome: people with the facts can't decide, and people who decide don't have the facts.

The architect's job is to take the elevator: skip the floors, talk to engineers in their language in the morning and to executives in theirs in the afternoon, and keep both sides honest. Click a floor to send the elevator.

Span, not heightAn architect's value isn't how high they ride but how many floors they connect. In a skyscraper it's fine if a technical architect and an enterprise architect each cover half the building and meet in the middle. A digital company is more like a bungalow: a few stairs and the strategy people already know the tech.
2

Riding the elevator in both directions

The elevator only works when you keep riding it. Some people ride to the top once and never come back, announcing they "used to be technical." Without feedback from the engine room, their decisions drift into an architect's dream, developer's nightmare: elegant on paper, impractical in code. That is authority without responsibility. What architects aren't, and what they add

Expectations of architects can get absurd: fix the performance bug this morning, transform the culture this afternoon. A clearer picture comes from what they are not, and from the value they reliably add. Sort each statement.

NonrequirementsIt's often said that developers handle functional requirements and architects handle the "-ilities". In practice architects mostly deal with things nobody wrote down: the assumptions and future needs that no one thought to specify, but that will decide whether the system survives.

5

Architects live in the first derivative

When does a system need no architecture at all? When it never changes. A system frozen in place can be any shape; only change makes structure matter. So the single biggest driver of architecture is the rate of change, the first derivative of the system's position over time. Today's state matters less than how fast you can move from it.

A software system's first derivative is its toolchainEvery change passes through the build and deployment pipeline, so that pipeline sets the speed limit. That's why CI, continuous deployment and configuration automation matter so much, and why build systems now deserve production-grade infrastructure rather than being the shoemaker's children.
6

Confidence brings speed: the 20-line test

Line coverage percentages say little. A better test of whether a team's tests are good enough: offer to delete 20 random lines of their code, run the tests, and if they pass, ship straight to production. Their reaction tells you everything. Try it on a toy function below.

Fear is the hidden brake: change that is never made out of fear can't be sped up by any toolchain. Poor quality breeds fear, fear lets code rot, rot raises the risk of change. Tests break the spiral.

7

Multispeed architectures and the second derivative

If rate of change shapes architecture, why not split systems by speed? "Bimodal IT" puts fast systems of engagement (apps, websites) in front of slow systems of record (core banking, ERP). The trouble: most real business changes cut through both layers, so the slow half sets the pace anyway. Digital companies know one speed: fast. Splitting by business domain works better.

The second derivativeSpeed is the first derivative of position; acceleration is the second. To speed up, you must change the system that changes the system: the toolchain, team structure and processes. And architects need their own acceleration too, because technology arrives faster each year. Staying close to the engine room is how they keep up.
