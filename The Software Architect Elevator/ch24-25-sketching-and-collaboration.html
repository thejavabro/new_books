Sketching and Collaboration

Architects often act like police sketch artists, drawing what others describe until the team recognizes its own system. And modern software tools show how teams can collaborate at scale.

1

Architects as police sketch artists

After a bank robbery, every witness saw the robber, yet ask them to draw and you get stick figures. Knowing something, articulating it and drawing it are three different skills. A police sketch artist asks easy questions ("Was he tall?"), draws, shows the sketch, adjusts, until the witnesses say "That's him!" Architects often do the same for teams who know their system intimately but can't draw it. Play the sketch artist below.

2

Start with a metaphor, then choose viewpoints

First establish what kind of thing the system is: its system metaphor, the idea popularized by Extreme Programming. A plain customer information portal? A new sales channel? A high-volume engine? A quick experiment that must evolve fast? The metaphor then decides which viewpoints deserve attention. That's the difference from a full analysis with a fixed checklist of sections, which risks becoming paint-by-numbers with no emphasis. Pick a metaphor.

3

Architecture therapy: what a team's drawing reveals

Family therapists sometimes ask children to draw their family and read the dynamics from proximity, size and position. Team drawings work the same way. Don't dismiss a rough or incomplete sketch; ask what it says about how the team thinks. Choose a drawing.

"That's wrong!" is a successWhen someone looks at your sketch and objects, you've found a mismatch in understanding that would otherwise have stayed hidden. If you're a fair proxy for later readers, you've saved them the same confusion. Draw it again.
4

Software is collaboration: got Git?

Buildings change slowly; software can change quickly and cheaply. So can documents, plans, slide decks and status reports, which are really software too. Yet many enterprises manage them in ways no development team would accept. Look at any shared drive: someone had the right idea (versioning) and stumbled on the implementation.

The shared drive

Single source of truthVersion control only works if everyone looks at the same version. Real-time collaborative editing changed how teams work: no mailing, finding and merging copies, no "my version vs your version" debates, and adding a collaborator is trivial.
5

Trunk-based development, for documents too

Branches let you work undisturbed, but that freedom is short-lived: eventually you must merge, and the longer you wait the worse it gets. Trunk-based development keeps everyone on the main line with very short branches. Teams use feature toggles to integrate unfinished work safely; the slide-deck equivalent is a hidden slide. Set how long branches live.

6

Always be ready to ship: iterative, not incremental

Ask your team: "What if we had to present in one hour?" If you work incrementally, polishing slide after slide, halfway through you have half a deck and nothing to present. If you work iteratively, you first sketch the whole storyline roughly and then refine, so you're always ready. Move through the preparation time.

7

Transparency, pairing and resistance

Many software teams have a glowing orb or screen showing build status: anyone walking by sees how things are going. That transparency builds trust outside and motivation inside, and works for any project. Two hurdles: managers who prefer to "massage" status in meetings, and, worse, teams that don't have the data at hand. How do they steer?

Review cycles versus "pair PowerPointing"

Model: each review round means scheduling, someone misreading the comments, and reconvening. Sitting together in one session settles it in hours.
