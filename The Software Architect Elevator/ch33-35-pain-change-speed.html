No Pain, No Change

Organizations rarely change until it hurts. Leaders then have to show a better way in a protected space, and replace the logic of economies of scale with the logic of economies of speed.

1

Not all change is transformation

Rearranging furniture is change; turning your house into a shop is transformation, from the Latin for changing shape or structure. IT transformation means changing how the organization works, not just installing new tools. Elastic cloud billing is useless with a fixed annual budget, and an API for servers is unexciting with a two-month approval in front of it. This is where an architect's skills combine: systems thinking to find the levers, communication to win support, leadership to make change last.

2

No pain, no change: the ten stages

A colleague came back from a glossy "digital showcase" full of hackathons to his desk, where he still had to clock his hours, waited three weeks for a server and couldn't install software. Was the company fooling itself? Probably not; it was just at stage 3 or 4. People don't wake up transformed after a good talk. 3

Getting over the hump: compounding odds

A chain of stages has an unforgiving property: the chance of finishing is the product of the chances of passing each step. Set a per-step success rate.

4

Leading change: the island of sanity

Showing results in a small team beats speeches. But trailblazers have a double burden: the pain of change, in an organization still at stage 1. And a new way of working can look worse in the old environment. You built a race car, and the track is a muddy field where the old tractor wins. Toggle the environment.

So pave some road too. Motivate with the carrot (a tangible picture of the better future) more than the stick (fear of disruption). Set goals carefully: "fewer outages" invites hiding outages and slowing down with more up-front testing; time to recovery is what customers feel.

5

How far off the mainland?

Some change programs sail far away: colorful offices, baristas, sticker-covered laptops, hoodies. Like offshore oil platforms, they're free of mainland constraints, and often irrelevant to it. You can't copy-paste culture. Too close, and the old rules smother the new team. Find the balance, and keep iterating.

6

Leaving your island gets your feet wet

Most organizations sit at a local optimum. Releasing every six months is a joke in the digital world, but processes have grown around it that make it work. Move to three months and life gets worse: twice the release pain, operations busy learning new tools. To reach a better place you must cross the valley, ideally by investing in automation first.

7

Economies of speed: death by efficiency

Traditional firms squeeze a few percent of efficiency: better discounts, black-and-white printing. Digital rivals aren't 10% faster but 10 times or more. One large IT organization spent six months of community work and a month preparing a steering board to standardize version control (and then recommended two products). A startup would sign up and make its first commit in ten minutes. Calculate the gap.

0×faster
Scale was the old advantageCities show what scale does well: density gives short paths and diverse skills, and benefits grow faster than size. Enterprise buying followed the same logic: supersize it for a discount. But software has no per-unit cost, and in the digital world an organization's size is limited by its ability to change.
Behold the flowOptimizing each department's utilization makes every one demand forms and queue numbers. Work hops from station to station and waits. That dead time isn't measured anywhere except in the customer's blood pressure.
8

Cost of delay, sandbagging and duplication

A business owner wanted a feature that only paid off in a second country, which delayed the whole launch. Flow thinking adds the cost of delay: revenue lost while waiting. For promising products it can exceed the development cost. Ship early, and you also learn whether that feature is needed at all. One fashion retailer built its empire this way, making most clothing close to home so new designs reach stores in weeks, not months.

Sandbagging: estimates are distributions

A project has a 50% chance of finishing in 4 weeks and a 20% chance in 3. Pick the estimate you'd commit to.

2 > 0Avoiding duplication isn't free: someone must find duplicates, coordinate, and keep a shared component compatible for everyone, which slows innovation. When told two teams might duplicate effort, one famous executive reportedly wrote "2 > 0" on the board. Two working solutions beat none.
