Queues, Dimensions, Truth

Queues hide everywhere in traditional IT and silently kill speed. Old trade-offs disappear when you add a dimension, and in the end the architect's main gift is telling the truth.

1

Who likes standing in line?

To speed up, most organizations look at the activities: is everyone busy, is each step efficient? For speed you must look between the activities. There you find not inefficiency but inactivity: work sitting in queues. Follow one request through a typical approval chain.

2

A little queuing theory: utilization versus wait

Two results from queuing theory explain a lot. Little's law: the average time an item spends in a system equals the average number of items in it divided by the throughput. Process two items per second with ten in the system, and each spends five seconds, mostly waiting. And for a simple queue with random arrivals, wait time explodes as utilization approaches 100%. Push utilization up.

3

Thinking in more dimensions

Architecture is full of trade-offs along a line: flexibility vs. complexity, decoupling vs. latency, and the classic quality vs. speed. "We'd like better architecture but there's no time" assumes you can only slide along that line. Plot the two on separate axes and you can ask a better question: can we shift the curve? Digital companies did, with practices that aren't rocket science. Toggle them.

What is quality, anyway?Traditional IT defines quality as conformance to the specification, on schedule, with good uptime: predictability. That's a proxy that assumes we know what customers want. Digital companies don't pretend to know; they observe behavior and adjust, often via A/B tests. Fast iteration ends up delivering what customers actually want, which is the better quality. Moving from a world with that extra degree of freedom into one without it feels like a headache, so reverse-engineer the beliefs behind the single line.
4

Epilogue: architecting IT transformation

Why should architects lead transformation, rather than managers with polished communication skills? Because technology evolution has become inseparable from organizational evolution. DevOps needs automation and new team structures; cloud needs new budgeting and new skills. The architect's job broadened from designing systems to designing a matching organization and culture: a chief organizational engineer disguised as a chief architect.

5

All I have to offer is the truth

In The Matrix, Morpheus offers Neo the red pill: reality, not comfort. He doesn't promise it will be pleasant. Transformation is the same. One architect announced that for transformation to succeed, the architect's life must get easier. He was bound to be disappointed. Choose.

Which will it be?

6

Abandon ship, and look for the iceberg

Imagine you're an officer on a famous liner, just told it will sink, slowly but surely. Most passengers sip champagne on the upper decks, unaware. Announce it too gently and nobody moves; too loudly and you cause panic. Start gentle, and ratchet up when you see inaction.

Disruptors don't look dangerous, any more than a block of ice looks like it can sink a ship. They don't rebuild your whole business; they attack weak spots that are inefficient or annoy customers but seem too small to matter. Where would they hit this value chain? Tap the links.

Send distress signalsNobody has a proven recipe for transformation. Exchange experiences with fellow architects, share your stories, and call for help when things look dire. You're not the only one taking the red pill.
