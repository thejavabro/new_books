Architecture Is Selling Options

Architecture is not boxes on a slide. It is the set of meaningful decisions that are hard to undo, and in an uncertain world its main value is buying options that let you change course later.

1

Architecture: three meanings, and there always is one

People use "architecture" for three different things: a system's structure ("a microservices architecture"), the act of defining it ("the architecture review"), and a team ("enterprise architecture is hiring"). Every system has the first. Not every company has the third, and having the team doesn't guarantee the first gets designed. So "we don't have time for architecture" is meaningless: lumping everything into one monolith is also an architecture decision, just an unconscious one.

2

Is this architecture? Look for decisions

A slide with boxes and lines is not automatically an architecture. The simplest test: does it record meaningful, non-obvious decisions with a rationale? If there was nothing to decide, why pay an architect? The point is easiest to see with three house sketches. Pick one.

Fit for purpose: no house is simply good

A glass house with a flat roof can be celebrated architecture, and collapse after a few Alpine winters. Architecture is fit or unfit for a context, which is why spotting hidden assumptions is the architect's job.

Does this pass the decision test?

All meaningful decisions have downsidesIf a "decision" has no downside, everyone would make it the same way, so it isn't really one. A good architecture document names what was traded away. Even a big ball of mud can be fit for purpose: some architectures must be "management proof," for example to hit a deadline at any cost.
3

Architecture is selling options

Should an architect be measured by the number of decisions they make? That's as bad as counting lines of code. A better idea: a key task is to eliminate irreversibility, so that fewer decisions must be made early. Finance has the perfect vocabulary for this: an option is the right, but not the obligation, to do a transaction at fixed terms in the future. Deferring a decision has value, and that value can be priced.

What is an option worth?

Black-Scholes, today's price 100

More uncertainty, more valueIf your app's load is predictable, the option to scale out is worth little. If you might be featured on national TV tomorrow, it's worth a lot. Only the business can judge that volatility, which is why "the business doesn't want to be involved in technical decisions" leads to bad choices.
Different horizons, different pricesA project manager looking six months ahead and an architect looking ten years ahead compute different values for the same option. Neither is irrational; they are plugging in different T.
4

Choosing a strike price: the lock-in example

Options cost money up front, and paying for the lowest possible switching cost is rarely the most economical choice. Take vendor lock-in. You could call the vendor directly, isolate dependencies and use containers, or build a full abstraction layer. Set how likely you are to switch, and see which option has the lowest expected total cost.

Agile and architecture are friendsBoth exist to cope with uncertainty, and both gain value as uncertainty rises. Architecture that sells options is the opposite of big up-front design. When good options aren't knowable in advance, aim for evolutionary architecture, guided by fitness functions that say which changes are improvements.
5

Every system is perfect for what it was designed to do

Structure diagrams show components; systems thinking shows behavior. A heater's structural view puts the thermostat in the middle. The systems view puts the room temperature in the middle, pushed up by the furnace and down by heat loss to the outside. A thermostat creates a negative feedback loop that keeps temperature stable, but only within limits. Try it.

6

Systems resist change

Most organizations settled into a steady state that served them well. When the environment changes, they push back toward that state, like a car you're pushing out of a ditch that keeps rolling back until it crosses the hump. Push enough times in a row to get it over. Stop and it rolls back.

Tap push quickly and repeatedly.

Example: ask for better architecture documentation and the system may answer with long workshops, or with rushed, poor documents that add review rounds. Pressure alone won't do it. Address the underlying structure: show the value of good documentation, train people, make the information visible. Visibility widens people's bounded rationality, like an electricity meter in the hallway that makes everyone save energy without any rule.
