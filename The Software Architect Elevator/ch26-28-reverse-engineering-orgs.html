1

Organizations are systems, too

Architects are good at reasoning about systems: scaling, coupling, feedback loops. They often hesitate to apply the same thinking to the system they work in. An org chart is the static view: who reports to whom, and how many levels you sit below the CEO. But how an organization works depends on the dynamic view: how fast cycles run, who talks to customers, how decisions flow. And underneath, organizations are people with families, bills and hangovers. Toggle the view.

2

Reverse-engineering organizations

To change behavior permanently, you must change the system, and an organization's behavior is driven by its culture, much of which is shared beliefs. Those are hard to see. You can't simply ask people about them because most are unaware they hold them. Slogans are a good place to start digging. Tap twice on each.

You don't know your own beliefsA younger colleague once drove to a strong-beer festival and planned to take a taxi home. How would he get the car back? He wouldn't: it was a shared car, left anywhere. The assumption "you come back with the car you drove" had never been challenged, until technology changed the world under it.
3

Common IT beliefs, and why they stick

Beliefs persist because people have proof: everyone has a story of the running system someone touched that then took 48 hours to revive. A child who burned a hand on the stove won't believe the new induction cooktop is cool to the touch because you say so; you have to touch it yourself. Luckily, many IT shops learned the same lessons, so their beliefs are similar. Open the cards.

4

Reprogramming the organization

Learning is hard; unlearning is harder. Selling continuous delivery to someone who equates change with risk is uphill until the old belief is gone. No three-step recipe exists, but four behaviors help: observe unusual decisions and ask which belief would make them rational, keep asking why, explain what has changed in the environment, and lead by example. Some beliefs aren't even based on experience, only handed down. Step through the (unverified, but telling) parable.

5

Control is an illusion

Control assumes that directions set at the top are followed and have the desired effect. Sitting at the top pushing buttons, how would you know? A thermostat works because it's a closed loop: it measures the room. Many organizations run open loops, or rely on status reports that tell them exactly what they want to hear. Peel these "green" projects.

Every project reports green. Tap to cut one open.

Hard data beats reportsOne ads team reviewed every A/B experiment weekly and accepted, rejected or extended each based on real user data, not projections. Shipping earned little praise; real traffic did. That data is much harder to fake than a status slide. Smarter loops add more signals, like a thermostat that reads the weather forecast and learns your schedule.
6

Three gaps, and mission command

Military strategists two centuries ago described friction between plans and outcomes. A modern framing names three gaps: knowledge (what we'd like to know vs. know), alignment (what we plan vs. what people do) and effects (what we expect vs. what happens). IT tries to close them with thick requirement documents, detailed plans and tight controls. The old army accepted them, and switched from orders to mission command: state the intent, let units adapt. 7

Autonomy versus anarchy

Ironically, giving teams autonomy increases control because it stops pretending the gaps don't exist. But "everyone does what they think best" isn't autonomy; it's anarchy. Real autonomy needs three elements. Toggle them.

Autonomous teams need better managementNon-autonomous teams do as they're told: easy. Autonomous teams need leaders who set intent and goals. Note the uncomfortable corollary for enterprise architecture teams that set direction without carrying the consequences.
Control the control loopAutoscaling absorbs load spikes without humans, and can also hide a slow new release by quietly adding servers until the monthly bill arrives. An outer loop that watches the inner loop catches that.
8

They don't build 'em like that anymore

Pyramids are rare: Egypt, Central America, the dollar bill and IT. Architects love layered pyramid diagrams with a generic base, then industry functions, then business functions, then customer configuration. The problem: the base needs enormous material and has to anticipate needs of layers not yet built. And nobody lives in a foundation. Compare how value appears.
