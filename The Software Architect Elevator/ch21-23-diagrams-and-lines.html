Diagrams That Mean Something

A good diagram chooses what to leave out, uses its visual vocabulary consistently, and draws the lines, because the relationships between components are where architecture lives.

1

Emphasis over completeness

"System X is missing from your diagram" is well-meant feedback, but completeness isn't the goal. A diagram is a model, and like a map it needs a deliberate scope: big enough to be meaningful, small enough to grasp, cohesive enough to make sense. A city map that shows half of downtown is awkward; one that includes the whole lake and the next state at the same scale is useless. All models are wrong; to know which are useful, first know which question you're answering.

2

The five-second test

Show a slide for five seconds, then ask people what they saw. For most busy slides the answer is a few words from the headline. Slides meant to double as handouts, "slideuments," never pass. Try it: press the button, look for five seconds, then answer.

3

Diagramming basics: fix the drawing

Default 10-point fonts and hairline strokes, plus poor placement, ruin more diagrams than bad ideas do. The tool isn't the only one to blame, but defaults can be changed. Apply the fixes one at a time.

Zoom your diagram to 25% to check what's still readable. If your tool won't draw a decent arrowhead, put a triangle on the line: blaming the tool is like a cook blaming the farmer for bland tomatoes.

4

Making a statement: one story, many slides

Titles should be full sentences that make a statement: "Stateless services and automation support elastic scale-out" beats "Server architecture." Then string the statements into one story. Read only the titles, as an outline view shows them, and you'll know whether the deck tells one story or twenty.

Twenty stories
  1. Server architecture
  2. Network overview
  3. Current challenges
  4. Vendor landscape
  5. Next steps
One story
  1. Peak traffic triples every December
  2. Fixed servers can't absorb the peaks
  3. Stateless services allow adding servers on demand
  4. Automation adds them within minutes
  5. We ask for funding to automate two services

Nothing is confusing in itselfTopics may be complicated. Whether they're confusing is up to you.
5

Diagram-driven design: cheating in pictures is harder

It's easier to hide a muddled design in words than in a picture. Drawing a design forces decisions, and a consistent visual vocabulary validates it: in a messaging pattern language, for instance, every split of messages visibly needs an aggregation later. A historical example is the graphical train schedule: distance on one axis, time on the other, each train a line. On a single track, two crossing lines between stations mean a collision. Shift the second train until the schedule works.

6

Drawing the line: architecture without lines isn't one

A sketch of a car with engine, seats, wheels and steering wheel in the right places looks like architecture, but it can't tell you how the car works. The same four components A, B, C and D can form very different systems. The lines decide. Choose a wiring.
