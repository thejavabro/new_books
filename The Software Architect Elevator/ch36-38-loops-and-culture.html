Loops, Inside-Out, Culture

Fast feedback loops beat long-range plans. You cannot look digital to customers while staying analog inside, and no vendor can sell you a new culture.

1

The infinite loop: learning per dollar

Running in circles sounds bad, unless the circle is the build-measure-learn loop. Digital companies know control is an illusion, so they crave fast feedback: launch a minimum viable product, measure real usage, learn, refine, repeat. Their key metric is how many revolutions they make per dollar and per month. A traditional project often stops at launch, about a third of the way around. Spin the wheels.

Learning stays humanMachines take over more tasks every year, but learning how to build a product that excites users is still a human job. That makes learning speed a good thing to optimize.
2

Who is inside the loop?

Each revolution teaches the team what users want, how to build better experiences, and how to scale. If externals do the work, the externals get smarter. Keep your own people inside the loop and use outside help to coach. In that sense, digital transformation starts with HR and recruiting.

3

Pivoting the layer cake

Layered organizations separate concerns, which becomes a liability when speed matters: the customer sits at the bottom, far from where decisions are made. Turn the cake on its side. Vertical teams own a product from concept to code to operations ("you build it, you run it") and draw feedback straight from customers. A bit of crust keeps it one company: brand and strategy on top, shared automated infrastructure below.

4

You can't fake IT

You can't build a fancy house on a crumbling foundation, and you can't be digital on the outside while the engine room takes eight weeks to provision a server by email. A management study found that companies which aligned IT with the business without first making IT deliver well spent more on IT and grew more slowly than average. Tap the quadrants.

Corporate IT also has to compete with outside providers on engagement, not just cost: co-create services with business units to create pull, instead of pushing commodities through governance.

5

Eat your own dog food

IT departments far from end customers have friendly, motivated testers right next door: their own employees. Offer every new service as an internal beta first. One large internet company even merged employee and customer accounts into a single system, so employees experience exactly what customers do. Compare that with a bank that banned a phone platform for employees as insecure while serving the majority of its customers on it.

6

Money can't buy love, or a culture change

"We're a big corporation; what works there won't work here" often means "we have a lot of money." Money is a curse for transformation in subtle ways. Rate-of-return hurdles kill disruptive projects early, the classic innovator's dilemma. Try to fund a new idea.

7

Hollowed-out IT and the limits of buying

In the late 1990s, highly profitable telecoms outsourced nearly all technical work. IT shrank into budget administration, and real talent stayed away because its skills weren't valued. All seemed fine while money flowed, until internet companies took over messaging and the SMS gold mine shrank fast. Outsourcing locks you out of the build-measure-learn cycle and of deep understanding of your own technology.

Can you buy talent back with salaries? People who come for money leave for more money. Strong engineers are drawn to strong teams. Paying more attracts mercenaries, like the unpopular kid handing out candy: surrounded, but without friends. Of the four things that describe an organization, which can money change quickly?
