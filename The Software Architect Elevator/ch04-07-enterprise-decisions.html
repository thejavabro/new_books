Architects in the Enterprise

What is enterprise architecture actually for? This page covers the architect's job in a big company: balancing business and IT, standing on skill, impact and leadership, deciding well under uncertainty, and asking the questions nobody else does.

1

Enterprise architect, or architect in the enterprise?

The title enterprise architect hides two jobs. One is architecting the enterprise itself: how business processes and IT fit the company's operating model. The other is doing IT architecture at enterprise scale, across many departments. A widely used definition calls EA the organizing logic for business processes and IT infrastructure, reflecting how much the company wants to integrate and standardize. That puts EA squarely between business and IT, not inside IT.

Nobody has a chief paper officerWhen business ran on paper, nobody created a paper department with its own executive. In digital companies IT is the business. Firms that run on digital technology don't think of themselves as "tech companies" but as retailers, advertisers or logistics businesses that use technology to win. "We're a bank, not a tech company" is no longer an excuse.

3

An architect stands on three legs

How senior is an architect? A useful frame is a three-legged stool: skill (knowing which tool from the drawer fits which job), impact (benefit to the business in revenue, cost or speed) and leadership (mentoring, sharing, advancing the field). A stool with a short leg wobbles. Adjust the legs.

4

The virtuous cycle: round and round

The legs feed each other. Applying skill to real problems shows which skills matter. Leadership multiplies impact: ten well-mentored architects do more than one brilliant one, which is just horizontal scaling applied to people. Sharing openly earns access to communities of other experts, which sharpens skill again. And because technology keeps moving, you go around the loop many times in a career. Step through it.

Architect as the last stop?Many architects stay architects for most of their career, and that's fine: doctors and lawyers stay doctors and lawyers. In technically minded firms the engineering career ladder runs up to senior vice president, so getting better beats chasing a title.
5

Humans are poor decision makers

Win the lottery: great decision? Cross a busy road at night with your eyes shut and survive: also great? We judge decisions by outcome when we should judge them by the risk taken. Psychology catalogs the traps. Here are a few that show up in IT meetings.

The law of small numbers

Each week, outages come from the same unchanged system (on average 3 per week). The ops meeting still labels weeks "good" or "bad."

Loss aversion: take the bet?

Heads you pay 100, tails you win 120. The expected value is +10 per toss, yet most people decline because losing hurts more than winning pleases. Play 20 tosses and see.

6

Model thinking: a decision tree

All models are wrong, but some are useful. A decision tree separates likelihood from impact and takes the emotion out. Scenario: you need a server licence now. Buy at list price today (value 0), or bridge a month with a temporary licence and hope the vendor's rumored discount arrives. Then ask a second question: what would it be worth to know for sure?

The best decision is the one you don't need to makeA key architect task is removing irreversibility from designs. If a choice can be changed cheaply later, you can decide it quickly now, or defer it. That's the bridge to the next page: architecture as options.
7

Question everything: who doesn't ask stays dumb

Chief architects don't know everything better. A good one knows which questions to ask, a bit like the Oracle in The Matrix. The classic tool is the five whys: keep asking why until you hit a root cause you can fix. In reviews, "why" exposes the decisions and assumptions behind a design that was presented as if it fell from the sky.
