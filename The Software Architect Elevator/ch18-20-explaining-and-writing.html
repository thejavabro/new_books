Explaining and Writing

Explaining technical topics well is a design problem. Build a gentle ramp for your audience, show the whole system before its parts, and write documents that busy people can actually use.

1

You can't manage what you can't understand

"You can't manage what you can't measure" is only half true: measurements help only if you understand the system's dynamics. Decision makers increasingly can't, so architects must explain the consequences of technical choices in business terms like cost, flexibility and time to market. When critics say "all architects do is produce paper," remind them what good documents do. Tap the cards.

Isn't the code the documentation?Partly, if every reader has access to well-structured code and good search. But code won't explain your value proposition or why you made critical decisions. Generated diagrams miss the forest for the trees because they don't know what deserves emphasis. That stays a human job. And keep it short: a five-page limit is a good rule.
2

Build a ramp for the reader, not a cliff

A team once briefed a steering committee on a new high-performance computing stack, from workload managers down to storage hardware, comparing integrated stacks with standalone products. Within a few slides nobody could follow. Executives are smart and can climb a steep ramp. Nobody can climb a cliff. 3

Mind the gap: the curse of knowledge

Experts silently fill gaps in their own reasoning, so they can't see them. Architects once explained that servers in an untrusted network zone need separate network cards for inbound and outbound traffic, so there's no direct path from the internet to trusted systems. Then they said the vendor's "three-NIC design" couldn't meet that requirement. To everyone else, three cards sounded like more than enough. The gap was narrow, but deep.

A cheap test: explain it to someone outside the topic and ask them to teach it back. Wherever they stumble, there's a gap.

4

First create a language, then use it

Step one: give the audience a plain mental model with no product names or acronyms. Step two: use it to explain the actual decision. For file systems, the model is a layered stack: disk, block storage, file system, operating system, application. Now the difference between products becomes obvious. Toggle between them.

Keep the level of detail consistentHere "POSIX" is treated as one thing, though it really has many versions and parts. That's fine: pick a level and stick to it. File systems on slide one and bit encoding on magnetic platters on slide two will bore half the room and lose the other half. And use every meeting with management as a teaching opportunity, instead of hiding behind jargon so no one questions your "decision." Advise managers not to approve anything that isn't clear to them.
5

Show the kids the pirate ship

A box of LEGO doesn't show a pile of bricks on the cover. It shows the assembled pirate ship in a bay with cliffs and sharks. Technical presentations should too: grab attention, build excitement, show purpose. A table of contents is no way to start; no movie opens with its outline. Here's the classic exercise: draw a monitoring system. Most people draw the bricks. Then ask, "what is it for?"

6

Pack some pathos

Aristotle named three modes of persuasion: logos (facts and reasoning), ethos (trust and authority) and pathos (emotion). Most technical talks run about 90% logos, 9% ethos, 1% pathos, so a small extra dose of emotion goes a long way. Just make sure the box contains the cannons the cover promised.

Play is work, too. Playing is how people learn, so in times of rapid change architects should play more, not less.

7

Writing for busy people

Writing takes more effort than talking, but it scales to large audiences, is read two to three times faster than speech, is searchable and versioned, and exposes sloppy thinking. The catch: nobody has to turn the page. Impact rises with quality in a non-linear way. Below a bar, the paper lands in the trash. Above a point, polishing is gold-plating. Move the slider.

8

Making it easy for the reader

Text is linear but topics aren't, so give the content a tree structure (the pyramid principle) and, like a good animated film that entertains kids and parents at once, offer different paths: headings and diagrams for skimmers, detail for those who dig in. Then remove noise. Parallel structure lets readers focus on content instead of parsing.
