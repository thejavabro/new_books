Scaling, Discipline, Governance

The workarounds people use reveal how an organization really runs. Scaling teams follows the same rules as scaling systems, going fast demands more discipline, not less, and good governance plants ideas instead of policing.

1

Black markets reveal how things really get done

Large organizations wrap work in control processes. One could approve technical decisions worth tens of millions but needed sign-off for a $200 plane ticket, whose price rose while waiting. Underneath, organizations know their processes hinder progress, so they tolerate a black market: you just need to know whom to ask. Ask how long a server takes. If the answer is "it depends on who's asking," there's a black market.

2

Beating the black market with a white market

Cracking down with more control won't work: control is what created the black market. The only fix is an efficient white market that makes the official route the easy one. Design processes from the user's side, not the reporting side; one way is to apply for your own job openings to find the hurdles. And make everything transparent. Reduce the friction and watch where requests go.

3

Scale an organization the way you scale a system

Architects know how to scale systems: stateless services, few synchronization points, asynchronous messaging, caches, backoff on retries. The same patterns apply to organizations. Start with the worst throughput killer: the meeting, a synchronization point that needs several people in one place at the same time.

4

Slow chaos is not order

"We're Agile, so we have no plan, no documentation and no architecture." Agile isn't the same as fast. It's about hitting a moving target through frequent recalibration. And speed needs more discipline, not less. A Formula 1 pit stop looks chaotic and is in fact the most choreographed, rehearsed four seconds in sport. Check the attributes of fast, good delivery your team has.

5

Governance through inception

Governance harmonizes things through rules and standards: more purchasing power, less operational complexity, better security. Done in the abstract it produces lowest-common-denominator or overengineered "universal" solutions, often set by people who don't use them (executives with special laptops rarely feel the standard one). The best standards connect things: in 1904, fire crews from other cities came to help with a great fire in Baltimore, and stood idle because their hoses didn't fit the hydrants.

Standardize connecting elements, like monitoring or source control, before endpoints like laptops or IDEs. TCP/IP and HTTP, the most successful IT standards, are open interface standards. Now the governance trick: change is constant, so every system will be replaced eventually. If central IT already has a good, ready-to-use answer when a unit needs something new, units adopt it as if it were their own idea. That's inception. Central must simply innovate faster than the units.
