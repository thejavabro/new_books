The Flat World and the Coffee Shop

Product slides make every road look short. Architects draw the map that shows where the real choices lie, then look at an ordinary coffee shop to learn how scalable, asynchronous systems handle failure.

1

The IT world is flat: draw your own map

Every world map distorts. Flattening a sphere forces compromises in angles, areas or distances, and every map chooses a middle: put Europe in the center and Asia becomes the "East." Enterprise vendors do the same. Each lives in its own middle kingdom, drawing its home territory huge and the periphery small. That's not deception; it's the worldview of people who grew up building one kind of product. If you build databases, every application looks like a database with some code around it. Switch the lens.

The star on the hoodIf you'd never seen a car and only ever talked to one premium carmaker, you might conclude that a star emblem on the hood defines an automobile. Vendors define categories by features only they have. Your own map shows that the drive train matters more than the emblem. And any architect with a product name in their title is probably carrying the vendor's map.
2

Architecture lives between buzzwords and product names

Calling a big data system's architecture "SQL Server" is like calling a house's architecture "aerated concrete brick." Both may be good choices, but neither describes how things fit together. Start mapping where change is happening: a product to replace, or a differentiating capability. Then place borders. Is "data" one continent with warehouses, lakes and marts as countries? Does monitoring include performance testing, as vendors from that background like to claim? Sort these statements.

Architecture, or just a product name?

Most reference architectures deserve a movie-style disclaimer: any similarity to real systems is purely coincidental. Map-making gets called "academic" by some managers. If "pragmatic" means haphazard, academic is the better place to be.

Once borders are drawn, populate the countries with products. Fit depends on what you already have, so pick the product that fits best, not the "best" product. 4

Check the vendor's product philosophy

When an account manager opens with "help us understand your environment" (roughly: tell me what to sell you), steer to someone senior who shapes product strategy and ask two questions. The answers reveal the edge and the center of their map.

Ask a question.

Comparing worldviews, not right and wrongA software engineer may believe a good language and a disciplined life cycle beat "easy" configuration; others are glad to skip compiler errors. The aim is to know whether your worldview and the vendor's line up. And since IT territory shifts fast while product philosophies change slowly, look for old products in a new coat of paint.
5

Your coffee shop doesn't use two-phase commit

Architects like ACID transactions and clean binary states. The real world is messier, and it's a good teacher. A busy coffee shop is an asynchronous messaging system: the cashier takes your order and money, writes your name on a cup and puts it in a queue; baristas, competing consumers, pull cups and make drinks. You wait at the counter. That decoupling maximizes throughput. Run the shop.

Correlation identifiersDrinks finish out of order: a drip coffee ordered last beats the blended smoothie, and baristas batch. So every cup carries your name, which is exactly what a correlation identifier does for messages.
6

Handling errors without a transaction

With two-phase commit you'd stand at the till with money, receipt and drink on the table until all three could change hands at once. Nobody would get served. Instead, the shop handles errors one of three ways. Pick the right one for each situation.
