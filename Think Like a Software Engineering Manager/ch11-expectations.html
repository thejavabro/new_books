Managing Expectations

Most conflict is a gap between what someone expected and what they got. Set expectations explicitly, negotiate openly and keep adjusting them as things change.

1

What's said, and what's assumed

Filmmakers storyboard before they shoot; managers should agree expectations before work starts. Expectations can be realistic (grounded in data, SMART) or unrealistic, and explicit (written down, as in a job description) or implicit (assumed: proactive learning, initiative). Most friction lives below the waterline, together with perception: people judge today's work through yesterday's experiences. Tap the iceberg.

2

Clarity, relationships, risk

Alice asks Bob, who is new, to "deploy these changes to production". Bob has never deployed. Look at how many questions that one sentence leaves open, then give him a proper brief.

Beyond clarity, set expectations build relationships (Bob feels trusted and asks for help) and mitigate risk: when Bob admits he's unsure, Alice arranges for a teammate to shadow him, a built-in plan B. Agile delivery repeats this loop constantly: set expectations, deliver a slice, take feedback, reset.

3

Ambiguity, saying no, taking it personally

Humans read the same instruction differently; ask three people to open a door and one opens it, one peeks through the window and one asks why. Three challenges stand out. Try two of them.

In a kickoff, Julie questions whether David's requirements are feasible with current resources. You're David.

For ambiguity the fix runs both ways: the person setting the task should state outcome, timeline, prerequisites, documentation and contacts; the person receiving it should ask until those are clear, and say if the skills don't match (asking a developer who has never used React to lead a React feature sets everyone up to fail).

5

Negotiating with a capacity sheet

Jason's team sized 12 features (10 from product, 2 efficiency items from engineering) as S, M or L, then worked out real capacity after holidays, vacation, on-call and meetings. The plan was about 106 engineer-days over. Engineering capacity is zero-sum, so he negotiated what to defer. Reproduce it: adjust capacity, then defer features until the plan fits. (Sizes here: S = 15, M = 35, L = 55 engineer-days.)

One engineer is on call each week of the 63-working-day quarter, so 63 days go to on-call.
