Organizational Change Management

Reorganizations are unsettling by default. A structured approach, with champions, a road map and honest measurement, keeps people informed and work moving.

Why companies reorganise

Change is constant in tech, and in the end a company has to stay profitable whatever its mission says. Unfreeze, transition, refreeze

Before you change anything, understand why: the rationale, what it costs people and the company, the expected outcome, the alternatives, whether it can be reversed, and how long it should take. Kurt Lewin's classic model describes what follows in three phases. Step through it.

4

Where is the change stuck? (ADKAR)

Champions are self-driven, respected people who believe in the change, involved early (even at brainstorming) and given training. They spread it person to person. The ADKAR model describes the five things each person needs, in order. In the book's example, company ABC moves from bought project-management software to an in-house tool. Rate how far one team has got; the first weak stage is where to focus, because later stages can't hold without it.

5

Strategy, road map and a SWOT check

A strategy turns the idea into steps: tenets (guiding principles), success metrics (for example "35% faster, half the security incidents"), an execution approach (big bang or phased, with risks and dependencies), the stakeholders, and a communication plan that gets ahead of rumours. A SWOT analysis helps you ground it. Sort these points for a team planning to rebuild a legacy system.

During execution, roll out in phases and expect pushback. Stay objective, keep feedback channels open, ask clarifying questions, offer training, and use proofs of concept and demos to show that it works. Sometimes, after negotiating, you still have to enforce the decision. Then measure against the success criteria with surveys and retros, thank the champions, and be patient: cultural change is slow. Failed changes cost trust, money and morale, which is why the planning matters.

When the boss changes, or you are the new boss

8

Reductions in force, mergers and acquisitions

Layoffs happen for market reasons (the post-pandemic over-hiring corrections of 2022 and 2023 are the chapter's example), for automation and AI, or when merged teams overlap. Sometimes the EM knows in advance, sometimes not even the EM knows. For the people affected, it hits income, health cover, identity and confidence. You have to deliver the news to Sam in a one-on-one. Pick your opening.

Before the conversation: agree the method and message with HR, book a private room, know the severance and support resources, and be clear on timelines. Afterwards, for the people who stay: reach out quickly, reassure them about their own status, redistribute the work (renegotiating scope if necessary), warn stakeholders about delivery risk, and accept that productivity will dip for a while.

Mergers and acquisitions add culture clashes to the mix. Understand the context, integrate cultures through shared values, align both leadership teams on roles, involve employees from both sides, and work deliberately to keep key talent.
