Project Management, Execution, and Delivery

A project is a life cycle, not a deadline. Each phase has its own questions, artifacts and failure modes, and the EM owns the thread through all of them.

1

Five phases of a project

A project is like a sports season: training, game plan, the match, and the review afterwards. The EM, product manager (PM) and technical program manager play as one team. The chapter follows an invented example throughout: Project Accessible, a website for a toy company's new line of accessibility-friendly toys, due in three months. Tap a phase.

One level up: portfolio managementWhen you run several projects at once (say two new products and one legacy cleanup), project portfolio management helps allocate people and budget, weigh risks, prioritise and monitor them as one collection aligned to company strategy.
2

Preplanning: feasible, prioritised, scoped

Feasibility comes first: is there any fundamental business, technical, legal or financial flaw? Answer the four questions for Project Accessible.

Next, prioritisation. Leadership ranks the project against everything else (it becomes a must-have). Within it, the MoSCoW technique sorts requirements: Must, Should, Could, Won't (this time). Tap a feature, then a column.

Scope definition then documents the aim, customers, requirements and acceptance criteria, what's in and out, assumptions, constraints, risks, budget and timeline, plus nonfunctional needs such as security review. Goals are often framed as OKRs, for example "Enhance user experience" with key results like lifting satisfaction from 80% to 90% and cutting page load from 5 to 3 seconds.

3

Planning: work backward from launch

Start with the end: what does success look like on launch day? Then walk backward, breaking the work down (a Work Breakdown Structure gives the pieces; the working-backward plan puts them in order). Pick a launch date and see the plan.

Estimate with T-shirt sizes first (an example scale: XS = 2, S = 4, M = 8, L = 12 engineer-weeks), then story points for detail. Compare with capacity. Size the Project Accessible work and see whether it fits.

4

Working model, risks and user stories

The working model is the team's operating manual: methodology (agile and scrum, kanban, or waterfall), ceremonies (kickoff, grooming, sprint planning, retros, UX design reviews, QA meetings, demos, scrum of scrums), how decisions get made, communication channels and escalation paths. Add a skills survey and training plan, and get the RACI signed off by every stakeholder.

Track risks with ROAM: Resolved, Owned, Accepted or Mitigated. Tap a risk to move it to the next column; the goal is no risk left unhandled.

Backlog items are often written as user stories ("As a [user], I want [goal] so that [reason]") and checked against INVEST: Independent, Negotiable, Valuable, Estimable, Small, Testable. Pick a story.

5

Dependencies ripple

Project Accessible depends on UX mockups, an upstream data team, the backend API, QA and a choice between an open-source library and building in-house. Delays at the start ripple down the chain. The team avoided a waterfall by letting the frontend build against mock data while the backend progressed. Try it. (Track these in a RAID log: risks, assumptions, issues, dependencies.)

6

Execution and launch

During execution the EM gives technical guidance, enforces coding standards and reviews, champions security, leads people through delays (add people, simplify, or trim scope), manages vendors with legal and procurement, and keeps leadership informed through demos, dashboards and weekly or monthly business reviews. When priorities shift mid-project, run it through change management: re-estimate, reallocate, and negotiate with the PM what moves out.

Testing comes in layers: developer tests, cross-functional checks, QA, usability and user acceptance testing, security, load testing and internal "dogfooding" or a beta. Then launch needs readiness. Tick what's done.

7

After launch: measure, support, reflect

Measure against the success criteria: timeline, budget, return on investment, customer and employee satisfaction, code quality and DORA metrics, operational stability. Project Accessible beat sales expectations and scored 95/100 with customers; an internal survey showed 70% satisfaction and pointed to retrospectives as a way to catch testing problems earlier.
