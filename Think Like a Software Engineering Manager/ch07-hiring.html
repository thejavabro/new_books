A five-step hiring framework

Early morning, browser tabs full of résumés: each hire reshapes your team and its future. The chapter turns hiring into five steps. Step through them.

Sourcing with inclusive language

Diverse talent pools (conferences such as Grace Hopper, meetups, university programs, online platforms) and close work with recruiting partners come first. Then small things matter: gender-neutral wording in job descriptions and interview feedback ("they", the candidate's initials, "the candidate"). Running a fair interview loop

A junior loop might have three to five rounds covering problem-solving, algorithms, scalability and design, plus collaboration and openness to feedback. Practices from the author's teams at large tech companies: a leveling guide, a calibrated question bank with defined answers for each rating, adaptive questions (ask a Go developer about Go, not Java trivia), interviewer training on bias, and a proper close with time for the candidate's questions. Sort these observations into green and red flags. Tap once for green, twice for red.

Split loops happen: a candidate shines in coding and stumbles in design. Interviewers submit feedback independently (no sharing in between), then debrief against the leveling guide. Set the five ratings.

The sales call and a 30-60-90 onboarding plan

In the sales call the hiring manager goes beyond the recruiter: day-to-day work, the onboarding plan, growth, team culture and the road map. It is not just selling; let the candidate judge fit, so there are no surprises. The author won over a senior engineer holding several offers by connecting his AWS passion to next quarter's cloud projects.

Staffing from scratch versus hiring for an existing team

A new team (triggered by a new initiative) needs a purpose, an org chart, and a 1/3/5-year head-count estimate; avoid overhiring, which leaves people idle and leaving. An existing team (more workload, new scope, or a backfill) needs someone who fits a known mix and culture. Both should stay within a two-pizza team of six to eight engineers. 6

Hire externally or grow internally?

No universal answer. External hires bring fresh perspectives, missing skills (machine learning, say), seniority balance and a cure for tunnel vision, at the cost of ramp-up time. Growing internally signals a future, keeps hard-won context and ramps up faster, but on its own can narrow the team's view. Slide the mix and read the risks.
