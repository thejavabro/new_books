Time Management

An EM's calendar fills itself. Protecting time for what is important but not urgent is the difference between leading and reacting.

Busy is not the same as productive

The picture is a familiar one: back-to-back calls, a planning document due tomorrow that you haven't opened, and a list of tasks scribbled at 7 p.m. Paul Graham's distinction helps. Engineers work on a maker's schedule (long uninterrupted blocks), managers on a manager's schedule (many short slots and context switches). An EM needs some of both. Declutter your calendar

You control your own calendar. Below is a cluttered Tuesday. For each meeting ask "Am I needed?" Then keep it, replace it with an async update, send someone else, or decline it, and watch your longest focus block grow.

Other tips from the chapter: colour-coded focus blocks at your peak hours, a short block at the start and end of each day to plan and reflect, real lunch breaks, recurring reminders for regular prep (one-on-ones, status updates), a Friday review of the week, and a meeting-free day if your org allows one. Schedule 25- or 55-minute meetings so people get a breather between them.

Delegate, say no, keep lists and trackers

Jacob, a new EM, spent two days on a hard bug because he knew the code best. That cost him two days of manager work and cost someone on the team a chance to learn. Delegation (chapter 5) is a time tool as well as a growth tool. Saying no protects existing commitments. When the author's manager brought one more urgent task, she explained the team's load and offered options: re-rank priorities, adjust timelines, or bring in other resources. That made it a decision they shared instead of a flat refusal.

A living to-do list (David Allen's Getting Things Done suggests concrete next actions for each item) becomes essential in heavy seasons such as annual planning and performance reviews. Add a 15–30 minute shutdown ritual at the end of the day to clear messages and triage tomorrow. Project trackers (Jira, Asana or a shared document) show early when a project needs more people, which saves time later.

5

Async by default, sync when it matters

Switching between complex tasks costs real productivity. Async messages let you reply inside your own time blocks; urgent issues can still page you. Shared meeting notes also reduce the fear of missing out. But a long async thread on a contentious topic can cost more than a 15-minute call. Choose for each case.

The Pomodoro Technique

Francesco Cirillo's technique works like this: 25 minutes of single-task focus, then a 5-minute break; after four rounds, a longer break. It cuts interruptions and makes you notice where your time goes. The demo below runs one minute per tenth of a second.

Urgent versus important

"Urgent" means it has to happen now or there are visible consequences. "Important" means it moves long-term goals. The Eisenhower Matrix crosses the two: Do (urgent and important), Schedule (important, not urgent), Delegate (urgent, not important) and Delete (neither). Sort this EM's to-do list.

8

Spend time to find time

To see where your week actually goes, either estimate time per quadrant at the end of each day, or (more accurately) log in 30-minute chunks for a week or two: 25 minutes of work, 2 minutes to note it, 3 minutes of break. Enter your rough split.

9

Questions to take away

  • Do you feel in control of your work time? Which tasks eat most of it, and could they be delegated or automated?
  • Which meetings could you cancel or defer this week?
  • How many hours a day does your team spend in meetings? Do they get enough uninterrupted time?
  • What one change would you make to your calendar, and how?
