Engineering and Operational Excellence

Fast teams stay fast because they invest in the boring parts: reviews, tests, deployment safety, monitoring, on-call and paying down debt.

1

Excellence versus operational excellence

The chapter opens with a team of eight that ships every two weeks and still had four critical production issues in one month. Two related ideas help here. Engineering excellence is about the quality of the technical work: code, tests and design. Operational excellence (OE) is about how reliably the running system behaves and how well the team operates it. An EM's job is to make both a habit rather than a heroic effort. Tap each reason to open it.

2

Raise the bar on code and tests

The bar starts with written coding standards, reviews that teach as well as gatekeep, regular training and modular design, so that one change doesn't break five things. Tests are the safety net. The book's testing pyramid puts many fast, cheap tests at the bottom and a few slow, expensive ones at the top. Tap a layer.

Other test types fill gaps the pyramid doesn't show: load and performance tests for scale, regression tests so old bugs stay fixed, user acceptance testing with real users, and chaos experiments (in the spirit of Netflix's Chaos Monkey) that deliberately break things to prove the system survives.

Paying down tech debt

Tech debt is the shortcut taken today that slows you down tomorrow. Some of it is a sensible trade-off; what hurts is debt that nobody tracks. Ask what it costs, who feels it, what risk it carries and what happens if you wait. Below is a toy model (not from the book): each quarter, work you don't spend on debt adds drag. 4

Deploy in phases, behind a flag

Rather than pushing to everyone at once, ship through stages (alpha, beta, pre-production), letting each one bake for about a day while you watch the metrics. Then canary a small slice of production traffic before going to everyone. Feature flags let you turn the new behaviour off without redeploying. Security scans (dependency checks, static and dynamic analysis) belong in the same pipeline. Pick where a hidden bug gets caught.

5

Monitoring, on-call and the four numbers

You can't fix what you can't see: structured logs, dashboards with alarms on the metrics that matter, runbooks for each alarm and clear service-level agreements. On-call health is often summarised by four averages. Set up one incident and see them.

MTTA = mean time to acknowledge; MTTR = mean time to recover; MTBF = mean time between failures (repairable systems); MTTF = mean time to failure (things you replace rather than repair). Availability here is MTBF / (MTBF + MTTR).

Good on-call habits: a weekly operations meeting to review alarms and tickets, written handoffs between shifts, and blameless postmortems. Lowering OPEX (the cost of keeping things running, from cloud bills to toil) is part of the job too, and naming an OE ambassador who keeps the topic visible stops it fading away between incidents.

6

DevOps, DevSecOps and away teams

DevOps means the team that builds a service also runs it, with automation joining the two. DevSecOps "shifts security left": scans and reviews happen while code is written rather than after launch, and a security review comes before any new production service. The OWASP Top 10 is a good starting checklist. Adopting all of this takes years, so start early.

Sometimes a critical project needs a change in another team's codebase and that team has no capacity. In the away-team model, your team (the away team) does the work in the host team's code under a written agreement. Tick what your agreement covers.

Accessibility is part of excellence too: products should work for people with different vision, hearing, motor and speech abilities. It often gets postponed, so advocate for it with training and by following the relevant laws and W3C guidance.

7

Incidents, the 5 Whys and no blame

When a deployment breaks something, the order of work is: tell stakeholders, find the root cause, size the blast radius, fix, confirm resolution with a summary, and document what happened. Postmortems look for causes, not culprits. The 5 Whys keeps asking until it reaches something you can actually fix. Step through the chapter's database example.

Excellence is a loop, not a finish line. In the chapter's example, a team builds a static-analysis plug-in, gets infosec approval, and finds it produces false positives. Instead of dropping the tool, they let developers mark false positives so they stop reappearing. It's a small fix, and that kind of small fix is what continuous improvement mostly consists of.

8

When product wants features, not OE

Product partners usually want every feature, and engineering capacity is finite. Your PM pushes back on spending 20% of next quarter on reliability. How do you respond?

The chapter's strategies: align and communicate openly about why quality pays off, prioritise with explicit criteria (customer impact, complexity, OE goals), decide together with cross-functional partners, improve in small iterations, and negotiate compromises instead of all-or-nothing positions.

9

Questions to take back to your team

  • What pain points would DevOps practices actually remove for us, and are those goals tangible?
  • Who could be our OE ambassador, and do we have an OE road map?
  • How do we track, measure and review tech debt today?
  • Which success metrics would show that OE work is paying off?
