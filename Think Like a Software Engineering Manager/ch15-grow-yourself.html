Keep learning: breadth or depth?

Technology keeps changing, so an EM's learning should be deliberate: set a goal, map the gaps, turn them into concrete actions, and repeat. The chapter points to the idea of a knowledge breadth versus depth triangle. Depending on where you start and where you're going, you either go deep in one area or widen your view. Move the slider to see the trade-off.

Knowing about a practice such as delegation isn't the same as having mastered it. Mastery comes from doing it repeatedly as a leader, visibly, so the team sees it modelled.

Finding a mentor

A manager helps you grow inside the company; a mentor adds an outsider's view. Look outside your own reporting line, at someone in a similar role, a few levels up, or who has done what you want to do. Agree time, expectations, confidentiality and whether the arrangement is time-boxed. Several mentors with different angles (one inside the company, one outside; technical and business) work well. Sources include company mentoring programmes, LinkedIn and dedicated mentoring platforms.

5

Pet projects, communication, interviews

Pet projects. Small, simple side projects keep your technical judgement current and earn credibility with engineers. The author built a chatbot with Amazon Lex, Lambda and CloudWatch to answer her team's repetitive customer questions, learning while removing real toil.

Communication. This is about tact, not vocabulary. A new EM who criticises a teammate's pace on a topic the EM knows well can hurt morale; sharing how they learned it themselves helps instead. Practise through Toastmasters, conference talks, online courses and writing articles.

Interviews. An unusual suggestion: interview occasionally for similar roles even if you aren't looking. It keeps your interviewing skills sharp, shows what the market expects and reveals gaps. (Be careful in small, close-knit hiring markets.)

6

Taking feedback well

Your manager tells you: "In planning, you cut people off when they disagree with you." How do you respond?

Keep a career-gap analysis or growth plan (chapter 3) and review it with your manager. Ask for specific examples and treat feedback as data. Stone and Heen's Thanks for the Feedback goes deeper into receiving feedback well.

7

Questions to close the book

  • How confident do you feel in your role as an engineering leader?
  • How do you keep learning, and is it enough?
  • Do you have a mentor or sounding board?
  • How do you react when your boss or a peer gives you feedback?
  • What are your resolutions for improving as an EM?
