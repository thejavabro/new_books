Fair reviews and the Halo and Horns Effect

Good practice: transparency built on psychological safety, fairness against a leveling guide, recognition on objective criteria, tailored communication, training, and continuous rather than annual management. The trap: the Halo Effect lets one shining trait inflate everything, and the Horns Effect lets one mistake sink everything. Here is one (invented) engineer's year. When the feedback meets pushback

People push back out of fear of looking incompetent, job insecurity, or because they feel overlooked or want faster advancement. You are delivering a "meets most" review to an engineer who expected "exceeds". Choose your replies.

5

Managing high performers

Managers often neglect stars because they seem self-sufficient, yet they are the costliest people to lose. The chapter's markers: proactive, growth mindset, the go-to helper, extreme ownership and self-motivation. Support them with autonomy, growth (even into a different role; one engineer moved into product management on the same team), recognition in the form they prefer, competitive pay, candid feedback and big but sustainable challenges.

One risk: autonomy can turn a star into a single point of failure. The bus factor is how many people would have to disappear before a system is stranded. Toggle who the star has mentored.

6

A framework for underperformance

Underperformance means falling below the role's expectations on timelines, quality or behaviour. It should never come as a surprise to the person. The author's framework follows a real case, a junior engineer called John. Pick the root cause you uncover to see how the path changes.

PIP in practiceJohn's 90-day PIP (30, 45 and 60 are also common) had SMART goals such as answering code review comments within two business days, meeting sprint goals barring technical blockers, and contributing to team docs. It was run with HR and documented weekly.
