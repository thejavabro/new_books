Working with Cross-Functional Partners

Most of what an engineering team ships depends on people outside it. Shared goals, clear roles, a decision process and a feedback loop keep the partnership working.

Who you depend on

David, an EM at a fictional music company, is integrating recommendations across products. His team is capable, yet productivity is falling: priorities get lost between teams, goals conflict, and work styles clash. That is typical. One study cited in the chapter found nearly 75% of cross-functional teams are dysfunctional, often because leadership has no structured approach. Tap a partner to see what you need from each other.

2

Eight common challenges

A profile-page launch stalls because marketing has questions and the PM is unreachable for a week. The chapter lists eight recurring causes: competing priorities, ill-defined goals, poor coordination, unclear roles, conflicts, geography, missing or underperforming partners, and overwhelmed engineers. Diagnose these situations.

3

Forming, storming, norming, performing

Tuckman's model describes how a new group becomes a team. Expect the dip: storming is normal, and the EM's job changes at each stage. Step through (the curve is illustrative).

4

Goals, roles and RACI

Start by aligning on goals: a charter, what is in and out of scope, a few purposeful channels, and anonymous ways to raise concerns. Nemawashi, a Japanese practice, helps here: quietly talk to stakeholders one by one before the formal meeting, so the meeting confirms rather than surprises.

Then make roles explicit with a RACI matrix: Responsible (does the work), Accountable (owns the outcome; exactly one per row), Consulted, Informed. Tap cells to cycle. The checker flags rows with no single accountable owner or nobody doing the work. Note the gap in "deploy the ML model": the chapter's story of Rajesh's team stalled on exactly that unowned step.

5

Collaborating across time zones

The author once coordinated teams in the US, UK, India and Australia. With London a few hours away it was workable; adding India and Australia shrank shared hours to almost nothing. Her fixes: relationships and protocols set up early, regular check-ins per team, clear agendas and roles for design sessions, and async by default (documents and recordings). Pick the locations to see the overlap of 9-to-5 working hours.

Explaining technical work to nontechnical partners

The chapter's example: a team runs a graph database platform. Adoption is booming, but each new customer takes two days of manual onboarding, a leftover from the MVP. In quarterly planning, "tech debt" loses to features unless you translate it into numbers partners care about: reliability, SLAs, product effect and short versus long-term cost. Build the case.

8

Trust, conflict, support and recognition

Trust erodes through hidden decisions, poor communication, weak quality, no accountability and personal friction. Counter it with transparency and by giving everyone a voice. For conflicts, refer back to the kickoff's shared goals and name a directly responsible individual (DRI) whose call is final. Watch for people needing extra help: remote members' time zones, a pinch-hitter engineer for crunch periods, training, appreciation. And recognise partners' work as you would your own team's.
