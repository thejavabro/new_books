Managing People, Teams, and Yourself

Three layers of management: the individuals in front of you, the team as a system, and the manager who has to keep going. Neglect any one and the other two wobble.

Three things to manage

An army plans by understanding its own strengths as well as the opponent's. An EM manages three distinct things: individuals, the team as a whole, and yourself. Each supports the others: matching people's strengths to their work lifts the team, and a manager who is running on empty cannot do either. Tap a ring.

2

One-on-ones and career conversations

A one-on-one is the employee's meeting more than yours: information sharing, social connection, career growth and feedback. Open with "What's top of mind for you?" and listen more than you talk. For career goals the chapter adapts the SMART framework, offered as a flexible starting point rather than a form. Build a goal for a fictional engineer, Alex: pick one option per row and see which letters light up.

Feedback works best in SBI form: the Situation, the Behavior you saw, and its Impact. It is factual, so nobody has to guess. Tag each sentence of this (invented) example. Tap a sentence repeatedly to cycle S, B and I.

Gap analysisA simple table of competencies from the leveling guide, each with SBI evidence and a status (to do, in progress, done), makes career conversations concrete. Frameworks such as SFIA give a shared vocabulary for skills and levels.
3

Supporting junior and senior engineers

There is no one-size-fits-all. Juniors are energetic "blank sheets" who need prescriptive guidance, mentors and encouragement to earn certifications and share what they learn. Seniors may hold stronger opinions and want autonomy and harder problems; give them big, ambiguous challenges and people to mentor. In the author's example, a senior engineer was handed a messy configuration-management problem (a kill switch and a central view of configs) and the resources to own it. Slide through the experience range.

4

Resolving conflict: an escalation ladder

Conflict is normal and can even be healthy. The author's engineers Jack and Jamie split over building a new promotions tool versus reusing a sister team's. Hands-off didn't work, so she climbed a ladder of interventions. Step through it; each step is only needed if the previous one fails.

A challenging teammateA strong senior engineer was dismissive of a junior's well-researched proposal and skipped meetings. The author raised it one-on-one with specific examples, linked it to his own career, then made him the junior's mentor with goals and check-ins. His engagement improved.
Walk the talkA manager who preaches punctuality and then runs late to his own talk undoes the message. Leading by example means doing what you ask of others.
5

Goals, morale and trust

For the team, the EM sets tenets, vision, goals (often OKRs) and a road map, collaboratively and in line with neighbouring teams. Then comes the hard part: keeping morale and trust high. Tick the warning signs you see on a team and read the gauge.

Gemba WalkBorrowed from Toyota: go to "the real place", watch how work is actually done and ask questions. It is for learning, never for blame or snap changes.
6

Persuading another team to take a dependency

Sometimes your road map needs another team's help. In the chapter's scenario, you run the platform team at a car-rental company and are revamping promotions. Royalties for accessory partners would be better owned by the royalty team, which pushes back. Step through how it plays out.

7

Remote teams, metrics and burnout

Remote and hybrid teams get global talent and lower office costs, but lose hallway cues. The chapter's tactics: equipment and secure setups, regular check-ins, cameras on, no multitasking in meetings, virtual events (escape rooms, cooking classes, hackathons) plus an in-person meetup every quarter or half year, and explicit working-hours boundaries.

Burnout usually starts with uneven load. Below, a sprint's tasks are spread across five engineers, each with capacity for 6. Move tasks with the arrows to balance the team, and keep anyone from going red.

For team metrics, set baselines (timelines, SLAs, OKRs, tech debt), survey satisfaction, collect 360 feedback, track growth and promotions, and look at velocity alongside customer adoption.
