Analyze Spreadsheets and Data

Upload a spreadsheet and ask questions in plain words. Claude can clean, summarize and chart it, but every number must be traceable back to the rows.

1

Validation sits before the decision, on purpose

Claude can run code in a secure analysis environment to clean, summarise, chart and project data. The chapter follows an invented training provider deciding which workshops to run more often. Its five steps put validation between the chart and the decision: a chart is not a decision until the numbers behind it are checked.

2

Explain the data and the question before any numbers

"What does this spreadsheet show?" gets a vague answer. "Which course earns the most per session, without hurting attendance?" gives Claude something to calculate. Ask Claude first to explain what one row is, what each column holds, which are categories, dates, counts, money or ratings, what the business question is, and what the data cannot tell you, with "do not edit or calculate yet".

3

Clean the data, and log every change

Real spreadsheets arrive messy. Ask Claude to keep the original sheet, create a cleaned copy plus a Data Issues log, remove only exact duplicates, standardise labels only when they clearly mean the same thing, convert dates, and never fill gaps or fix odd figures by guessing. 4

Allow once, or always?

Claude may ask before running commands or touching files. Read what it plans to do. Allow once approves only this action; continuing permission is for actions you understand well enough to stop being asked about. Match oversight to the stakes.

5

Pick the measure before the chart

Ask for specific calculations (attended = bookings minus no-shows; net revenue = gross minus refunds; attendance rate) and a chart only after you know which numbers matter. The author charts average net revenue per session, because totals favour whatever ran most often. Toggle and watch the ranking flip (invented figures).

6

Do one row by hand

Pick a few rows, do the arithmetic yourself, and compare. Also ask how many rows each figure rests on and what was excluded: an average can sit on a tiny sample or an outlier. Session S-07: 20 bookings, 3 no-shows, £45 fee, £90 refunds.

Can you explain the method?Ask Claude to explain its method in plain language. If you cannot follow it, ask again more simply rather than trusting a number you do not understand. A good closing question: "Which single number would change my decision if it were wrong?"
