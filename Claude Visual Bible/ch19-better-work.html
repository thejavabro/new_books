Choose, Audit and Measure Better Work

Book 4 starts with you, not Claude: decide what "better" means, find the high-value parts of your role, and record improvement as evidence.

1

Choose what better work means to you

Book 4 asks what to do with the ability you have built. There is no single right answer; two readers with identical skills can choose differently and both be right. The book maps four directions. Tap your first choice, then your second. (Earning independently takes real work: be suspicious of any promise of quick income, including Claude's.)

Saved time does not spend itselfFreed hours drift into other routine work unless you decide where they go. Pick one primary direction; scattered effort across many tends to produce less progress.
2

Audit your role: where is the friction?

Over two or three days, list every recurring task, small ones included. Then look for friction: retyping the same message, hunting for the same information, waiting on a step that could run without you. Often the effort around a task dwarfs the task itself. Example: a weekly status update that takes 30 minutes to write and an hour to gather numbers for.

3

Retain, assist, automate, delegate or stop

Retain: needs your judgment, relationships or authority. Assist: you keep it, Claude speeds or checks it. Automate: stable enough for a skill or workflow. Delegate: better done by a colleague, a specialist sub-agent or someone else. Stop: no longer serves a purpose. Classify these.

Which category?

4

Pick one task: most friction, least risk

Do not fix everything. Cross out retain and stop items (they need a decision, not a build). From the rest, pick the one with the clearest friction and lowest risk. Anything involving confidential data, money, legal duties or decisions about people keeps a human check even after you automate the rest. Tap the task you would improve first.

5

Baseline first, then the same four measures

A claim of improvement without evidence is an opinion. Record the baseline before changing anything, then measure time, quality (meets your standard), errors (needed correcting) and outcomes (the real-world result) the same way afterwards.

6

Avoid invented precision

A precise-sounding number built on a guess misleads more than an honest estimate. If you cannot explain how you got a number, round it or call it an estimate.

Which would you put in your record?

7

Keep an achievement record

Memory is a poor archive. Keep one document or sheet with each improvement: task, baseline, result, date, and enough detail to explain it a year from now. Include the ones that only partly worked, with what you learned: a record of nothing but wins looks less credible. Try it here (saved only in this browser).
