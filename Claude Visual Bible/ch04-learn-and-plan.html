Learn Something New and Plan Everyday Life

Use Claude as a patient tutor that makes you do the thinking, and as a planning partner that lays out options without making the decision for you.

1

Ask for an explanation at your level

A good teacher first asks what you already know. Claude cannot see your background unless you say it. Three moves: say what you can already do, name the exact part that confuses you, and set a length. Here is how the same question about compound interest lands at different stated levels.

If it still is not clearSay which part did not land and ask for it another way: shorter, a different example, or fewer new words.
2

From reading to remembering

Reading an explanation is not learning it. The chapter builds a ladder: a learning path of topics in order, analogies (then ask where the analogy breaks), quiz questions without answers that you answer first, and finally explaining it back in your own words for Claude to check. A day or two later, explain it again without looking. The chart is illustrative, not data from the book.

3

Let Claude question you

Ask for questions with the answers held back, answer them, then ask Claude to check. If it slips an answer into a later question, ask for a fresh set. Try a sample exchange (wording ours).

Confident is not correctRules, translations and facts can be stated firmly and still be wrong for your region or dialect. Check anything you will say to another person with a native speaker, textbook or trusted course. Try exercises yourself first; give Claude the job of checking your reasoning, not replacing it.
4

Support, not substitution

Read the AI policy for the specific course and assignment as well as the school's general rule; they increasingly differ. If nothing is written, ask, and keep a record that you did. The line: brainstorming, outlining, explaining a source, checking grammar and reacting to your draft is support. Submitting paragraphs Claude wrote as your own is not, even where AI is welcome, and detectors are unreliable anyway. The question is not "will it be caught?" but "can I honestly defend this as mine?"

5

Helping a child without doing it for them

Pasting a worksheet question in and copying the answer back leaves a child stuck at the next test. Stay in the room, decide what the child sees, and narrate what you are doing ("I'm asking it to question you instead of telling you"). Children copy how adults use tools.

Maths and scienceAsk for a method and hints, one step at a time. Check the working, not just the final number.✗ "Solve this for me."✓ "Don't give the answer; give one hint, then wait."
English essaysThe child writes first; Claude reacts with questions and feedback, never a rewrite.✗ "Write a book report on…"✓ "Don't rewrite. Point out two strengths and one thing to improve."
History and artQuiz dates and causes, suggest project directions, explain a technique seen in a museum.✗ A ready-made "five causes" list.✓ "Ask my child three questions about…"
The deadline trapThe hardest moment to catch: reaching for Claude because time is short, not because the child is stuck. That is the moment to slow down.
6

Plan and compare a real decision

Use a real decision you face. Describe goals and constraints, ask what is missing, get several options before any recommendation (so you do not anchor on the first idea), then compare on criteria you pick. Below, weight your own criteria for a family weekend away (an invented example) and see the ranking move.

Claude leans toward what you seem to wantLeft alone, it tends to favour the option matching your stated preferences. Ask for disadvantages and hidden assumptions at least once, especially for the option that sounds best.
7

As stakes rise, Claude prepares you for a professional

Picking a restaurant: let Claude advise. Health, legal rights, taxes or safety: let Claude help you prepare (list symptoms, draft questions, organise documents), then confirm with a qualified person.

9

Try it now: learn one topic, decide one thing

Learning (15 to 20 minutes): state your goal and what you know, ask for a path, then an explanation, analogy, unanswered quiz, and explain it back. Planning: describe a real decision, ask what is missing, get options, compare them in a table on your own criteria, and ask what could go wrong with your likely choice.
