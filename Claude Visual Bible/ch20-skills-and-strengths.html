Learn New Skills and Combine AI with Human Strengths

Close the gap to the role you want with a learning plan, and lean into the human strengths AI fluency amplifies rather than replaces.

Research what the role looks like today

Roles change with tools and industries. Ask Claude to search current descriptions and recent postings for your target role, list skills that recur across sources, and flag anything newly emphasised. Then check a few sources yourself (Chapters 1.8 and 1.9). Here is the kind of tally you might get for an invented operations analyst search.

2

Map current versus required, one skill at a time

Be specific: "can build a pivot table but not a formula-driven dashboard" is actionable; "comfortable with data" is not. Drag each blue bar to your honest current level; the pink line is the role's requirement. Your two biggest gaps are highlighted.

3

A plan you will actually finish

Pick two or three skills (largest gap, most important to the role), have Claude break each into a learning path, set checkpoints rather than one distant deadline, and keep everything in a project. An overloaded plan is easy to write and hard to finish. Test yours.

4

A demonstrable project, and understanding you can test

Reading about a skill is not using it. Choose a small, real project close to the target role (for example, analysing your own team's data to find which process step causes the most delay). And guard against producing good-looking work you do not understand: explain concepts without notes, do a small task without Claude, and treat "I can follow it but could not have produced it" as a signal to study more. Self-test at the end of each stage.

Real understanding, or dependence?

5

Seven strengths that stay yours

AI fluency is valuable, but not the whole of your value. Seven areas depend on experience, relationships and context. The book's example: two analysts build the same model with Claude; one notices its seasonal-demand assumption contradicts three years of real sales. Her judgment, not the model, protects the business.

6

Notice skill atrophy before it bites

Skills you stop practising fade quietly, especially writing, calculation and structured thinking. Signs: hesitating over something you used to do easily, or no longer catching an obvious error in Claude's work. The fix is deliberate unassisted practice. Illustrative only:

7

The decision stays yours

Claude drafts, analyses and suggests, but carries no consequences. You do. Review important output carefully, question results that feel off even before you can say why, and ask: could I explain and defend this in a meeting without reading from the screen? If not, review it more.
