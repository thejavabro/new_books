The Four Fundamental Team Topologies

Every team in a software organization can be described as one of four types. Use only these four and the purpose of each team, and how it should relate to the others, becomes obvious.

1

Only four team types

Organizations accumulate team types: infrastructure-and-tooling teams, middleware teams, QA teams, "DevOps" teams. The sprawl makes it hard to see whether the right teams exist. The book's answer is to treat four types as magnets: every team should be pulled toward one of them and take on its purpose and behaviours. Research by Jiao Luo and colleagues (2018) found that less ambiguity about roles is key to successful organization design. Tap a pole.

Where is the Ops team? The support team?Deliberately absent. Stream-aligned teams run what they build, even when little new code is being written, so there's no hand-over. Operations and support become aligned to streams (see "Silos and support" below).
2

Stream-aligned teams: the main event

A stream is a continuous flow of work aligned to a business domain or capability. A stream-aligned team owns one valuable stream (a product, a service, a set of features, a user journey, a persona) and delivers it end to end with no hand-offs. It's funded long term, not as a project. Most teams should be this type; the other three exist to reduce its burden.

Nine capabilities, not nine people

A stream-aligned team needs all of these as a team. Pick a staffing style for a six-person team and see how well it covers them.

3

Enabling teams: help, then leave

High performers keep improving their capabilities, but a stream-aligned team under delivery pressure has little room to research and learn. An enabling team of specialists does the research, tries options and coaches teams through new practices (Jutta Eckstein calls them "technical consulting teams"). They are not an ivory tower dictating choices. The book warns that teams underestimate the effort of learning by ten to fifteen times. Watch an enabling team move through an organization.

Greenleaf's test for enabling teamsDo those served grow? Do they, while being served, become healthier, wiser, freer, more autonomous? If the enabling team does its job, the team it helps no longer needs it after a few weeks or months.

Case study: an enablement team at a large legal organization (2017)

A mix of external consultants and the organization's own developers. They published a charter, planned for their own extinction from day one, and spent only about a quarter of their time implementing; the rest went on sharing knowledge through mob programming, recorded demos and open showcases. Community of practiceDiffuse group of interested people from many teams, meeting weekly or monthly. Spreads knowledge widely. The two can coexist.
4

Complicated-subsystem teams: only when you must

A complicated-subsystem team owns a part of the system that needs so much specialist knowledge that most of its members must be specialists: a video codec, a mathematical model, real-time trade reconciliation, face recognition. It exists to reduce the cognitive load on the stream-aligned teams that use the subsystem. The key difference from a traditional component team: it is created because of cognitive load, not because a component looks shareable. So there should be few of them. Run the check.

Behaviour to expect: close collaboration with stream-aligned teams while the subsystem is being explored, then mostly X-as-a-Service with a focus on the interface once it stabilizes; delivery faster and better than when a stream team owned it; and priorities set by the needs of the stream-aligned teams that use it.

5

Platform teams: a compelling internal product

Evan Bottcher defines a digital platform as a foundation of self-service APIs, tools, services, knowledge and support, arranged as a compelling internal product. Stream-aligned teams still own building, running and fixing their apps; the platform takes away the lower-level detail (provisioning, monitoring, deployment). Its value is measured by the value of the services it gives product teams (Peter Neumark, Prezi). Slide from thinnest to thickest.

Thinnest viable platformAllan Kelly warns that developers love building platforms and, without product management, will build a bigger one than needed. Aim for the smallest platform that still accelerates and simplifies delivery. Fewer services of good quality beat many shaky ones. To stop every team asking for "premium" service, Don Reinertsen suggests internal pricing, such as tracking cloud costs per team.

Sky Betting & Gaming learned the hard way: a central team built a large Chef configuration environment to fix disaster recovery, and soon many teams were tripping over its shared dependencies and differing priorities (the story continues in Chapter 8). Auto Trader turned an enabling continuous-delivery team into an Infrastructure Engineering platform, with an Ops "squad buddy" for each dev squad instead of embedding Ops people.

6

No silos in the flow of change

Teams of a single function (QA, DBA, UX, architecture, ETL, a central Ops team) sit across the flow and force hand-offs. Specialist teams may support the stream, but never sit in it. A pleasant side effect of cross-functional teams: solutions that need deep expertise lose out to simpler ones everyone on the team understands.

Support works best aligned to streams, sitting with the tribe that builds the software (some rename them "service-experience teams"), plus dynamic swarming for big incidents. Step through an incident.

Two effects: streams stay independent at runtime (a single support team for everything pulls production toward a monolith, by Conway's law), and new flaws are fed back quickly to the teams building the systems. Frontline staff in swarms also learn faster. A service desk for phone or in-person contact still exists, off to one side of the flow.

7

Game: convert your teams

Most organizations would gain by mapping each existing team to one of the four types and changing its remit to match. Classify each team as the book recommends.

Check your ratio

Successful organizations report roughly 6 to 9 stream-aligned teams for every other team.
