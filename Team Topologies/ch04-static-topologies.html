1

Two ways teams form by accident

A team topology is a team structure placed deliberately in the organization, with a clear boundary of responsibility. Most organizations don't design them; teams just happen. Two anti-patterns turn up again and again.

Questions to design with insteadGiven our skills, constraints, cultural and engineering maturity, desired architecture and business goals: which topology will help us deliver faster and safer? How do we reduce hand-offs in the main flow of change? Where should software boundaries go, and how can teams align to them?
2

Design for the flow of change

Many organizations treat delivery as a one-way trip: specification, design, coding, testing, release, then "business as usual" operations, each owned by a different silo. Nothing comes back from the live system. Organizations built for flow keep the work inside one stream-aligned team and let operational information flow back into it. Watch a change travel.

Accelerate concluded that delivery teams should be cross-functional, with the skills to design, develop, test, deploy and operate on the same team. Frontline teams are also the organization's best sensors: when information from live systems, and from other teams, flows back, problems in platforms and interfaces surface early.

3

The Spotify model, and why not to copy it

Spotify's 2012 arrangement: squads of 5 to 9 with a long-term mission, grouped into tribes; people with the same skills across a tribe form a chapter (where line management happens, led by someone who still works in a squad); and looser guilds cross the whole company. Tap a person to see their four groupings.

A snapshot, not a recipeSpotify's own authors called it "a journey in progress, not a journey completed". Many organizations copied the boxes without the purpose, culture or trajectory behind them.
4

DevOps and the wall of confusion

Around 2009, as Agile pushed for more frequent releases, development threw software over the "wall of confusion" to operations, mostly via tickets. DevOps raised awareness that the problem was how teams interacted, and that it applied to QA, InfoSec, networking and others too. Organizations that only bought tools and didn't fix team misalignment missed most of the benefit.

Matthew Skelton's DevOps Topologies catalog (2013, later expanded with Manuel Pais) collects team patterns and anti-patterns for these conversations. Two ideas run through it: there is no one-size-fits-all structure, and there are several known-bad ones. In short: no "right" topology, but several "bad" ones for any given organization.

5

Patterns that work only in the right setting

A team pattern succeeds or fails mostly because of the environment around it, not the people in it. Pick a pattern to see what it needs.

6

SRE: a relationship that changes over time

Google's Site Reliability Engineering is "what happens when you ask a software engineer to design an operations function" (Ben Treynor). SREs code, automate away toil, and balance features against reliability using service-level objectives and error budgets. What makes it interesting for team design is that the relationship between the SRE team and an application team moves through stages. Step through.

SRE is optionalNot every Google team uses it. As one Google SRE put it, scale SRE support down as a project shrinks, and let the development team own the SRE work if the scale doesn't need it. Without strong engineering discipline and management commitment, the balance slides back into an "us and them" silo.
7

Choose for your size and maturity

Organizations at different stages need different structures. By 2013 Amazon and Netflix already had end-to-end cross-functional teams. A traditional organization new to Agile may benefit from a temporary DevOps team of experienced engineers, but only with a clear mission and an expiry date; otherwise it becomes one more silo. A large enterprise may need DevOps evangelists. Pick your situation.

Split responsibilities to break silosSeparating database development (inside the stream-aligned teams) from database administration (which moves into a platform or a cloud Database-as-a-Service) removes a blocking dependency on a central database team. Rather than adding people as work grows, ask which dependencies to remove and which to accept on purpose.
8

Detect and track dependencies

To keep teams independent you have to see the dependencies. Dominica DeGrandis suggests a physical dependency matrix or dependency tags on kanban cards. Strode and Huff classify dependencies as knowledge, task or resource. Spotify used a simple spreadsheet that flagged whether a dependency stayed inside a tribe (fine) or crossed tribes (a warning that team design or work assignment may be wrong). Add dependencies and watch the alert.

9

Use the topologies to plan an evolution

The DevOps Topologies were always meant as snapshots. Some organizations used them to plan a path from today's best fit toward a target. Step through Accenture's work with a healthcare client.

TransUnion did the sameExpanding from 2014, the UK credit agency used the patterns to avoid a separate "DevOps team": two temporary DevOps teams helped bring Dev and Ops together over time. The journey was an evolution of team relationships, not a one-off reconfiguration.

The classic trap is the "DevOps team" anti-pattern: sensible on paper, but if it executes steps in every application's delivery path instead of building self-service capabilities, it becomes a hard dependency for everyone.
