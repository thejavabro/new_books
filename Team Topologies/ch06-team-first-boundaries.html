Choose Team-First Boundaries

Where you cut the software decides how teams can work. Look for natural seams, the fracture planes, and split along them so each team owns something that fits its cognitive load.

1

Boundaries decide whether teams can flow

When each team depends on a web of other teams, flow is impossible. Most organizations give little thought to whether the responsibilities they hand teams are viable, and the result is lack of ownership, disengagement and very slow delivery. Eric Evans puts it bluntly: when code doesn't work, the problem starts in how teams are organized and interact.

Accelerate found tightly coupled architectures hurt team autonomy, and pointed to bounded contexts and APIs as ways to decouple. But splitting a monolith without thinking about the teams (their cognitive capacity, location and interests) can cut in the wrong places and produce a distributed monolith: many services, yet almost every change touches several of them.

2

Spot the hidden monolith

Monoliths come in more shapes than one big application. An organization can split its app into services and still have a monolithic release further down the pipeline. Know which kinds you have before you start changing things. Name each one from its symptoms.

Amy Phillips' testIf you have microservices but wait to end-to-end test a combination of them before each release, what you have is a distributed monolith.
3

Split along fracture planes

"Monolith" means single stone. Stonemasons strike a rock along its natural fracture planes to split it cleanly. Software has seams too. Splitting has costs (less consistency, possible data duplication, a patchier user experience, more distributed complexity), so look for the natural seams. 4

Mostly, follow the business domain

Most fracture planes should map to business-domain bounded contexts, a term from Eric Evans' Domain-Driven Design: a part of a larger domain with its own internally consistent model and a ubiquitous language shared by developers and domain experts. Finding them takes business and technical knowledge, so expect early mistakes and piecemeal evolution. As Michael Nygard notes, a concept can look atomic only because one word covers it; look harder and seams appear.

One word, three meanings

Aligning to bounded contexts aligns technology with the business, cuts "lost in translation" rework, and lets business owners and technologists speak one language inside each context. These contexts usually map to natural streams of change, so they can be owned by stream-aligned teams.

6

The litmus test

For any proposed split, two questions decide it. Does the resulting architecture support more autonomous teams (less dependent) with reduced cognitive load (less disparate responsibilities)? And the quick heuristic: could we, as a team, effectively consume or provide this subsystem as a service? Try a candidate.

Case study: Poppulo, from one team to ten

Expecting rapid growth in 2015, Poppulo first focused on the team (pairing, then mobbing) and added telemetry and end-to-end pipelines so teams could see and own their code. They used event storming to model domains (people, content, events, email, mobile, analytics), played through scenarios on whiteboards to check how independent each was, and then split along those lines. Cross-functional "matrix product teams" own an area completely; Pact contract tests set expectations between teams. A few areas follow regulatory (ISO 27001) or cross-domain reporting boundaries, a UX team acts as internal consultants, and an SRE capability handles traffic. Engineering grew from 16 to 70 people.

7

When a technology split is right

Technology splits are usually a bad idea, but there are valid exceptions. A large manufacturer makes IoT devices controlled from a mobile app and from the cloud. One stream-aligned team owning embedded, cloud and mobile end to end would need a rare skill mix and suffer huge context switching. The very different pace of change of each stack is the real reason for separate teams. Then one of them must act as a platform for the others. Compare the two options.

Features that cross stacks still need coordination (for example, cloud API changes before a device release). That coordination is a chance to build shared practices such as semantic versioning, consistent logging and API-first development, which may later allow team boundaries to change as the stacks' pace of change converges.
