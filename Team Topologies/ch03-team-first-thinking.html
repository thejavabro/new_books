Team-First Thinking

The team, not the individual, is the unit that delivers software. So size teams to fit how humans trust each other, give them only as much as they can hold in their heads, and make their edges explicit.

1

The team is the unit of delivery

Research from Driskell and Salas, the US Army's "team of teams" and Google's own studies all point the same way: for knowledge-rich problem solving, a cohesive team beats a collection of individuals, and who is on the team matters less than how the team works together. So in this book "team" means something specific:

A team is…a stable group of five to nine people working toward a shared goal as a unit. It is the smallest unit of delivery, so work is assigned to teams, never to individuals.
2

Sizes that trust can hold

Seven to nine (Amazon's "two-pizza team") comes from limits on human trust described by anthropologist Robin Dunbar. The same limits apply one level up: to tribes, departments and business lines. When a grouping outgrows its circle, the dynamics change and it is time to split. Slide the group size.

GroupingNormal limitHigh-trust limit
Single team5 to 8 (up to 9)up to 15

The circles: about 5 close relationships, 15 deep trust, 50 mutual trust, 150 people whose capabilities we can remember. Researchers suggest further limits near 500 and 1,500 (roughly a ×3 step). When groupings change, the software architecture should be realigned so each team can still own its part: team-first architecture.

3

Flow the work to long-lived teams

A team takes from two weeks to three months or more to gel. Breaking it up after a six-month project throws that away. Brooks's law adds that new members slow a team down at first: ramp-up, more communication lines, and a fresh round of "storming". The advice: keep teams stable but not static and bring the work to them. Compare the two policies.

TuckmanForming → storming → norming → performingPamela Knight found storming keeps happening throughout a team's life, so team dynamics need continuous care.
High trustMove every 9 to 12 monthsPivotal rotated engineers about yearly without hurting performance.
Typical trustStay 18 to 24 monthsIn lower-trust settings people should stay put longer, with coaching for cohesion.
4

The team owns the software

Every part of the system is owned by exactly one team. Others may send pull requests or suggestions, and the owner might trust another team with temporary access, but ownership never splits. When several teams change the same code, nobody owns the mess. Ownership also lets a team plan across horizons: today's quick fix is fine if it knows it will clean it up in a few weeks.

Gardening, not policingOwnership is stewardship, not territory. The team cares for the code; individuals don't guard "their" files.

A team-first mindset in practice: arrive on time, keep discussions on track, focus on team goals, unblock teammates before starting new work, mentor newcomers, and explore options instead of "winning" arguments. Some people won't put the team first even with coaching; the book is blunt that such "team toxic" people need to be removed before they damage the team. Two more team-first habits: diverse teams find creative solutions faster and empathize better with other teams; and reward the whole team, not individuals (Deming argued against merit ratings; Nokia paid small, team-based bonuses). Even the training budget can belong to the team.

5

Three kinds of cognitive load

John Sweller defined cognitive load as the total mental effort being used in working memory, and split it three ways. The aim for a delivery team: minimize intrinsic load (training, good tech choices, pairing), eliminate extraneous load (automate the fiddly stuff), and leave room for germane load, where the valuable thinking happens. 6

Limit the number and type of domains

There is no formula for cognitive load, and lines of code are a poor proxy. Start by asking the team, without judgment, whether it feels effective and able to respond in time. Then count its domains and classify each as simple (clear path), complicated (needs analysis and some iteration) or complex (needs experimentation and discovery). Compare pairs of domains across teams to fine-tune. Build a team's load and see what the book's heuristics say.

OutSystems split into microteamsThe overloaded engineering productivity team from Chapter 1 split into three microteams, one domain each (IDE productivity, platform-server productivity, infrastructure automation), the first two sitting with the product areas they served. Cross-domain issues were rare, so the old single team had been optimizing for the exception. Within months motivation, flow and quality all rose.
7

Size the software to fit the team

Don't argue "monolith or microservices" in the abstract. Design boundaries so every subsystem fits the cognitive load of the one team that owns it. Toggle the two pictures.

A team can take on a bigger subsystem if the environment lowers its intrinsic and extraneous load: a team-first workspace, fewer meetings and emails, a dedicated person for support queries, management that sets outcomes and stays "eyes on, hands off" (McChrystal), good developer experience from the teams it depends on, and a platform designed to reduce load. Without these, subsystems must be smaller, which means more teams and more cost. IKEA's high-performing mobile team discovered it was carrying two products in one codebase and split in two, despite having autonomy, mastery and purpose. A shared mental model of the code is also a good predictor of team performance.

One heuristic to remember"Minimize cognitive load for others" is one of the most useful rules in software development.
8

Design a team API

Stable teams that own software can publish an API for the whole team, not just its code. It should be easy for other teams to use, and kept up to date. Fill in a card for an imaginary team.

Pivotal Cloud Foundry kept more than fifty teams apart with contract-based, API-style separation: no shared repositories, changes by pull request or cross-team pairing. AWS went further: every other team is a potential source of denial-of-service, so services need service levels, quotas and throttling.

Make the virtual space navigable

Predictable chat-channel prefixes help people find the right place fast. Prefixing display names, like "[Platform] Jai Kale", shows which team someone belongs to.

9

Space for three modes of work

Teams need time, space and money to meet across team lines: guilds, communities of practice, internal conferences. Teams that rehearse interacting there find it easier to interact on real work. Physically, neither cubicles nor pure open-plan suits teams. Office design should support three modes. Tap each.

Engineering practices are foundationalNone of this works without continuous delivery, test-first development, and attention to operability and releasability. Team-first design multiplies good engineering; it doesn't replace it.
