Evolve Team Structures with Organizational Sensing

Team topologies are not a one-off reorg. Interaction modes should change as discovery turns into delivery, and the organization should watch for signals that its structure no longer fits.

1

Design the design rules

Markets, regulations, users and technology all keep shifting. So what matters most is not the shape of the organization today but the rules and heuristics for changing it when new challenges arrive. Conway himself said the first design is almost never the best, so flexibility of organization matters.

Neither main mode is better; each suits different work. Collaboration pays a collaboration tax in cognitive load, worth it only for rapid innovation. X-as-a-Service is clearer and lighter, but innovates more slowly across its clean API. Accelerate's high performers needed little communication between delivery teams: loosely coupled architecture and teams.

Should this collaboration continue?

Unnecessary collaboration is expensive and can hide gaps in platforms or capabilities. Ongoing collaboration must be justified as one of three things.

uSwitch: tech change to drive team changeAfter years of creeping complexity, uSwitch's dev teams had to understand too much of the underlying stack. They adopted Kubernetes as a platform abstraction to cut that load: "We didn't change our organization because we wanted to use Kubernetes; we used Kubernetes because we wanted to change our organization." That is strategic technology leadership.
2

Collaborate on purpose to learn

Switching two teams into collaboration for a few months can produce a step change in capability, especially when their backgrounds differ. A cloud team building analytics for IoT data, and an embedded team writing device software, trade what each knows. Tap a team to see what it brings.

3

TransUnion: an evolution, not a reorg

In 2014 Dev and Ops at TransUnion were far apart. The gap was too wide for one team of evangelists, so they made two: a system-build (SB) team from Dev and a platform-build (PB) team from Ops, and got those two collaborating first. Step through.

The planned twelve-month transition took about three years longer, partly due to the move to Azure. People coped because everyone knew it was an evolution with clear but changing boundaries. Results: safer, more regular production changes, fewer deployment mistakes, better traceability for a regulated business.

4

From collaboration to X-as-a-Service

Interaction modes should change regularly. Explore a domain another team owns? Agree to collaborate for a set time. Need predictability after discovery? Move toward X-as-a-Service and tighten the API. In an enterprise this "discover, then establish" pattern runs continuously, with different teams at different stages. Drag through time.

Topologies change over months, not days or weeks, and the architecture changes with them. Sometimes that means seating people together to encourage collaboration, other times moving teams to different floors to help enforce an API boundary. As John Kotter says, strategy is ongoing "searching, doing, learning, and modifying". Jeff Sussna calls the result a continuous design capability.

Sky Betting & Gaming, part 2The Platform Evolution team that had built the Chef monolith became Platform Services: a product team offering managed services (AWS integrations, build and test environments, logging) driven by its customers. Boundary disputes with Infrastructure, the last function still split between delivery and operations, led Infrastructure itself to reorganize around products and services. Platform Services moved into it; firewall and load-balancer automation went to networking squads. Now there are infrastructure-platform feature teams, and everyone knows whom to talk to.
5

Several modes at once

At any moment, different teams need different interactions. Four stream-aligned teams: one collaborates with the platform team on new logging technology, the other three consume the platform as a service, and an enabling team facilitates all four. Because the modes are explicit and tied to purposes, everyone understands why they work differently, and friction becomes a useful signal.

6

Triggers to evolve your topology

Mapping teams to the four types at one moment is easy. Knowing when to change is harder. Tick the symptoms you see.

The platform wrapper

For the third trigger: (1) "platformize" the many lower-level services behind a thin wrapper with a consistent developer experience (correlation IDs, health checks, test harnesses, SLOs, diagnostic APIs); (2) stream-aligned teams own just enough telemetry and diagnostics to trace their flow end to end.

7

Organizational sensing and self-steering

Treat operations as rich sensory input to development, and you get a cybernetic feedback loop that lets the organization steer itself. Stable teams with well-defined communication pathways act like an organism's nerves and senses (Peter Drucker's "synthetic sense organs for the outside"). Organizations with unstable teams, dependence on key individuals and suppressed voices are "senseless" in both meanings. Pick a question an organization should keep asking.

Handing maintenance to a cheaper team is false economy (Sriram Narayan). And the operations service desk should include some of the most experienced engineers, who can triage quickly and send accurate signals back to the builders.
