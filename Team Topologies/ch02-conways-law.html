Conway's Law and Why It Matters

Systems end up shaped like the communication paths of the organization that builds them. You can fight that force, or you can use it: design the teams first and let the architecture follow.

1

What Conway actually said

Mel Conway's 1968 paper, "How Do Committees Invent?", observed that an organization designing a system is constrained to produce a design that copies its own communication structure. Not "tends to", constrained. Ruth Malan's modern restatement is blunter: if the architecture of the system and the architecture of the organization are at odds, the organization wins.

The evidence is more than anecdote. Alan MacCormack's team at Harvard compared open- and closed-source products and found strong support for architecture mirroring organization; studies of cars and aircraft engines agree. Adidas went from an IT cost center relying on one vendor and many hand-offs to in-house, business-aligned teams, and quality improved along with speed.

Why the shared database keeps appearingMonolithic shared databases are a classic Conway artifact: specialist DBA teams, short-lived outsourced projects and stack-layer teams all funnel changes through one place. The database then couples every application and attracts stray business logic. Avoiding it takes the right team structure as well as good architecture.
2

Teams in, architecture out

The book's deliberately simple example. Four teams each have front-end and back-end developers, and all database changes go through one DBA. Rotate the team diagram ninety degrees and you get the architecture that will "naturally" emerge. Then try the reverse Conway maneuver.

If four two-tier apps over one shared database is what you want, fine. If you want independent microservices with their own data stores, the shared DBA's "fan-in" pulls against you. Put data skills inside each team (perhaps with a central DBA group for advice) and the force works for you instead.

3

Game: predict the architecture

Conway also wrote that for any team organization there is a class of designs it cannot effectively pursue, because the communication paths do not exist. The organization limits the "solution search space". Read each organization and predict the system it will probably build.

4

Architecture for team-scoped flow

Michael Nygard: "Team assignments are the first draft of the architecture." Since the team is the unit of delivery, the architecture should let each team move fast on its own. Happily that just means classic good practice. Tap each.

At a conceptual level, the architecture should look like the flows of change it enables: streams running on top of a platform, rather than a web of interconnected components. MacCormack calls the result an "architecture for participation": modules small enough to understand, and changes that don't ripple. Don Reinertsen adds that we can partition the architecture to "gracefully absorb change".

Organization design needs technical peopleIf managers decide which teams build which services, managers are deciding the architecture (Ruth Malan). So decisions about team shape and boundaries should never be made without technical leaders, and architects need a say in organization structure. Technical people bring the right concepts: interfaces, encapsulation, abstraction.
5

Restrict unnecessary communication

More communication is not always better. If everyone has to talk to everyone, Conway's law predicts a tangled, tightly coupled system. The aim is high bandwidth inside a team, mid bandwidth between a few paired teams, and low bandwidth everywhere else (after Henrik Kniberg). Change the number of teams. In the designed version, teams work in a few close pairs (thick) and reach a platform with low-bandwidth, self-service calls (dotted).

Mike Cohn's health check: does the structure minimize paths between teams, and does it encourage teams to talk who otherwise wouldn't? When two teams that shouldn't need each other are talking a lot, ask why. Is an API weak? Is the platform unsuitable? Is a component missing, or are two parts in the same repository that should be split along a fracture plane (Chapter 6)? Practical levers: seat teams apart, and watch chat traffic for unexpected pairs. A large team that splits its time between two separate areas can split in two, but only if different people work on each; if the whole team works on both by design, keep it together.

Warning signs"Everyone should read every message in the channel." "Everyone attends the giant stand-up." "Everyone must be in the meeting to approve." Each is an organization design problem in disguise.
6

Tool choices drive communication

Shared tools pull teams together; separate tools push them apart. So choose tools with team relationships in mind: shared tools for teams that must collaborate, separate tools (or instances) for teams with a clear boundary. Decide each case.
