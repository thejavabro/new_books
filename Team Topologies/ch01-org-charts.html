The Problem with Org Charts

The org chart shows who reports to whom. It does not show how work actually gets done. This opening chapter sets out why team structure, team interaction and cognitive load deserve as much design attention as the software itself.

Start with your problem

The authors call this a "functional" book: each chapter stands fairly well on its own, and the preface suggests reading routes for common situations. Their analogy is that Team Topologies is sheet music for an orchestra, not a melody for a star soloist. It gives a shared vocabulary and structure, and leaves plenty to the players. Pick the problem closest to yours.

The four ideas the book builds onAn organization is a sociotechnical system: people and technology shaping each other (cybernetics, systems thinking, Cynefin). A team behaves differently from a collection of individuals and needs nurturing (Tuckman, Lencioni). Conway's law drives the shape of software. And real organizations such as Adidas, Auto Trader, Ericsson and Netflix show gains from team changes within months.
2

The org chart versus how work happens

An org chart draws reporting lines that run up and down. Real work does not follow them. People reach sideways to whoever they depend on, and bend the rules to get things done. That sideways problem solving is valuable, so it should be supported, not squeezed back into the boxes. The chart still has a use, mostly for compliance and legal reporting, but it is a poor tool for splitting up work.

Like an architecture document, an org chart goes stale the moment work begins. Matrix management tried to fix this by giving people two bosses, but it is still a static picture. Each new reorg breaks the working relationships that teams had just built, which is why re-orgs bring so much fear.

3

Three structures in every organization

Niels Pflaeging, in Organize for Complexity, argues that every organization has three structures at once. Team Topologies cares most about the last two, and about how teams interact within them.

4

Local fixes, global bottlenecks

Decisions made along org-chart lines tend to optimize one department and ignore what happens upstream and downstream. The book's example: a team adopts cloud and infrastructure as code, so provisioning drops from weeks to minutes. But if every production change still waits for a board that meets once a week, delivery is still weekly at best. Try it.

Systems thinking in one loopLook at the whole flow of work, find the biggest bottleneck today, remove it, then repeat. Rigid team structures and poor team communication may not be your biggest bottleneck yet, but the book argues every growing organization runs into them eventually.
5

Four team types, three interaction modes

Team Topologies gives a small, fixed vocabulary: four team types and three ways teams interact. Combined with Conway's law, attention to cognitive load and the idea of a "sensing" organization, this is the whole model. Every diagram on these pages uses the same key. Tap a shape.

6

Conway's law, revived

In 1968 Mel Conway observed that organizations designing systems are constrained to produce designs that copy their own communication structures. Allan Kelly calls this pull the homomorphic force: software and teams drift toward the same shape. Eric Raymond's joke puts it best: four groups writing a compiler will produce a four-pass compiler. Change the number of groups.

Around 2015, as microservices took off, James Lewis and others proposed the reverse (or inverse) Conway maneuver: organize the teams to match the architecture you want, instead of handing a blueprint to whatever teams exist. Chapter 2 goes deeper.

7

Cognitive load: the forgotten constraint

Each person can hold only so much in mind; a team's capacity is roughly the sum. Yet responsibilities are handed to teams as if their capacity were unlimited and their existing systems needed no care. The book's OutSystems example: an engineering productivity team kept adding CI, CD and infrastructure automation to its remit. Sprint planning became a jumble of requests, context switching soared, and motivation fell. Add the responsibilities and watch Dan Pink's three motivators.

The fix the book argues for, and develops in Chapter 3, is to put the team first: size its responsibilities to fit what it can hold, and design how it talks to other teams.

8

From obstacles to fast flow

Many organizations treated software as manufacturing: individuals in functional silos, large projects planned up front, little thought for how people and technology interact. Agile, Lean IT and DevOps showed the value of small, autonomous teams aligned to the business, but organizations often adopted the tools and left the team structure alone. Tap each obstacle to flip it to the Team Topologies view.

The goalKnow where and when collaboration is needed, and when it is better to focus on execution and cut communication overhead. The book's image is the grouper and the moray eel: two unrelated species that signal each other to hunt together, the eel flushing prey out of crevices for the grouper.

Five rules of thumb for organization design

Naomi Stanford lists five rules for designing organizations. Team Topologies adds what most organization design misses for tech companies: dynamic change and the ability to sense when it is needed. Tick the ones your organization already follows.
