1

Integration as a non-event

Without continuous integration, software is assumed broken until someone proves it works, usually during a long, painful integration phase at the end. With CI, every change triggers a build and a run of automated tests, so the software is proven to work with every check-in, and any break is found within minutes and fixed at once. The preface describes it as building and testing every change "so you can ensure that your software is always in a working state".

3

Check in regularly

The longer you wait between check-ins, the more your copy diverges from everyone else's and the harder the merge. Small, frequent commits also mean that when the build breaks, the culprit is a small change that is easy to understand or revert.

Making the build guard the codebase

Beyond the essentials, the chapter suggests using the build to enforce standards: fail it for architectural breaches (a forbidden call from one layer to another), for tests that are too slow, and for compiler warnings or style violations. Extreme Programming's practices (TDD, pair programming, refactoring, collective ownership) support CI and are supported by it. Pick which checks this build enforces.


    

  






  
7
Distributed teams and DVCS

  

    

      
Teams spread over sites and time zones need CI even more, because informal hallway communication is gone. The headings cover the impact on process, a centralized CI service shared by every site, the technical issues of slow links to a central repository, and alternative approaches when the network makes that hard.

      
Distributed version control systems (Git, Mercurial) give every developer a full repository, which makes local commits cheap. The book's stance, developed in Chapter 14, is that this helps, as long as people still merge to a shared mainline frequently; a DVCS does not remove the need to integrate.

Always workingEvery change is built and tested; the software is never broken for long.
