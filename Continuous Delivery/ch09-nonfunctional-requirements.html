Testing Nonfunctional Requirements

Performance, capacity, security and availability make or break real systems. Treat them as requirements, measure them with automated capacity tests, and put those tests in the pipeline.

1

Performance, throughput, capacity

The chapter starts by pinning down words that are often used loosely. Then it argues that nonfunctional requirements (NFRs) such as capacity, security, availability, maintainability and auditability carry as much delivery risk as functional ones: a system that does everything but falls over at peak load, or leaks card numbers, has failed.

PerformanceHow long one transaction takes to process, on its own or under load.
ThroughputHow many transactions the system can process in a given period.
CapacityThe maximum throughput the system can sustain while keeping response times acceptable.
2

NFRs pull against each other

NFRs are crosscutting: they touch many parts of the system and shape its architecture, so leaving them until late is expensive. They also interact unhelpfully. Very secure systems are often harder to use; highly flexible ones are often slower. So everyone (developers, ops, testers and the customer) needs to think about them at the start and agree the trade-offs. Push one quality up and watch the others.

3

Analyse NFRs with real numbers

For a project in flight, capture NFRs as stories or acceptance criteria with enough detail to estimate and prioritise them against features. "As fast as possible" or "the system must be secure" cannot be tested or traded off. Say what, under which conditions, and how much.

Another misuse: writing performance requirements as a lazy way to express usability. "Every page loads in under 0.5 s" may really mean "users shouldn't feel kept waiting", which a progress indicator might satisfy more cheaply.

Don't optimize prematurely, but plan for capacity

Poorly analysed NFRs lead teams to over-engineer: the chapter recalls a legacy system built around an elaborate in-memory scheme for a load it never saw, which made every change hard. Knuth's warning applies: premature optimization is the root of all evil, and the key is to find the real bottleneck first. Yet high-capacity systems can't be bolted together at the end either. 5

What to measure, and what counts as a pass

Capacity testing covers scalability (how response time and concurrency change as you add servers), longevity (does it degrade over days), throughput (transactions per second), and load (what happens as load rises to production levels and beyond). Benchmarks catch specific regressions; realistic scenarios mixing many kinds of user activity catch the rest. Drag the load and see where the system falls over the threshold.

Make it a test, not a measurementA capacity test must have a pass threshold. Set it from the requirement, slightly below the system's demonstrated ability, then ratchet it up as the system improves; if set too high it flickers between pass and fail and gets ignored. Aim for stable, reproducible runs: dedicated environment, no other work on the machines, and repeatable data.

7

The capacity-testing environment

Absolute capacity measurements should happen in an environment as close to production as you can afford, and dedicated to capacity tests. Don't assume linear scaling. A team at a well-known web company found their capacity rig, made of underpowered machines, had less processing power than a cluster of iPods. The chapter also recounts a client who refused a second production-sized environment and relied on a scaling factor from a smaller rig; production still threw up problems the factor never predicted. A reasonable compromise: a slice of production, scaled down in a consistent ratio.

8

Automating capacity tests

Capacity tests should test real-world scenarios, have predefined success thresholds, be short, be robust to change, compose into bigger scenarios, and be repeatable in sequence or in parallel. Where to inject load? Through the UI (realistic but costly and often impractical at scale), through a service or public API, or through a lower-level API. A good trick: record the interactions that acceptance tests make, turn them into templates, and replay many copies with fresh data.

Capacity tests in the deployment pipeline

  

  
Beyond gating releases, the capacity rig becomes an experimental lab: reproducing production defects, finding memory leaks, longevity runs, tuning garbage collection and configuration, simulating worst days and integration failures, rehearsing rollbacks, and testing graceful degradation.
