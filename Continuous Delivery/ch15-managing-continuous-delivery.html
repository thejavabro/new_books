Performance and conformance are not a zero-sum game

The closing chapter is for managers. It frames delivery with CIMA's definition of enterprise governance: conformance (compliance, risk, assurance) and performance (value, resource use). The business wants new software out fast; risk and compliance people want control. The authors argue these aren't in conflict: the deployment pipeline gives speed through automation and incremental delivery, and gives conformance through visibility, traceability and repeatable, audited processes.

A maturity model for configuration and release management

The book's maturity model has five levels, from −1 Regressive through 0 Repeatable, 1 Consistent, 2 Quantitatively managed, to 3 Optimizing, across six practice areas. The descriptions below are paraphrased. Rate your organization, then use the model as the chapter suggests: pick the area where immaturity hurts most, plan a change with acceptance criteria, implement it, measure, and repeat.

The lifecycle of an iterative project

The chapter sketches five phases, compatible with ITIL's view of service strategy, design, transition and operation, but iterative. Step through them.

The underpants gnomesThe authors find it startlingly common to start an IT program with no business case, like the South Park gnomes' plan: 1. Collect underpants. 2. ?. 3. Profit. Without a business case you cannot prioritise requirements. Also identify every stakeholder up front, including ops, sales, marketing and support.
4

Why iterate, and the Nokia test

The book's minimum for iterative development: software is always working (proved by an automated test suite), working software is deployed to a production-like environment every iteration, and iterations are no longer than two weeks. Benefits: high-value features ship early; regular customer feedback; "done" means signed off; and the team is pushed toward production-readiness, including nonfunctional requirements, all along.

Scrum is popular, but the chapter flags three failure modes: lack of commitment, ignoring good engineering (Martin Fowler's "flaccid Scrum"), and adapting the process until it isn't agile. Nokia created a test for the last one. Try the first part.

5

Risk management 101

Risk management means identifying the main risks, putting mitigation in place, and continuing to identify and manage them throughout. Not every risk needs a mitigation: following Tom DeMarco and Timothy Lister, weigh each risk by likelihood and impact, expressed in money, and spend on mitigation only where it pays. Adjust these delivery risks.

Timeline: by the end of inception, have a release strategy and a plan for initiation; by the end of initiation, the team must be able to build, test and deploy to a production-like environment. During development, review the deployment plan, hold mini-retrospectives after every showcase, and raise risks at the daily stand-up. On a team that is regularly delivering working software, a risk review can be light.

Compliance and auditing

Regulations such as Sarbanes-Oxley demand control: locked-down access to privileged environments, change management, management approvals, documented processes, separation between those who write and those who deploy, and audits of every deployment. The pipeline helps with all of them.

8

A change management process that doesn't slow you down

For organizations struggling with uncontrolled changes, the chapter's recipe: a Change Advisory Board with people from development, operations, security, change management and the business; a defined set of environments under its control; an automated change-request system; a back-out plan and acceptance criteria for every change; and an automated process for applying approved changes. Deciding is a matter of risk against benefit. Keep and publish metrics (how long approval takes, how many changes succeed) and hold regular retrospectives on the process itself. Route a change.
