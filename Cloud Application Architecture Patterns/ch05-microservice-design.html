Microservice Design

Where does one microservice end and the next begin? Domain-driven design answers by listening to how the business talks and drawing boundaries around meaning.

IDEALS: SOLID for services

Object-oriented design has SOLID. For service design the chapter adopts IDEALS, six principles that keep showing up in good microservice systems. Tap each letter.

2

What is the right size for a microservice?

"Small enough to rewrite in two weeks" and "fed by two pizzas" are folklore. The authors give two bounds rooted in domain-driven design. The lower bound is an Aggregate plus the services that operate on it, because splitting below that splits a transaction. The upper bound is a Bounded Context: a cohesive group of aggregates. A quick test for the upper bound: if this service failing would take down more than one business capability, it is too coarse or sits on too many critical paths. Slide the scope.

Err on the large sideIt is easier to split a coarse-grained Macro Service later than to merge two fine-grained services that turned out to belong together. A service with 10 or 20 operations on one set of entities may feel like a mini-monolith, but splitting it would be worse.
3

Root pattern: Model Around the Domain

ProblemHow can you get stakeholders to explain enough of the domain to reveal the capabilities your application needs?
SolutionBring developers and domain experts together to describe the subject area in the domain's own terms, and design from that.

Teams that start from their old system's design, or from a pile of frameworks, end up with services named after technology rather than anything the business recognizes. The symptom: few or no domain microservices. The first tool is a Ubiquitous Language: one shared vocabulary per context, used in conversation, in diagrams and in code. The catch is that the same word can rightly mean different things in different parts of the business. Pick a context.

Event Storming

ProblemHow do you get stakeholders to understand and describe the elements and events of the domain?
SolutionRun an Event Storming session to lay out the domain events that flow through the system.

Alberto Brandolini's workshop, from 2012, is deliberately low-tech: a long wall, lots of coloured sticky notes, people standing up. The team agrees a colour legend (commonly orange for events, blue for commands, yellow for aggregates) and works in four passes. Step through a session for the book's running example, Joe's Pizza.

5

Domain Event

ProblemHow do you model the things that happen in the scenarios the system encounters?
SolutionWhenever something significant changes, create a Domain Event recording that it happened, so the right parties can act.

A domain event is a fact, stated in the ubiquitous language and in the past tense: "Order Placed", "Pizza Packed". It is immutable, because the past does not change. Events crossing a bounded context are how contexts cooperate without calling into each other, which leads straight to Chapter 6. Is each of these a good domain event name?

Bounded Context and Context Map

ProblemHow do you define the edges of a domain or subdomain within which particular terms and rules apply?
SolutionGroup related events, aggregates and behaviour into a Bounded Context that acts as a cohesive whole for one purpose.

A bounded context usually matches a subdomain, though a subdomain may hold several. Its insides are reached only through its API, which the book calls an Application Service. Drawing the contexts and the relationships between them gives a Context Map: start from the storming output, group aggregates and events, label the links by the events that cross them, iterate, and name the team that owns each context. Aggregate and Repository

ProblemHow do you tie together groups of tightly related concepts and their values?
SolutionIdentify Aggregates: clusters of entities and value objects treated as one unit, reached only through a root entity.

In Joe's model an Order is the root, with Items, each tied to a Pizza entity (crust, size) that carries Topping values. Pizza is an entity because you could send back that particular pizza; a topping is a value, since you would never return just the pepperoni. Rules that span the cluster, such as "the total equals the sum of the items", are guarded by the root. A Repository loads and saves the whole aggregate at once. Try changing the order both ways.

8

Domain Service

ProblemHow do you model operations that do not belong to any single entity or aggregate?
SolutionDeclare them as a stand-alone Domain Service inside the bounded context.

Pricing Joe's order depends on the Order's items and on the Customer (regulars get a discount, delivery cost depends on the address). Putting that logic in Order would couple it to Customer. A PriceCalculator domain service, in effect a Strategy, reads both. Good domain services are named in the business language ("funds transfer", "adjudication", "price an order"). Login, logging and CRUD are not domain services, and domain services are never called directly from outside the context.

// illustrative, not from the book
class PriceCalculator {                      // domain service
  price(order, customer) {
    const items = order.items.reduce((s, i) => s + i.price, 0);
    const discount = customer.isRegular ? 0.10 * items : 0;
    return items - discount + deliveryFee(customer.address);
  }
}
9

Anti-Corruption Layer

ProblemHow can bounded contexts interact without becoming tightly coupled?
SolutionPut an Anti-Corruption Layer between them that adapts and translates each side's interface. Translation only, no business logic.

When Joe's Order context hands payment to an external payment provider, the provider's model (merchant references, amounts in minor units, its own status codes) must not leak into Joe's ubiquitous language. The ACL turns one into the other. A dispatcher (BFF) is a familiar kind of ACL.

10

From model to microservices

The rule of thumb: one microservice per bounded context. Inside it, expose each aggregate through the service API, implement domain services and publish domain events within it, and give each persistent aggregate a repository. The application service is the context's front door. Tap a piece of the model to see where it lands.

When business logic spans contexts, the contexts interact through events (choreography) or an orchestrating service, and an ACL sits wherever their models differ.
