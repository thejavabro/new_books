Escaping Monolithic Hell

A food delivery startup's once-tidy monolith has grown into a big ball of mud that nobody can change quickly. This chapter explains how that happens, what the microservice architecture offers instead, and why it is no silver bullet.

1

Meet FTGO, a tidy monolith

The book's running example is FTGO ("Food to Go"), an online food delivery company. Consumers order from local restaurants, FTGO dispatches couriers, and it pays restaurants and couriers. It started life as one Java application packaged as a single WAR file. That is the Monolithic architecture pattern: the whole application is a single deployable unit.

Inside, it is well organized. The architecture is hexagonal: business logic sits in the middle, and adapters around it connect to the outside world: the REST API and web UI on the inbound side, databases and cloud services (payments, messaging, email) on the outbound side. The slow march into monolithic hell

FTGO was successful. Every sprint added features, the team grew into several Scrum teams, and the once small codebase became a big ball of mud. Drag the slider through the years and watch the symptoms the book lists pile up.

The downward spiralWhen code is too big for anyone to understand, changes are made incorrectly. Each incorrect change makes the code a little harder to understand. Complexity feeds on itself, and the clean hexagon on paper stops matching reality.
3

The scale cube

The scale cube describes three independent ways to scale an application. The first two keep the monolith; the third is what microservices are made of. Pick an axis.

A working definitionThe microservice architecture functionally decomposes an application into a set of services. Size is not the point; each service has a focused, cohesive set of responsibilities. A service is a standalone, independently deployable component, and services are combined with X- and Z-axis scaling as needed.
4

Services as modules, each with its own database

Big applications must be modular, but inside one codebase module boundaries erode: a quick cross-package call here, a shared table there. Services make the boundary physical. A service can only be reached through its API, so its internals cannot be leaked into. And each service owns its data: no other service may touch its database. That keeps services loosely coupled at runtime and at design time.

A pattern language, not a silver bullet

Technology debates often turn into hype-driven "suck/rock" arguments. The book uses patterns instead: a reusable solution to a problem in a context, written so that the trade-offs are explicit. A pattern names the forces (competing concerns), the resulting context (benefits, drawbacks and new issues it creates) and related patterns (predecessors, successors, alternatives, generalizations). Related patterns form a pattern language.

7

Beyond architecture: process, organization, people

Architecture alone will not speed up delivery. The book pairs microservices with DevOps (continuous delivery and deployment, automated testing) and small, autonomous, cross-functional teams. Conway's law says a system's structure mirrors the organization that builds it; the reverse Conway maneuver designs the organization so its structure mirrors the services you want.

The change also hits people. The book borrows William and Susan Bridges' transition model for how people react emotionally to change. Step through it.
