Shortcuts that Undercut

Evolution wired us with mental shortcuts to survive information overload. They are usually helpful and often misleading, especially when we judge chance.

Case file: a note in class

What happens in the story
  • John Scott presents on how we misjudge chance. Raj applies it to Cicor: about 50 deaths among more than a million patients, a probability of 0.00005. Clara answers that it isn't small if it's your family.
  • Phil slips in late and passes notes: the informal exam found petechiae in Laura's eyes, tiny burst blood vessels. She was probably suffocated, likely with a pillow.
  • Larry crumples the notes into a ball and tosses it in the classroom trash as he and Phil slip out. Shawn has now ordered a full autopsy.
  • Larry notes the irony of the murder-vs-suicide statistic: a killer who knows suicides are more common might fake one.

What's easy to recall feels likely

Recap on one page

ShortcutsBuilt-in rules of thumb that save effort and distort probability judgments.
Gambler's fallacyBelieving independent random events will "correct" themselves.
Random streaksRuns and patterns appear in pure chance far more often than we expect.
Availability biasIf we can easily recall examples, we think the event is likely.
Base ratesStart with how common something is overall, then adjust for the specifics.
A calm routineDefine the goal, get informed, develop alternatives, deliberate, then track feedback.
