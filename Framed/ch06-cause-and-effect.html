Cause and Effect Is Hard to Detect

We see causes everywhere, but proving one is hard. Two things moving together can mean five different things, and even careful medical trials only narrow the doubt.

Case file: Laura

What happens in the story
  • Clara found Laura slumped over her desk, a syringe by her hand. A massive chemical cocktail with sodium pentothal. No sign of a struggle or break-in. Larry notices one oddity: a folded electric blanket placed next to her shoes in the closet.
  • Police chief detective Shawn Douglas leans toward suicide: Laura's Prozac prescription is almost untouched. Larry is consumed by "what ifs".
  • At Cook County Hospital, Raj and his brother Dr. Sunder Kumar teach cause and effect, FDA trials and placebo. Clara spots that Cicor's trial patients were healthier (and all male) compared with real patients.
  • Phil, being a contrarian, searched Laura's desk and found a cruise brochure: she visited a travel agent on Saturday afternoon. "Would you make travel plans on Saturday and kill yourself on Sunday night?"

Recap on one page

Five possibilitiesA→B, B→A, both ways, a hidden third factor, or pure chance.
Confounders confoundControl for the other factors before you claim a net effect.
TrialsTreatment vs. control (placebo) groups isolate the drug's effect, minus placebo.
PlaceboBelieving you're treated can improve symptoms; in some studies about 30% of a control group.
Sample biasIf trial patients are healthier than real patients, real risk is understated.
Confirmation biasWe hunt for evidence that fits our first belief. Be a contrarian.
