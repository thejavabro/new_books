Values and Facts

Many arguments that look like fights about facts are really fights about values. Separate the two, let experts settle the facts, and you can at least see where the real disagreement lives.

Case file: an email and a presentation

What happens in the story
  • On Laura's laptop Larry finds a message she sent Donald six days earlier: she resents him meddling in her life, partly over his low opinion of Larry. "Don't try to run my life."
  • Larry keeps an appointment with Professor Armstrong, who says he copes with grief by focusing on reducing other people's suffering. They discuss how to build consensus on divisive issues, and he invites Larry to an AIDS camp in Africa.
  • Phil gets Shawn's okay for an informal medical exam of Laura's body.
  • At GO, the frame teams present their Cicor recommendations: longer trials before higher doses, clearer warnings, separate trials for sicker patients, and a central side-effect database.

4

Signal vs. noise in warnings

Why did doctors ignore the FDA's Cicor warnings? Too many routine "red alerts" issued for liability. Switch to a tiered system where only real danger gets three stars, and see what a busy doctor notices.

5

When incentives backfire

A central side-effect database needs doctors to report. Should you pay them? Armstrong warns with a story: a bounty on dead rats, meant to fight plague, led people to breed rats. Raise the bounty.

For the database, Armstrong suggests modest incentives plus a real benefit (access to up-to-date shared records), with patient privacy protected and only aggregate data shared.

Recap on one page

Values color factsOur values act as a filter on what we see and believe.
Decompose the conflictLet experts settle facts as bias-free as possible; then the remaining gap is values.
Never fully separateIt is impossible to untangle them completely, but trying gives insight.
SignalingToo many shrill warnings get ignored. Fewer, graded, unambiguous signals work.
Perverse incentivesBig rewards can backfire: the rat bounty bred rats.
Weakest linkWhen the chain breaks anywhere, the company holding it takes the blame. Frame wide.
