What Means to What Ends?

In a bare cell, Martin Armstrong explains himself with the tools he taught: a cost-benefit ratio of three lives against millions. Larry has no good answer at first. The chapter's answer: means and ends are one frame, not two.

1

Case file: the cell

What happens in the story
  • Martin Armstrong is arrested at Fernando's. Larry, betrayed by the man he saw as a father, can barely function.
  • Angela gets a small reward, a lawyer and a likely suspended sentence. Phil earns a citation. Phil confesses he never truly suspected the professor either.
  • In his cell, Martin talks about two million AIDS deaths a year and what $15 billion could do. With all three heirs dead, the fund would be his to direct.
  • Larry pieces it together: the mugging that killed Diane was arranged; the hit-and-run on Donald was an attempt; Martin was Peeping Tom; he read their notes from the classroom trash. He knew Angela from a summer in Guadalajara. Maybe, he says, part of him wanted to be caught.
  • Leaving, Larry asks him to finish the line from Kipling's "If": the truth you've spoken, "twisted by knaves to make a trap for fools". And then: "No frame control!"

The twisted cost-benefit ratio

Martin's argument rests on one scale. Add the things his frame left out and watch the scale.

3

A slippery slope

Martin's own example: we choose not to repair a bridge, and a school bus falls. "Every day we weigh life and death." Step down his reasoning and see where it slides.

4

Three Cs for the road

Phil's lesson in the car: the decision-making process is a vehicle, not a destination. How you drive it is up to you.

C1CuriosityKeep asking disturbing questions, including about people you admire.
C2CautionCheck your assumptions and the reliability of the facts that anchor you.
C3Clear thinkingSeparate values from facts, and feelings from evidence.
