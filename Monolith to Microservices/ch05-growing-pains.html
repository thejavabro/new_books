Growing Pains

Microservices are a dial, not a switch. Each turn buys options and brings new problems. Here is what tends to hurt, roughly when, how to spot it and what helps.

1

Turning the dial

Adopting microservices is turning a dial, not flipping a switch. Each turn (more services) opens up more of the benefits and brings different pain. The book sketches roughly when each problem tends to bite; it admits this is unscientific and that tighter coupling brings problems earlier. Turn the dial; the thresholds below are this page's rough reading, not the book's numbers.

2

Ownership at scale

Martin Fowler's three kinds of code ownership map onto services. Strong: owners decide on outsiders' changes (e.g. via pull requests). Weak: anyone may change anything, but you talk to the owner first. Collective: nobody owns anything. One fintech grew from 30–40 to over 100 developers on "people know what is right" and ended with a tangled distributed monolith a developer called "colander architecture": everyone punched new holes.

3

Breaking changes

Every service has a contract with its consumers, whether written down or not: data shapes and behavior. Warning signs: outages right after a release, or teams coordinating lock-step deployments. The rules, half joking: 1. Don't break contracts. 2. See rule 1. More realistically: eliminate accidental breaks; think twice before a deliberate one; if you must, give consumers time to migrate. Is each change below breaking?

Breaking or safe?

Giving consumers time to migrate

Make contract changes visibleExplicit schemas catch structural breaks (tools like protolock even refuse incompatible protocol buffer changes); hand-written schemas make people pause. Semantic breaks (a method that now multiplies instead of adds) need tests. Prefer expansion: add, don't remove. Treat consumers like customers. Organizations that don't sort this out rarely live long enough to become large microservice organizations.
4

Reporting

Analysts used to join everything in the monolith's database (perhaps a read replica). Split the schema and their reports break, often discovered halfway through a migration because reporting sits out of sight. The fix: a dedicated reporting database, possibly matching the old schema, which services push data into. If you already have a warehouse or lake, just keep feeding it.

Monitoring and troubleshooting

"We replaced our monolith with microservices so that every outage could be more like a murder mystery." Is one process at 100% CPU worth waking someone at 3 a.m.? First step, before anything else: log aggregation. It's immediately useful and a test of whether your organization can make operational changes at all. Local developer experience, and running too many things

Too many services for one laptop

Four or five JVM services may fit on a laptop; twenty won't. Builds slow, people ask for bigger machines. Stub what you don't own, or point at remote instances (Telepresence proxies calls to a remote Kubernetes cluster). Teams practicing collective ownership feel this sooner, because they switch between services more.

Desired state management

Manual deployments and Chef or Puppet stop scaling. You want automation, self-service and a platform that keeps the right number of instances running. Kubernetes is the common choice (often a packaged form like OpenShift). On public cloud the author leans serverless-first: try functions-as-a-service before containers.

Don't reach for Kubernetes too earlyIt excels at managing many processes. Wait until your current approach strains. With five services you may be fine with what you have.

End-to-end testing

End-to-end tests give confidence but, across many services, they slow down and fail for reasons unrelated to your code: an instance died, a deployment half-finished. People rerun them "to see if they pass", add scenarios "just in case", and ask for a test team. See how the odds stack up:

Automated release remediation

Progressive delivery with guard rails: roll a canary out to more users only while its p95 latency and error rate stay under thresholds; roll back automatically otherwise (Netflix built Spinnaker partly for this). Set the guard rail and release.

8

Global versus local optimization

Invoicing picks Oracle (they know it), Notifications MongoDB (fits their model), Fulfillment PostgreSQL (already there). Each choice is sensible locally. Globally: three sets of skills and licenses for similar capabilities. REA found many different ways teams deployed services, a pain whenever people moved teams. Problems like this are often discovered by accident over lunch.

9

Robustness and resiliency

Rare failures (lost packets, time-outs, dead machines) become common as calls multiply, and they mostly appear in production. For every call, ask: do I know how this can fail? Do I know what to do if it does? Then: time-outs, circuit breakers, asynchronous communication, multiple instances, a platform that restarts crashed ones. Try a slow downstream service:

Resilience is more than patterns: record production incidents and what you learned, or the same problems return months later. See also Release It! by Michael Nygard.

Orphaned services

Independent deployability means some services run untouched for years. Then something needs changing and nobody knows what they do, who owns them, or even where the source code is. Several companies built simple registries (crawling repositories for metadata files, merged with data from service discovery like Consul).
