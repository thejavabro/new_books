Planning a Migration: The Journey

Once you know why, you need a way to move: bring people with you, take small reversible steps, choose the first services with a domain model, reshape teams and keep checking that it is working.

1

Taking people on the journey

"How do I sell microservices to my boss?" Usually the disagreement is about the goal, not the method. Get agreement on what you're trying to achieve and you're only arguing about how. For bigger changes the book borrows John Kotter's eight-step change model; even for a small team, the early steps help. Step through it.

2

Chip away at the marble

"If you do a big-bang rewrite, the only thing you're guaranteed of is a big bang." Martin Fowler's line sets the tone for the rest of the book. Extract a piece, get it into production, learn, repeat. Each mistake stays small, value arrives early, and you can stop at any point. Compare the two strategies over a two-year migration in which one serious mistake happens around month 9.

It's production that countsAn extraction isn't done until the new service is in production and being used. Troubleshooting, tracing, latency, referential integrity, cascading failures: most lessons only show up there.
3

Reversible and irreversible decisions

Jeff Bezos split decisions into one-way doors (Type 1) and two-way doors (Type 2). The book renames them Irreversible and Reversible, and treats them as ends of a spectrum: the question is how costly it would be to change your mind later. Microservices force many more decisions; most are reversible, and treating them all as life-or-death grinds progress to a halt. Place each decision.

Which end of the spectrum?

The cheapest place to make a mistake: the whiteboardMoving code is cheap; splitting a database, untangling an integration or rewriting an API with many consumers is not. Sketch the proposed services and run use cases across them (search for a record, register, buy an album). Circular calls or two very chatty services are signs the boundary is wrong.
4

Use a domain model to see what's easy

Each bounded context in a high-level domain model is a candidate service, and the relationships hint at difficulty. Lots of inbound dependencies means lots of call sites in the monolith to change. None means a strangler can simply intercept calls at the edge. Click a context in this sketch of Music Corp.

You need just enough model to choose a first step, not a complete one. Event Storming (Alberto Brandolini) is a good way to build it: technical and non-technical people list domain events, group them into aggregates, then into bounded contexts. The shared understanding is as valuable as the diagram. And remember the model is logical; the code may be more tangled than it suggests.

5

Combine ease with benefit

Easy isn't enough. Invoicing might be simple to extract, but if it rarely changes and your goal is time to market, it buys little. Rate each candidate on how easy it looks and how much it helps your goal; the top-right corner is where to start, with one or two services. Expect ratings to change as you learn.

6

Reorganizing teams, without copying Spotify

Siloed teams (analysts, developers, testers, DBAs, ops) mean hand-offs, just as layered architectures mean multi-layer changes. Many organizations now have teams owning more of the delivery cycle, with central teams shifting from doing to enabling. But don't copy another company's structure: "Copy the questions, not the answers." Even Spotify doesn't use "the Spotify model". The book's method: list every delivery activity, map who does it today, then draw the six-to-twelve month target.

DevOps doesn't mean NoOpsOrdering developers to "deploy and run 24/7 support" overnight alienates people. DevOps is about breaking down barriers and shared understanding, not eliminating operations specialists. Frame big shifts as aspirations, explain why, and build the path with your people.
7

Changing skills: private self-assessment

At The Guardian, each developer privately rated themselves from 1 ("means nothing to me") to 5 ("could write a book") on the skills the new platform needed, shared only with their mentor, and set their own targets. Anonymized together, the scores show where the whole team needs training or a new hire. Rate yourself; the team view blends your answers with five invented colleagues.
