Planning a Migration: Why (and Why Not)

Nobody wins by having microservices. This half of Chapter 2 asks what you are actually trying to achieve, what else could get you there, and when the whole idea is a poor fit.

1

Microservices are not the goal

You don't win by having microservices. Adopting them should be a conscious decision to achieve something you can't achieve with your current architecture. The book tells of a workshop where attendees didn't know why they were there: their boss had sent them, because the CTO had announced "we're doing microservices". Without a goal you can't prioritize, you can't measure, you risk a cargo cult ("it worked for Netflix"), and a year in, nobody remembers why the work started.

Question 1What are you hoping to achieve?Outcomes tied to what the business wants, described as benefits to users.
Question 2Have you considered alternatives?Often a more boring technique gets you most of the benefit. If you haven't looked, why not?
Question 3How will you know it's working?Define the measures and checkpoints before you start (covered on the next page).

Asking just these three has, more than once, been enough for a company to rethink going any further. And beware asking for "ROI of microservices": no return can be calculated until you say what return you want.

The book walks through the reasons people usually give. For each it also offers, honestly, ways to get the same benefit without microservices. Pick a reason.

3

Find the real bottleneck first

Want faster time to market? Map the whole path to production before blaming the architecture. At one investment bank, development to deployment took about six weeks, and automation might have shaved two. But ideas spent over 40 weeks waiting before a developer could even start. Choose where to invest.

4

Can more developers go faster?

Brooks's harvest example: adding people only helps when the work splits into pieces with few interactions. Clear service boundaries aim to make software more like a field of crops. Play with team count and how much the code forces them to coordinate.

A toy model, not data from the book: each pair of teams costs a slice of capacity in coordination. Low coupling is what lets the line keep rising. Microservices alone don't give you that; team alignment and how work is sliced matter too.

Robustness is not resilienceRobustness handles expected variation: a machine dies, so you load-balance two. Resilience is an organization able to adapt to what nobody anticipated. Spreading code across machines doesn't give you either for free; it can just add surface area for failure. Running several copies of the monolith across racks may get you much of the robustness, and if your uptime depends on humans never making mistakes, look at that first.

7

Rank your outcomes with sliders

Change programs pick up passengers: "if we're doing microservices, let's also go autonomous, and try Kotlin!" Soon everything is a priority and microservices are locked in as the answer, even when, say, horizontal scaling would have solved the real problem. The book's exercise: every outcome is a slider starting in the middle, and raising one means lowering another.
