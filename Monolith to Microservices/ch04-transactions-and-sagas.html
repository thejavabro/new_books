Decomposing the Database: Transactions and Sagas

Split the data and you lose the single transaction that kept it consistent. Distributed transactions mostly make things worse. Sagas model the business process explicitly and plan for failure.

1

What a database transaction gave you

Splitting data across databases costs you more than joins. Inside one database, an ACID transaction made a group of changes safe to reason about. The atomicity is usually the first thing teams miss.

AAtomicityAll the changes happen, or none do.
CConsistencyThe database is left in a valid state.
IIsolationConcurrent transactions don't see each other's half-done work.
DDurabilityOnce committed, it survives failures.
2

Try it: two databases, two transactions

Finishing a customer's enrollment means setting their status to VERIFIED and deleting their row from PendingEnrollments. In one database that is one transaction. After a split, each database still offers ACID, but only for its own half.

3

Two-phase commit, and why to avoid it

The usual first reach is a distributed transaction, typically a two-phase commit (2PC): a coordinator asks every worker to vote, then tells them all to commit. Step through it, with or without a misbehaving worker.

More participants, more ways to stall

Each participant here is 99.5% available (an assumption). Pat Helland's image: on a plane that needs every engine working, adding an engine reduces availability.

Distributed transactions: just say no2PC is really a protocol for coordinating distributed locks. Commits don't land at the same instant, so isolation is lost; some failures need manual unpicking; latency balloons with locks held. It suits only very short operations. If state truly must change atomically, keep it in one database and one service (or the monolith) and work on something else for now. Otherwise, use a saga.
4

Sagas: one business process, many local transactions

Hector Garcia-Molina and Kenneth Salem (1987) proposed sagas for long-lived transactions: break the work into a sequence of shorter transactions, each local and quick, so nothing stays locked for long. Across services, each step is one service's local ACID transaction. A saga does not give overall atomicity; it gives you enough information to know where you are and to recover, either backward (compensating transactions) or forward (retry). Run Music Corp's order fulfillment and break a step.

Compensation is a semantic rollbackThe original transactions did happen. You can't unsend an email; you send a second one saying the order was cancelled. Keep a record of the aborted order: it's valuable information.
Reorder to fail earlyMove steps most likely to fail earlier, and steps that are hard to undo later. Awarding points at dispatch means a packing failure never needs a points reversal.
5

Orchestrated or choreographed?

Which to use?The author is relaxed about orchestration when one team owns the whole saga: the coupling stays inside the team. With multiple teams he prefers choreography, which distributes responsibility. Mixing is fine (an orchestrated flow inside the Warehouse within a larger choreographed saga), as long as you can still see what happened.
About BPM toolsDrag-and-drop process tools promise non-developers will define flows; in practice developers end up using GUIs that are hard to version or test. Prefer code, and generate pictures from it. Developer-friendly options like Camunda and Zeebe exist.

Recap on one page

Atomicity goes firstTwo databases, two transactions. Plan for one succeeding and one failing.
Avoid 2PCDistributed locks, lost isolation, stalls that grow with participants.
Or don't splitKeep truly atomic state together; come back later.
SagasExplicit business process; local transactions; backward or forward recovery.
Design for failureSemantic compensations; reorder steps; mix fail-back and fail-forward.
Two stylesOrchestration: visible, more coupled. Choreography: decoupled, needs correlation IDs.
