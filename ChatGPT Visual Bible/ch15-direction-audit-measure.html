Choose a Direction, Audit Your Role, Measure the Gain

Decide what better work means for you, find the one task with most friction and least risk, and measure it before and after. Evidence, not opinion, is what the rest of Book 4 runs on.

1

What does better work mean to you?

Book 4 asks what all those skills are for. Two readers with identical skills can choose different paths and both be right. Tap your top two directions in order. The book's advice is to pick one primary direction: scattered effort across many tends to produce less than a focused one.

Watch outEarning independently means finding customers, delivering consistently and handling problems. Treat any promise of quick or easy income with suspicion, including one made by ChatGPT.

Saved time doesn't spend itself. Time freed by automation drifts into other routine work unless you decide where it goes. Write the decision down; you can change it later.

2

Audit your role: five buckets

For two or three days, list each task as you do it, including small ones like status updates and routine replies. Mark friction: retyping the same kind of message, searching for the same information twice, waiting on a step that could run without you. A project coordinator in the book spends 30 minutes writing a Friday update but an hour finding its numbers. That gap is friction. Then sort every task into one of five buckets.

Retain, assist, automate, delegate or stop?

3

Pick one improvement: most friction, least risk

Some tasks shouldn't be automated however often they repeat, because a mistake would be costly or hard to reverse: confidential information, financial commitments, legal obligations, decisions about someone's job or safety. Ask: "What happens if the result is wrong and nobody notices?" Click the best first candidate.

4

Measure before and after, honestly

A claim of improvement without evidence is an opinion. Record the baseline before changing anything, then measure the same way afterwards. Use four measures: time (start to finish), quality (how often it meets your stated standard), errors (how often it needs correcting later), and outcomes (the real-world result it was for). Fill in a scorecard.

Honest number, or invented precision?

5

Keep an achievement record

Don't trust memory to carry your evidence. Keep one document or spreadsheet; add an entry for every improvement, including ones that only partly worked (a record of only perfect results looks less credible); write enough detail to explain it a year from now. Draft an entry:

What to ask next"Turn this baseline and result into three sentences I could say out loud in a performance review." Then go back to your chosen direction and check the freed time is actually flowing there.
