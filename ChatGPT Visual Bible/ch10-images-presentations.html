Images, Diagrams and Presentations

Generated images convey a feeling; diagrams must be exact. Brief both carefully. Then build presentations the same way you build documents: audience and decision first, a story outline second, slides last.

1

Does the picture need to be exact, or only to feel right?

Image generation is built for illustrations, concept visuals, mockups and social posts, where small variations don't matter. A diagram showing a process or a comparison must be right in every box, label and arrow, and generation isn't built for that precision. Decide first which you need.

Generated image, or a precise diagram/chart?

The book cites OpenAI's August 2026 usage report: by mid-2026, 7.8% of all ChatGPT messages asked it to create, edit or find media, its fastest-growing use.

2

Write a visual brief before generating

A useful request names more than the subject: composition, mood, format, the exact text to include, the style, and what to avoid. The book first asks ChatGPT to turn an idea into a visual brief (and up to three questions) without generating anything. Its example is a social post for a fictional reading club. Change the brief and watch a rough layout sketch update.

Good practiceGenerate two or three versions of the same request before choosing. Small wording changes shift results more than you'd expect, and comparing shows which words are doing the work.
3

Edit by saying what changes, and what must not

You can upload an image and ask for a change in plain words: no layers or pixel selection. But ChatGPT may rebuild parts of the image while editing, so unrelated details drift. The fix is "change only" plus an explicit preservation list (face, pose, objects, lighting, every word of text, typography, positions, aspect ratio).

Check before you use itLook closely at hands, text, logos and small details; at who is represented (only one type of person in a role meant for everyone?); and never present a generated image as a photo of something real. Don't generate or edit an identifiable real person without permission, and don't recreate logos, trademarks or copyrighted art. Check your organisation's policy for published or commercial use.
4

Diagrams: plan the meaning, then pick a format

Start from what the diagram must teach, not its colours. A diagram brief lists the main point, the required items, their relationships, reading direction, exact labels, and what to leave out. The book's example: "AI assistance → Human review → Approval → Action", left to right, with those exact labels. Review that written plan before anything is drawn. Then choose a format.

5

Presentations that lead to a decision

A presentation should lead an audience to a decision, not just display information. The book's path: evidence → argument → deck, aimed at one decision. Define audience and decision first ("approve the recommended scheduling priorities", not "discuss next quarter"), ask for a presentation brief, then a slide-by-slide outline, and only then the slides. 6

Design rules and the final inspection

The colleague testAsk someone unfamiliar with the material what decision they think you're asking for. If their answer doesn't match yours, fix the narrative before the real meeting. The same material can also become a one-page visual report for people who won't attend; it must make sense without speaker notes.
Likely changed since publicationThe book generates decks as downloadable PowerPoint files and points to a "ChatGPT for PowerPoint" extension whose requirements change often.
