Learn Something New and Plan Real Choices

Use ChatGPT as a patient tutor that questions you rather than an answer machine, then point the same habits at real decisions: options first, explicit criteria, the case against your favourite, and a human expert when stakes rise.

1

Ask for an explanation at your level

A good teacher asks what you already know. ChatGPT cannot see your background unless you say it. The book's example is a traveller who can introduce themselves and order food in Spanish but keeps tripping over ser and estar. Three ingredients make the request work:

I can introduce myself and order food in Spanish. I still mix up ser and estar. Explain the difference in under 150 words, with two everyday examples of each, for someone with no grammar background.

If it's still unclear, say which part didn't land and ask for the same idea another way: shorter, a different example, fewer new words.

2

Break a subject into a learning path

One explanation covers one idea; a subject has parts that build on each other. Ask for a short ordered path, then question the order. Here is a possible path for "hold a simple conversation on a trip in three weeks". Put it in a sensible order, then check.

Ask why one topic comes before another, ask to shorten the path if you have less time, and ask which topics you can skip for your specific goal.

3

Let it question you, not tell you

Reading an explanation is not learning it; answering questions is what makes it stick. Ask for quiz questions without answers, answer them yourself, then ask for marking. (If an answer leaks into a later question, ask for a fresh set.) Try the kind of quiz the book describes.

ser or estar?

The harder test: explain it backWrite the rule in your own words and ask ChatGPT to point out gaps or mistakes, rather than comparing you to its own version. A day or two later, explain it again without looking. If you can't, that topic needs another round.
Analogies break somewhereComparisons make ideas memorable while hiding differences. After any analogy, ask: "Where does this comparison break down? Give one case where it fails." And check phrases you'll use in person with a native speaker, textbook or course: confident is not the same as correct, and dialects differ.
4

Schoolwork: support, not substitution

Read the AI policy for the specific course and assignment, not just the school's general one; they increasingly differ. If nothing is written, ask before you start and keep a record that you asked. Then sort these uses.

Learning support or substitution?

Watch outSubmitting text ChatGPT wrote as your own is misconduct at most schools whether or not a detector flags it, and detectors are unreliable. "Will it get caught?" is the wrong question. "Can I honestly defend this as my own work?" is the right one. When disclosure is required, be specific: "I used ChatGPT to build an outline and check grammar", not "I used AI".
5

Helping a child with homework

After a long day it's tempting to paste the worksheet in and copy the answer back. That fills the page and teaches nothing; the next test has no ChatGPT beside it. Stay in the room, decide what your child sees, and narrate what you are doing ("I'm asking it to quiz you instead of giving the answer"). Children copy how adults use a tool.

The hardest moment to catchReaching for ChatGPT because the deadline is close, not because your child is stuck. That is the moment to slow down.
Likely changed since publicationThe book points to OpenAI's Study Mode FAQ for a tutoring-style mode. Whether that mode still exists under that name, and how it is switched on, is a product detail to check in the current help centre.
6

Plan a real decision: options first, criteria second

Chapter 1.7 applies the same habits to a decision you actually face. The order matters: describe circumstances, goals and constraints; ask what else would help; ask for several options without a recommendation (so you don't anchor on the first idea); then compare against criteria you choose. The book's example: a five-day trip for two adults and a six-year-old who hates long car journeys, about US$1,500.
