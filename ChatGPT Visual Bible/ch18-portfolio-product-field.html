Case Studies, Offers, Your Field and a 30-Day Project

A one-page case study proves a result. A narrow offer with clear scope turns it into income. Evidence about your own field replaces headline anxiety. And a 30-day project measured against a baseline makes it real.

1

A one-page case study with proof

A case study is a short, honest account of a real problem you improved, with evidence. Choose a project from your achievement record with a clear before and after, and that you have the right to share. Structure: the original problem (understandable in a sentence or two), what you did including the safeguards (verification, review points, acceptance criteria), the result as before and after side by side, and the evidence someone else can check. Edit and watch the page.

Watch outA case study showing only the polished result raises doubts about how the work was done. Naming your checks makes it more credible, not less impressive. Write for a stranger with thirty seconds: headline claim first, then evidence.
2

Remove confidential detail before sharing

Before sharing outside your organisation, remove client names, internal figures and proprietary details unless you have explicit permission; switch exact numbers to proportions; ask a colleague if unsure; keep the unredacted version privately. Click each sensitive item to generalise it.

3

Turn a problem into a service or product

Start from someone else's real problem, not a skill you want to sell. Choose a narrow group you understand. Validate the need with real people outside your own head (it's easy to think a problem is common because it annoys you). Research how they solve it now: competitors, DIY, or living with it. Then pin down the offer.

4

Service or product, price, and the unglamorous basics

A service starts faster but scales with your hours; a product takes longer to build but serves many customers without your time growing in step. Many people start with a service and productise the proven, repeated version. Price from real cost and market research, not what feels comfortable (which tends to undercharge). Decide price and scope before your first sales conversation.

Watch outTax and business rules vary by country, state and income, and mistakes can cost more than you earn. Confirm with a qualified accountant or tax adviser, not general information (including ChatGPT's). The book's optional online chapter 4.14 on finding customers is not in the printed volume.
5

Will AI take my job? Two different questions

The book splits the worry in two. Can AI do some tasks in my job? For most jobs, yes. Can it do the whole job, including judgment, relationships and context? That depends heavily on the role, and changes as tools change. Research your own role and industry (not "AI and jobs"), note where sources disagree, check one yourself, and write a paragraph on what you found and how sure you are. Distrust any source, ChatGPT included, that gives one confident percentage or year.

The book's argument for calm: Jevons paradox

When something gets cheaper, people may use so much more of it that total demand rises (more efficient steam engines raised total coal use). Applied to "thinking work": drag the cost down and choose how strongly demand responds. Illustrative model only.

Time-sensitive claimsThe book's labour-market points (rising white-collar employment alongside AI adoption, radiologists earning more a decade after a famous 2016 prediction, a 2026 Fortune interview on Jevons paradox, the World Economic Forum's Future of Jobs survey) are snapshots. The author asks you to revisit your own research every few months; economists disagree about how long these effects last.
6

Book 4 project: a 30-day career value project

Choose the track that matches your direction from chapter 4.1, set a baseline this week, block time on your calendar for all four weeks, and fix the scope before you start. Thirty days is enough for real progress, not for perfection.

Finish with a dated next stepSummarise what you set out to do and what happened, whether you met the goal, the single biggest lesson, and your next concrete step with a date. Share it with at least one person: explaining it aloud clarifies what you learned.
