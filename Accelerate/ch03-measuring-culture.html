Measuring and Changing Culture

Culture sounds too fuzzy to measure. It isn't, if you pick a good model. Westrum's three culture types predict how information flows, and how information flows predicts performance.

1

Three levels of organizational culture

"Culture" can mean a country, a company's values or the unwritten rules of a team. Edgar Schein describes organizational culture at three levels, from the visible tip to the hidden base. The research zooms in on the middle one. Tap a level.

2

Westrum's three culture types

Sociologist Ron Westrum studied safety in high-risk, complex fields like aviation and healthcare. He sorted organizations into three types by what they reward, and found the type predicts how information flows. Click a column header to focus it.

Bureaucracy isn't the villainRules exist to make treatment fair and to encode hard-won knowledge. Westrum's "bureaucratic" means following the rules matters more than the mission. The authors have seen generative government teams and pathological startups.
3

What good information looks like

Westrum's key insight is that culture shapes information flow, and in high-tempo, high-consequence work (which includes running software), information flow is safety and performance. Good information has three properties:

Generative culture helps information move in three ways: people trust and cooperate across the organization and up and down it; the shared mission outranks personal and departmental politics; and hierarchy plays a smaller role, a more level playing field.

4

Measure your team's culture

Because Westrum's types sit on a continuum, they suit agreement-scale questions. The survey asks seven statements about your team (teams answer more accurately than whole organizations, and big organizations hold pockets of different cultures). Score each from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree); the average is your score. This construct has repeatedly tested valid and reliable, so you can use it too.

5

From answers to a construct

Before testing whether culture predicts anything, the researchers check the measure itself. Three tests (detailed in chapter 13) must pass: items that should relate do (convergent validity), items that shouldn't, don't (discriminant validity), and people read the items the same way (reliability). Then the items become a construct they can use in further analysis.

6

What culture predicts, and what drives it

The authors hypothesized that a generative culture would predict delivery performance, organizational performance and job satisfaction. All three held. And culture isn't fixed: practices change it. Click any box.

Google found the same thingGoogle's two-year study of 180+ teams expected the secret to be who was on the team. It wasn't: how members interact, structure their work and see their contribution mattered far more. Team dynamics win.
7

How each culture handles an outage

Complex systems fail through an interplay of factors, not one careless person. So "human error" should be where an investigation starts, not where it ends. Step through the same incident handled two ways.

8

Act your way to a better culture

John Shook helped transform the car plant in Fremont, California, where Lean manufacturing took root in the US. His lesson: don't start by changing how people think; change what they do, and the thinking follows. The research confirms it for software: Lean management and continuous delivery practices measurably improve culture.
