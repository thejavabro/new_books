When the Agile Manifesto appeared, Extreme Programming came with hard technical practices like test-driven development and continuous integration. As Scrum overtook it, many Agile adoptions sidelined those practices. The research says that was a mistake.

Continuous delivery (CD)A set of capabilities that let us get changes of every kind (features, configuration, bug fixes, experiments) into production or into users' hands safely, quickly and sustainably. Releasing becomes a routine, on-demand event.

From 2014 to 2016 the team measured CD's ingredients; in 2017 they measured CD itself (deploy on demand at any point; fast feedback on quality that everyone acts on first) and confirmed the ingredients predict it. Two newer ingredients joined: loosely coupled architecture and letting teams choose their tools. Click any box.

Quality is "value to some person", which is hard to measure. Beyond change fail rate, the researchers tried three proxies: perceived app quality, time on rework or unplanned work, and time fixing defects users found. All correlated with delivery performance, but unplanned work and rework correlated most strongly, and CD predicts less of it.

Keeping things in version control predicts performance. The surprise was which things: system and application configuration were more strongly linked to delivery performance than application code. Configuration is not a second-class citizen. Toggle what your team versions.

Who owns the acceptance tests?

High performers had fewer than three active branches, branches that lived less than a day, and no code freezes. This held regardless of team or company size. Drag the sliders and watch merge pain build up.

What about GitHub Flow?Short-lived branches merged at least daily are consistent with CI and do fine. Long-lived branches discourage refactoring and team communication. Longer branches do make sense in open source, where contributors are part-time.
