Software has invisible inventory, and design and delivery happen at the same time, so measuring it is hard. Most past attempts share two flaws: they measure output instead of outcomes, and individual or local results instead of team or global ones. Three classic examples:

Utilization vs. lead time

queueing theory, simplified

Lean thinking prizes lead time: from a customer asking to the request being met. In product work it has two parts that behave very differently. The book measures only the second.

Design and development
  • New products and features solving customer problems: hypotheses, UX, design thinking.
  • Often work never done before.
  • Estimates very uncertain; outcomes very variable.
  • When does the clock even start? The "fuzzy front end".
Delivery: build, test, deploy
  • Fast flow from development to production, reliable releases.
  • Integration, test and deployment done continuously.
  • Cycle times should be known and predictable.
  • Low variability: easy to measure. This is the one the survey asks about.

A good measure focuses on a global outcome (so dev and ops aren't rewarded for opposite things, the classic "wall of confusion") and on outcomes, not busywork. The authors settled on four. Two measure tempo, two measure stability. Answer them for your own service to see which 2017 cluster you sit closest to.

The researchers didn't define "high performer" up front. They gave the four answers to a cluster analysis, which groups responses so each group is as alike as possible inside and as different as possible from the others. The algorithm has no idea which answers are "good". Every year, three clearly separate groups came out.

The 2016 surpriseIn 2016 medium performers had a worse change fail rate than low performers. One explanation: mid-transformation teams were doing big re-architecture and new work, neglecting rework, piling up technical debt and fragile systems. It is an early sign of the "J-curve": things can get worse before they get better (more in Appendix B).

Strategic or commodity? Build or buy?

Because delivery capability is a competitive advantage, don't outsource the software that differentiates you. Commodity software (office tools, payroll) is usually better bought as a service.

In a learning culture the four metrics are a superb tool: benchmark, set targets, track progress. In a pathological or bureaucratic culture, measurement becomes control, and people hide anything that threatens their position. As Deming put it, where there is fear you get the wrong numbers.

So culture comes first. That is the next chapter.
