CD and DevOps were born in web companies. Can they work for mainframes, firmware, packaged software or a "big ball of mud" of thousands of tightly coupled systems? The team checked eleven kinds of system against performance. Guess which ones made a difference.

Linked to lower performance?

So focus on characteristics, not technologyHigh performance is possible with packaged software and mainframes. And the latest microservices on containers guarantee nothing if the system can't be tested and deployed independently. The outsourcing finding is another reason to bring strategic software in-house.

Two statements separated high performers: "We can do most of our testing without an integrated environment" and "We can and do deploy our application independently of the services it depends on." In 2017 the team tested a fuller set of six. Together they were the biggest contributor to continuous delivery, bigger even than test and deployment automation. How many can your team claim?

Melvin Conway observed in 1968 that systems end up mirroring the communication structure of the organization that builds them. The research supports the inverse Conway maneuver: shape your teams to get the architecture you want. Teams should be cross-functional, able to design, build, test, deploy and run their part without high-bandwidth chatter with others.

Orthodoxy says adding developers raises total output but lowers each person's output, thanks to communication overhead. The data on deploys per developer per day (for teams deploying at least daily) told a different story for high performers. Slide the team size.

Shape of the finding, not the exact data: low performers' frequency falls, medium stays flat, high rises significantly.

Approved-tool lists exist for good reasons. But they stop teams picking what suits their problem and experimenting with new approaches. The analysis found that when teams choose their own tools, it contributes to delivery performance and, through it, organizational performance. Weigh the arguments.

No contradictionThere is still a place for standards, especially a common operational platform and a debuggable way to run every service. The trick: build internal tools and preapproved security libraries so good that engineers choose them. Treat internal users like customers, and let their choice keep you honest.

Microservices or serverless? Kubernetes or Mesos? Which CI server? The research says these are the wrong questions. Tools are irrelevant if the people using them hate them or if they don't let teams change their products without depending on others.
