Agile has more or less won the methodology wars. Yet much of what's practised is "faux Agile": sprints and stand-ups wrapped in months of up-front budgeting and requirements, big infrequent releases, and customer feedback as an afterthought. Lean product development and the Lean Startup (Eric Ries, building on Lean, design thinking and Steve Blank) test the product and the business model with users from the very start.

The model has four parts. Together they predicted better delivery performance and organizational performance, a more generative culture and less burnout. Click to explore.

Imagine a feature made of 12 parts. Unknown to the team, users only want 7 of them. Building it in one big batch, you learn that after everything ships. In weekly slices with feedback, you find out as you go and stop building what nobody wants. Run both.

That's the MVP idea too: a version with just enough features for validated learning about the product and its business model. Slices should be completable in less than a week and released frequently. Working this way relies on CD: an experimental approach to product development is highly correlated with CD's technical practices.

In 2016 the model showed Lean product management driving delivery performance. In 2017 the team flipped the arrow and found delivery performance also drives Lean product management. Put together: a reinforcing loop. Better delivery makes small batches and user research easier; that makes better products, which pays off in organizational performance.

Many "Agile" teams must build requirements written by someone else. If they can't change the spec when they learn something without outside approval, innovation stalls. The ability to try ideas and update specifications during development predicts profitability, productivity and market share. But it isn't "do whatever you like". Combine it with the other three and see.
