The Science Behind This Book

How can survey research claim one thing "drives" another? This chapter lays out the ladder of analysis types and where the book's claims sit on it.

1

Primary or secondary? Qualitative or quantitative?

How do we tell actions that merely coincide with change from actions that drive it? Through rigorous primary research. Two distinctions first: who collected the data (you, or someone else) and what form it takes (numbers, or not). Tap a quadrant.

Surveys can be quantitativePeople often assume survey research is qualitative. It depends on the questions. A Likert-type item records a number: 1 for "strongly disagree", 4 for neutral, 7 for "strongly agree". That consistency makes statistical analysis possible.
2

Six types of analysis, from easy to hard

Jeffrey Leek describes six types of data analysis, ordered by the knowledge, cost and time they demand. The book's claims sit on the first three rungs, plus classification, which doesn't fit neatly on the ladder. Climb it.

3

Population and sample

The population is everyone you care about: say, every engineer in a technology transformation. Usually you can't ask them all, so you study a carefully defined and selected sample. Good sampling is what lets conclusions about the sample hold for the population.

4

Correlation, and why it isn't enough

Correlation measures how closely two variables move together. The book reports Pearson's r: +1 means they move in perfect lockstep, −1 in perfect opposition, 0 not at all. Drag r and watch the cloud.

Correlation can come from a hidden third variable, or from pure chance. Tyler Vigen's "Spurious Correlations" famously shows US cheese consumption tracking deaths by bedsheet entanglement with r ≈ 0.95. Go fishing yourself: generate 200 random, meaningless yearly series and find the best match.

5

Inferential prediction: theory first

To avoid fishing, the hypotheses in this research are driven by theory and prior evidence before any data is collected. That's the scientific method. Whenever the book says one thing "drives" or "impacts" another, this is the analysis behind it, using multiple linear regression or partial least squares regression. It's the standard approach where randomized experiments aren't possible: businesses won't sacrifice profit to be a control group.

6

Classification: how the performance groups were found

The high, medium and low clusters came from hierarchical clustering on the four delivery metrics. It starts with every response as its own group and repeatedly merges the two closest groups, building a tree. Step through a toy version.
