Deployment pain: where dev meets ops

The fear and anxiety engineers feel when pushing code to production tells you a lot. It marks the friction between how software is built and tested and how it's run, where environments, processes, mindsets and even vocabularies differ most. Where deployments hurt most, delivery performance, organizational performance and culture are all poorest. Rate your own.

2

Why deployments hurt, and the cure

Most deployment problems come from a complex, brittle deployment process, which usually has three roots. Pick one.

Deploying after hours is a smellIf deployments must happen outside business hours, that signals architectural problems. With enough investment, even large distributed systems can deploy fully automatically with zero downtime. Platform-as-a-service apps often deploy with a single command. Watch post-deployment pain too: systems that page on-call staff all night are unhealthy.
3

Case: continuous delivery at Microsoft

Before Microsoft's Bing team adopted continuous delivery practices, engineers' satisfaction with work/life balance was 38%. Afterwards it reached 75%. No more manual deployment rituals; work stress stayed at work.

4

Burnout: fix the environment, not the person

Burnout is physical, mental or emotional exhaustion from overwork or stress, but more than that: it can make what we loved about work feel pointless. Signs include exhaustion, cynicism, feeling ineffective, little sense of accomplishment, and work spilling into the rest of life. Stressful jobs can harm health as much as second-hand smoke, and job stress costs the US economy about $300 billion a year.

Christina Maslach found six organizational risk factors that predict burnout. Every one is something management can change. Tap each.

6

Values alignment and what managers can do

When personal and organizational values clash, burnout is more likely. Alignment protects. And what counts are the lived values people experience every day, not the mission statement on the wall.
