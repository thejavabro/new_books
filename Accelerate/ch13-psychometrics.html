Why people distrust surveys

The two questions the authors hear most: why use surveys at all (next chapter), and can survey data be trusted? Skepticism is fair. Many people's only exposure is to push polls, surveys with an obvious agenda that nudge you toward an answer or slip you "information" disguised as a question. Political campaigns are a common source. And many well-meaning surveys are written by people with no training. Spot the flaw in each item below.

What's wrong with this survey item?

Single-question "quick surveys"One question can be fine if it's well written and well understood, but draw only narrow conclusions. Net Promoter Score is a good example: carefully developed and studied, and a standard you can compare across teams and companies.
2

Measuring what you can't measure directly

You can read a room's temperature or a site's response time directly. You can't take a culture's temperature. A latent construct measures something hidden through several observable pieces, the manifest variables, each captured by a survey item. Like a Venn diagram, each item covers one facet of the idea. Toggle items to see how much of "culture" you capture.

Note the items are about my team, a deliberate change from Westrum's organization-level framing: big organizations contain pockets of different cultures, and people answer more accurately about their own team. (Strictly, survey "questions" are statements, so researchers call them items.)

3

Benefit 1: it forces you to define the thing

Before measuring, decide what you mean. Take "Is your culture good?" Click each word to see what it hides.

The book's definition of the culture that matters: high trust, good information flow, bridges across teams, welcomed novelty, shared risk. That is why Westrum's model fit.

4

Benefit 2: tests that the items hang together

With several items per construct you can run statistical tests that a single question never allows. Think of each respondent's answer as a dart, and the construct as the bullseye.

5

When the tests surprise you

The team wrote five items about how teams learn of failures. In a pilot with about 20 professionals, they loaded together as one construct. On the full dataset, the tests said otherwise. Run the analysis.

6

Benefit 3: one bad source can't fool you

Constructs that pass the tests and are re-checked periodically guard against bad data. The same idea works for system data. All measures are proxies; if you watch one number and its instrumentation breaks, you won't know. Watch several related numbers and the odd one out stands out. Break a metric.
