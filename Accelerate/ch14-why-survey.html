Why Use a Survey

Engineers tend to trust logs and distrust surveys. The authors argue both can be wrong, and that some things can only be learned by asking people.

1

Five reasons to use a survey

Many teams start measuring delivery by instrumenting their toolchain, and several tools report lead time out of the box. So why survey people? Open each reason.

2

Reason 1: speed and consistent definitions

Each year the team collected responses for four to six weeks from thousands of people in thousands of organizations worldwide, then analysed for three to four weeks. Imagine getting system data from the same teams instead.

Even with the data, what does it mean? Different teams use the same name for different things. Toggle the two teams.

3

Reason 2: systems don't see everything

One of the authors once worked as a performance engineer on enterprise disk storage. Step through what happened.

4

Reason 3: a system only knows what's inside it

Is everything needed for production in version control? A system can count files that are versioned, but it can't count the ones that aren't, so it can't give you a percentage. The people who do the work can. Toggle the view.

5

Reason 4: you can trust survey data

The authors run this exercise with audiences. Answer each question in turn.

Now compare bad actors. In a system, one person with access, or one bug that slips past review, corrupts the single number the business watches, possibly for months. In a survey of 23,000 people, liars would have to coordinate by the hundreds and skew every item of a construct the same way. Anonymity helps people answer honestly in the first place.

Bad system data is realThe plot of the cult film Office Space turns on a "rounding error" slipped into financial software that siphons money and never shows up in the reports. Humans build systems, and humans make mistakes, or worse.
6

Reason 5: some things only people can tell you

Perceptions, feelings and opinions can only be measured by asking. Teams often reach for an HR proxy for culture, usually staff turnover. But turnover happens for many reasons that have nothing to do with culture. Tap each.

You can watch network traffic to see who talks to whom, or run sentiment analysis on chat. But to know how people feel about taking risks and sharing information, and why they act as they do, you have to ask them, systematically and repeatably. Ask with improvement in mind: "beatings will continue until morale improves" gets you good-looking, meaningless answers.

Quick check

Survey, system data, or both?

8

Recap on one page

FastWeeks, not years, across thousands of organizations, with one shared set of definitions.
Full pictureSystems miss what's outside their boundaries, like the interface in the storage story.
CompletePeople can report what's not in the system: the denominator.
TrustworthyConstructs, statistical checks, anonymity and big samples resist bad actors better than one log.
UniqueFeelings and perceptions, like culture, can only be asked about.
Use bothPeriodic surveys of your technologists alongside system data reveal bottlenecks.
