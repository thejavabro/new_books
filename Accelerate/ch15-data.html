1

Who was the population?

Before you can sample, you define the population: the whole group you want to say something about. The research wanted to understand software delivery in teams working the DevOps way, so the target was practitioners and leaders familiar with DevOps: people already doing it, or on their way. Drag the slider to see the trade-off of widening the net.

The deliberate trade-offTeams that have never heard of DevOps are probably performing even worse than the study's low performers, so they're missing from the picture. In exchange, the people who answer understand the vocabulary and practices, which gives far more explanatory power about what works.
2

Ask about practices, not labels

"Do you do continuous integration?" invites people to say yes to a buzzword. The survey asked instead about the concrete practices behind it. Toggle each pair.

This also widens who can answer usefully: someone who doesn't call it "DevOps" but does the practices still gives valid data.

3

Probability vs. nonprobability sampling

In probability sampling every member of the population has a known chance of being picked, typically at random. That needs a list of the whole population. Nobody has a list of everyone who does DevOps, so the study used a nonprobability method instead.

4

How the snowball rolled

The study used referral (snowball) sampling: an initial set of people is invited, and each passes the invitation on to others who fit. It works well for populations that are hard to list but tightly networked, like DevOps practitioners. Step through how invitations spread.

SeedsMailing lists, online promotions, social media, and asked people to share.
SpreadEach respondent forwarded the link to peers who do the same kind of work.
Result23,000+ responses over four years from 2,000+ organizations worldwide.

6

A snapshot, not a time series

The study is cross-sectional: each year it surveys a fresh sample at one point in time, rather than following the same teams over years (longitudinal). Toggle the two designs.
