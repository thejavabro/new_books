1

A bank that wanted to learn faster

This guest chapter, by Lean practitioners Steve Bell and Karen Whitley Bell, follows ING Netherlands, a global bank, as its IT leaders tried to build a learning organization. The bank had already reorganized into Agile teams. The authors argue the structure was only the start: what made it work was how leaders behaved every day.

The core ideaHigh performance isn't installed. It's grown, one behaviour at a time, by leaders who change how they themselves work.

4

Stand-ups and catchball

Every team, from squads up to the leadership team, holds short stand-ups (around 15 minutes) at their own boards, in a rhythm. Problems a team can't solve are escalated up the chain the same day, and answers come back down. Goals pass both ways too: that back-and-forth is called catchball. Run it.

5

Strategy deployment: Hoshin Kanri and PDCA

Hoshin Kanri (strategy deployment) turns a few big objectives into aligned goals at every level, agreed through catchball rather than dictated. Each level then runs experiments with Plan-Do-Check-Act. Step through one turn of the loop.

6

What leaders do all day

In this model every leader has three jobs, and all three happen every day. Tap each.

Two phrases capture the attitude: "If the quality isn't there, don't release", and "slow down to speed up". Taking time to fix problems properly is how teams get faster.

7

Where does a manager's week go?

The leadership team wrote down how they actually spent their time, then looked for waste. By cutting low-value activity they freed about 10% of their week for coaching, improvement and going to see the work. Try it: remove waste to make room.

Illustrative split, not data from the chapter.

8

Process vs. practice

A process is a documented procedure. A practice is what people actually do, skilfully and repeatably. Lean's standard work is a practice: the current best known way, written down so it can be improved. Toggle.

9

A problem-solving loop everyone uses

Problems are treated as chances to learn. Everyone, from squad member to tribe lead, uses the same simple loop, often on a single sheet of paper. Click through it.
