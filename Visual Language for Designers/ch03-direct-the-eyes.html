Direct the Eyes

Viewers give a graphic a few seconds and look only where something calls them. Composition and cues decide which places those are, and in what order.

1

Attention is a spotlight, not a floodlight

The optic nerve carries more each second than the brain can fit into awareness, so we shift attention from place to place, one at a time. Our eyes skip empty or monotonous areas and fixate on whatever looks informative. Left alone, viewers may dwell on the wrong thing or drown in too much. And people often spend only seconds on a graphic, far less than on a text or a film. Directing the eye makes sure those seconds land on what matters.

There are common biases. Scanning often enters at the upper left; movements run mostly left to right and top to bottom; diagonals are rarer. After the first few fixations viewers have the gist, and then content and their own goals take over. Play a typical first scan of a page with no strong focal point.

A balanced layout with nothing dominant.

Two families of technique change that path. Compositional ones are built into the picture: position, emphasis, movement. Signaling ones are laid on top: arrows, color highlights, numbers, captions. Cues don't carry the message; they orient, point and highlight. Both work. In one study, viewers' eye paths over an artwork closely matched where the artist said he meant them to look, and highlighting or arrows improve retention and cut search time.

2

Selective attention is the gate

Whatever lands in sensory memory must be selected by attention to reach working memory. Eye movements usually track attention, but not always: you can notice something in the corner of your eye while looking at a speaker, and attention moves before the eyes. Directing the eye helps keep the two aligned. You can capture attention bottom-up (contrast, motion) or top-down (numbered steps, captions that set a goal).

3

Build a hierarchy of focal points

A focal point is the magnetic spot the eye goes to first; without one, a design can seem to fall apart. Several focal points of different weight create a ranking that leads the eye from most to least important. Give each element a size and a contrast and watch the predicted viewing order. Try to get image → headline → text → logo, then try making everything big.

The prediction is a toy: weight comes from size, contrast and a bonus for being near the top left. Real eyes also follow content and goals. The point is the ranking: primary, secondary, then equivalent elements, with no ties at the top.

4

Position: where in the frame

The frame, whether postcard, poster or screen, gives meaning to what it holds. An element's position creates a perceptual tension that changes its importance. We speak of important people being "at the top", and we expect the same of pictures: objects in the upper half are judged more active and potent, and in one study viewers spent longer on the left and upper areas than the right and lower ones. Move the dot.

5

Emphasis through contrast

Without emphasis a design is flat; with it, it has energy and a dominant-subordinate order. Contrast, an abrupt change, is the engine. Contrast can come from size, tone, color, texture or shape; Arnheim thought size matters most for visual weight, others argue for tone. Two other routes: isolation, and incongruence, an unexpected object in a familiar context, which grabs attention because it breaks our schemas. Whatever you contrast, viewers will read the difference as meaningful.

Contrast at several levelsThe primary focal point needs a vivid, abrupt contrast. Secondary and tertiary elements should be progressively toned down, so nothing competes for first place.
6

Movement: lines that carry the eye

A static picture can still move. We perceive direction and energy in lines, shapes and textures because we know motion from our own bodies; action images even activate brain areas that respond to real motion. Arnheim named three sources of visual direction: the pull of surrounding weights, the axes of shapes, and the direction of the subject's action. Pick a path and watch where it takes the eye.

A cross-cultural survey of Chinese, Japanese, Indian, Persian and Western art found important elements tended to sit left of less important ones, so the eye flows rightward. The left-to-right preference may be neurological, not just a reading habit.

7

Eye gaze: we look where they look

The brain has specialized machinery for faces, and even for eyes alone, whether in a photo, a painting or a smiley. We also shift attention, almost automatically, to where someone else is looking. Infants do it at three months. This joint attention works on pictures too. Turn the face.

8

Visual cues: arrows, color and numbers

Every fixation costs time, and complexity multiplies fixations. A cue is a shortcut: it makes search unnecessary, improves recall and keeps attention from being split, and a cue pointing at a target may even widen the perceived visual field. Find the drain valve in this water heater diagram, with and without help.

9

Implicit or explicit?

Implicit, compositional techniques have an aesthetic dimension that suits promotion: a sweeping line that guides the eye also pleases it. Explicit cues suit information and instruction. Also consider the audience: a child may not know that a dashed line implies direction, and children shift attention to important information less readily than adults.
