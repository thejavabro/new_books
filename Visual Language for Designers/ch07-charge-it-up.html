Charge It Up

Good design creates an emotional response, and emotion is not the opposite of thinking. It decides what gets noticed, how it is read and whether it is remembered.

1

Good design creates an emotional response

Some viewers are moved by composition, others by a poignant image or a symbol full of personal meaning, others by humor. Brain research backs the common belief that pictures stir emotion: pleasant and unpleasant pictures produce marked brain activity that neutral ones don't, and in one study the response persisted through ninety repeated showings. People also look longer at emotional pictures. Psychologists split affect into three parts.

Emotional graphics work at a visceral level. They grab attention before the content is processed and bypass much rational analysis, which shapes how the rest of the message is perceived and read.

2

Why aim at the emotions?

Mixed emotions can backfire

Emotions are multifaceted, and a viewer may feel several at once. A study of AIDS-prevention messages found that fear made viewers more likely to comply, but when the message stirred anger along with fear, its persuasive effect disappeared. Pick the reaction a design provokes.

Bar heights are a sketch of the direction of the finding, not measured values. For many purposes, one simple emotional response is the most effective.

3

Emotion, attention and optimal arousal

Emotional content generates arousal, an energized state. Most people find monotony unpleasant and stimulation pleasant, and many psychologists think we seek "newness, change, sensation, or inconsistency" in moderate amounts to stay at an optimal level. A graphic that hits that level keeps viewers with the message; a boring one sends them looking elsewhere. Emotion also affects memory: pleasant material tends to be processed more efficiently and recalled faster, and unpleasant memories fade sooner. Our life story lives in episodic memory, so pictures that trigger autobiographical memories become personal.

4

Emotional salience: color

Emotional salience stands out from neutral graphics much as a red dot pops out from gray ones; people seem to pick up emotional signals preattentively. Of all design elements, color may carry the most emotion, and English is full of it: feeling blue, green with envy, red with anger. Research finds fairly consistent patterns: cool colors calm and warm colors energize; green and blue lower anxiety; red excites; along red to yellow, yellower reads cheerier. Saturation and lightness may matter even more than hue: saturated colors feel more intense, light colors more positive, dark ones more negative. Mix a color.

Tension, faces and symbols

Composition can unsettle on purpose, because viewers need to resolve tension. Ambiguity (indistinct, obstructed forms), exaggeration (overstated form, color or texture) and distortion (people or objects off their expected shape) all keep the mind from closing the picture. Faces add more: we appraise expressions quickly, so an intense expression captures attention and sticks. Try the controls.

Symbols carry feelingThrough experience, people link the symbols of their culture to values and themes: religion, nation, status, oppression, justice. Many symbols thus carry emotional meaning, giving designers a huge vocabulary for profound ideas. A poster built from bullet shells, or a city's emblems after a disaster, can say in one image what a paragraph can't.
6

Narratives

People organize experience as stories and live them vicariously. We'll sit through a thin plot for the emotional drama; and in one study, imagining being inside a story activated brain areas that prepare the body for action. Visual narratives can be explicit, with a beginning, middle and end (comics, photo essays, animations), or implicit: an annual report or brochure that carries one emotional theme page after page. In a sequence, viewers fill in the gaps themselves. Hide the middle and see.

7

Visual metaphors

We understand the unfamiliar through metaphor, transferring the properties of one thing to another. Metaphors are especially good at making ambiguous, ethereal emotions explicit. Two common moves: combine the qualities of two images into one (a cooking mushroom fused with a nuclear mushroom cloud, warning about food-borne illness), or juxtapose two images so one's properties transfer to the other (a sleek computer beside a racing panther). A metaphor can also stand alone, like a history of money told on a board-game board. Build one.

A metaphor only works if viewers know the cultural context and read it figuratively rather than literally. Use recognizable objects and familiar ideas, and remember that young or less experienced viewers may take it literally.

8

Novelty and humor

In a flood of visuals, the unexpected is riveting. Any novel object triggers an orienting response, and because it doesn't match the schemas it activates, viewers spend longer resolving the mismatch. Novelty comes from unusual juxtapositions, odd viewpoints, unexpected themes, type and image at odds. But dose matters: moderate incongruity is liked best, extreme incongruity confuses. Turn it up.

Humor is novelty that amuses: an incongruity the viewer can resolve, a contrast between the everyday and the unexpected. Humorous messages are often found more interesting, but they need taste. A lighthearted take on a grave subject usually weakens the message.
