Reduce Realism

More detail is not more information. Stripping a picture down to what matters makes it faster to see, easier to hold in mind and closer to how memory stores it.

1

The fidelity dial

Realism can be thought of as fidelity: how closely an image resembles what we'd see. Full-color photographs and photorealistic 3-D sit at the high end, rich in detail, depth, shadow, texture and nuanced color. Line drawings, silhouettes and icons sit at the low end. Reducing realism means turning the dial down. Turn it.

Low fidelity works best when the goal is to focus on essentials, get a quick response, strengthen impact or explain something to people new to the topic. A beginner's cookbook may want simplified drawings; a cookbook for chefs may want lush photos. Purpose, audience and content set the right level. Wayfinding, education, explanations and promotion usually benefit from less realism.

2

Why less realism is easier to process

Minimalism helps every stage of the information-processing pipeline from chapter 1.

The surprise from the labInstructional researcher Francis Dwyer, after years of studies, reported that his most surprising finding was how often realistic images failed: the most polished visuals didn't always work.
3

What makes a picture look real?

Every picture is already an interpretation, not a copy. To abstract further, reduce detail and limit one or more dimensions, like color, depth or texture. Perception research identifies qualities that make images look less real: hard shadows rather than soft, smooth surfaces rather than rough, sharp color and contours, and no blurring of distant objects. Toggle them.

Perceived realism

Viewers fill in what's missing from experience: they know how familiar objects are usually drawn, so an abstracted version is read as a normal convention, not as an error. The aim is an idealized, processed version of the real thing, with enough left to recognize it and nothing that misleads.

4

Visual noise

In 3-D work, "noise" (dust, scratches, grain, shading) makes scenes feel real. In an explanation it is meaningless information that has to be decoded and can even create patterns the viewer chases by mistake. Graphics for learning or procedures should carry little or none. Which bolt is longer? Raise the noise and ask again.

To cut noise: reduce extreme texture variation, use flat uniform color, lessen shadows and shading, and quiet the background. Note that the measurement stays red and crisp at every level; the key information should never be part of the noise.

5

Silhouettes: shape alone

A silhouette keeps only the outline: no interior detail, uniform fill, flat space. Because the edge holds most of an object's shape information, recognition is fast when the contour is faithful. A neutral silhouette generalizes (a man becomes Everyman); in emotional work it suggests anonymity, isolation or mystery; given a number, it becomes a pictogram unit. Name these.

Pick a name.

Keep figure and ground apart

A featureless closed shape can flip into ground if it is ambiguous or blends with its background. Use a formless background, a well-defined edge or color contrast, keep negative space out of the figure, and remember smaller shapes tend to be read as figure.

6

Iconic forms

An iconic form is a distilled, stylized depiction that captures the essence of an object or idea through economical shape, line and color. Icons are computationally efficient: little processing is needed to read them, so they are quick to recognize and easy to remember. They suit signs, maps, technical displays, catalogs, diagrams and pictographs, and work as memory aids. Yvonne Rogers sorted icons by how they relate to what they mean. Classify these.

Resemblance, exemplar, symbolic or arbitrary?

Context decides meaning

Designing icons that read instantlyBe precise and use a simple orientation; the side view is often the most recognizable. Prefer an association the viewer can infer over an arbitrary one they must learn. Nigel Holmes notes that a symbol repeated consistently can come to stand for its subject.
7

Line art: edges carry the meaning

Early vision quickly extracts linear features: curvature, orientation and where lines end. Most visual activity happens at edges, so an outline alone can carry meaning, and we read outlines as shapes rather than "arrangements of wires". The Gestalt principle of closure fills the gaps. People identify objects in line drawings about as easily as in photographs, and line drawings can be more memorable. Open the gaps and see when the drawing stops working.

Line art shines in technical and explanatory work: inner workings of machines and bodies, documentation, textbooks, assembly instructions. Capture the contours and prominent features; let viewers bring the rest.

8

Quantity: fewer elements

The natural world is dense. Strictly limiting the number of images, shapes, lines and words reverses that, and every remaining element gets a clear job. At a glance we can count up to about four items without counting, called subitizing, which is close to the capacity of visual working memory. Test yourself: the dots flash for a fraction of a second.

How many were there?

The subtractive approach

Ask what can go. Remove extraneous imagery, shorten text, clean the background, and cluster items in a border so they read as one unit. The design has to keep working after each removal. Strike out elements from this brochure spread.
