Clarify Complexity

Some subjects are too rich to simplify. The aim is to clarify instead: break the explanation into steps, open up what is hidden, and let the shape of the information organize the page.

1

Complexity attracts, then repels

Visual complexity is a paradox. Intricate detail, patterns and overlaps catch the eye and stir curiosity, and viewers fixate on exactly those informative parts. But only up to a point: an extremely complex visual may be avoided altogether. Slide the complexity.

The book separates two kinds. Objective complexity lives in the subject: a system with many interrelated parts, information that is voluminous, dense and unstructured, or a task that needs many operations (worse when attention is split, like phoning while driving). Subjective complexity lives in the viewer. As Jan Elen and Richard Clark put it, complexity seems to be largely in the eye of the beholder.

Why parts add up so fast: interacting elements are harder than isolated ones, because each can affect the others. A router alone is easy; a whole network is not. The links drawn are every possible pair. Experts handle this by having larger schemas that treat clusters as single units, shown as shaded groups.

2

Clarify, don't simplify

Some subjects are too deep to pare down, and Evelyn Goldsmith warns that a drawing stripped of its context in the name of simplicity can lose its communicative value. Complex explanations demand a lot from working memory, but we cope by building schemas into larger chunks and by constructing mental models of how a type of system works. Two things help viewers build accurate ones.

And the book offers three approaches, which the rest of this page walks through.

1. Segment and sequenceSmaller units, a beginning, middle and end.
2. Specialized viewsCutaways, cross sections, exploded views, magnification, implied motion.
3. Inherent structureLet the information's own organizing principle shape the layout.
3

Segments and sequences

People understand less when a whole explanation arrives at once, especially with little prior knowledge: the processing needed exceeds what working memory can hold. Segmenting is a natural strategy; we already parse life into events, songs into verses and books into chapters. Here is how a pin-tumbler lock works, first all at once, then in steps.

overview

Keep the whole in viewSegmenting fails if viewers can't stitch the pieces into one model. Show the big picture or overriding concept first, keep visual continuity between segments, build each on the last, and connect them with the eye-directing techniques of chapter 3. Sequencing is segmenting in real-world order, ideal for procedures and cause and effect. Grouped steps are held together in visual working memory and tend to be encoded together.
4

Specialized views: see inside

Many structures are hidden by a surface or too small to see, and many processes can only be seen through their results. Technical illustration has conventions for revealing them. Novices tend to picture static structure; experts hold structure, function and behavior together, so a good view should help with both. Look inside a flashlight.

When realism helps after allContrary to much of chapter 4, when people must apply what they learn to a real physical system, more realistic drawings can beat highly schematic ones, which may omit details that matter. Another reason the goal is to clarify, not simplify.
5

Implied motion

Static views show structure but not dynamics: how a machine moves, how a product is assembled, how a body or an unseen force acts. Four techniques create the impression of movement. Try each on the same ball.

6

Inherent structure and LATCH

Information has a structure underneath it, like the spokes under an umbrella. A calendar is months and days; soil is layers; a genome map can be radial, suggesting a form of life with no chromosome more important than another. Memory improves whenever information is organized, perhaps because spatial layout is encoded with the meaning. Richard Saul Wurman's LATCH names five ways to organize almost anything: Location, Alphabet, Time, Category, Hierarchy. Each permits a different understanding. Reorganize a town's summer events.

Finding the structure is more than tidy arrangement: it means finding the conceptual basis of the content and expressing it visually, like an evolution exhibit organized along a DNA double helix, or a wetlands sign layered like the ground it explains. Arranging forms side by side by their physical attributes, as field guides do, lets viewers compare and infer.

Recap and quick check
