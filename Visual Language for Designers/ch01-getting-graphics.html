Getting Graphics

Before any principle, a model of the viewer. Light hits the eye, a sliver of it reaches awareness, and what survives is filed by meaning. Designing for that pipeline is the whole idea of the book.

1

We can't help looking

Vision is the dominant sense for most people. Over a million nerve fibers carry signals from each eye to the brain, and billions of neurons work on what arrives. We also remember pictures remarkably well: people can recognize thousands of images they saw only once. And the moment we see something, we ask what is it? and what does it mean?

The book's case is that designers who know how this machinery works can design on purpose: a process explained so it is easy to follow, a poster that lands emotionally, a chart that fits the limits of short-term memory. Information keeps growing, attention keeps shrinking, and audiences are global, so pictures that bypass language are worth more than ever. Each kind of communication job has a visual tool that suits it. Tap a job.

The new job descriptionAn informative graphic has to look good, carry the feeling of its content and make that content understandable. The designer's new role, as the book frames it, is to direct the audience's cognitive and emotional processing.
2

What you meant is not what they saw

A picture is a flat surface pretending to be a space, and viewers hold both readings at once. Each viewer also brings age, culture, language, education, values and expectations. In one study, some audience groups misread the intended meaning of pictures more than half the time, even though they recognized the objects. Recognizing what is shown is easy; getting why it is shown is not.

Designers add their own gap. Art-trained viewers spend more time on backgrounds and on relationships between shapes and colors; untrained viewers look at central figures and objects. Switch viewers to see where each tends to look on the same picture.

Why that mattersBecause you see differently from your audience, you can't fully predict their reading from your own. Knowing how people process pictures is the next best thing to watching them look.
3

The human information-processing system

Cognitive science borrows the computer as a metaphor. Raw sensory data passes through three memory structures: sensory memory, working memory and long-term memory. Only a small slice of what hits the senses survives each step. Walk through it with someone looking at a famous painting.

4

Sharp in the middle, blurry everywhere else

Only a tiny patch of the retina, the fovea, sees sharply. So the eyes keep jumping (saccades) to put whatever matters onto it, pausing in brief fixations about three times a second. It is during fixations that we actually take in a picture; the brain stitches the glimpses together. Move your pointer or finger over the drawing: only the spot under it is sharp.

5

Where bottom-up meets top-down

Perception is not passive recording. Bottom-up processing is driven by the stimulus: edges, color, contrast and motion are picked up fast and without effort, in parallel across specialized brain regions. Top-down processing is driven by what we know, expect and want, and it decides what the shapes mean. Some say we see more with the mind than with the eyes. Here is the same mark in two contexts.

6

Working memory: the bottleneck

Working memory is the mental workspace: where we hold what we are attending to, piece sensory data together and compare it with what we already know. It has separate visual and verbal parts. It is also small and leaky. On average people juggle about three to five chunks at once, and new information fades fast unless it is rehearsed. Try it: memorize the shapes, then pick them out.

Press start. The shapes show for 2.5 seconds.

Most people are fine at 3 or 4 and start slipping at 6 or more. Capacity is not fixed: it grows with age then declines, and it is larger for fast processors, for people who resist distraction and for experts in their own domain. The limit has an upside too: a small, focused workspace lets us switch attention quickly to whatever matters most.

7

Cognitive load

Counting barely touches working memory. Learning something new, solving problems, handling the unfamiliar and ignoring irrelevant material all tax it. The total demand is called cognitive load. When it exceeds capacity, people misunderstand, misread or simply miss information. Graphics can add load or take it away. Set up a situation.

8

Long-term memory: permanent, but hard to search

Long-term memory is functionally unlimited and probably permanent; the trouble is getting things back out. It has several stores. Tap each.

Depth of processing

Information gets in through rehearsal: plain repetition (maintenance) or relating it to what you know (elaborative). How deeply it is processed matters most. Attending only to the look of a graph stores little; following what it means stores far more. We remember best whatever we process at the level of meaning.

Bar lengths are illustrative, not data from the book.

Schemas and automaticity

Knowledge is stored as schemas: generalized structures, not copies. Your schema for house (a place people live, with rooms, windows, a roof) lets you recognize a mud hut and a townhouse alike. Learning is schemas changing; expertise is thousands of rich schemas. With practice a schema runs automatically and stops using working memory. Watch a beginning reader's three letters become one unit.

cat
Beginner: three units held in working memory.
Retrieval runs on cuesAn old song pulls up the band's name because the cue activates linked schemas, and activation spreads. Forgetting is often a weak cue, not missing knowledge. Mental models sit on top of schemas: working theories of cause and effect, like knowing what moving a layer up or lowering its opacity will do in any graphics app.
9

Dual coding: two channels are better than one

Verbal and visual information seem to travel separate channels that still talk to each other. Hearing an artist's name can bring back both facts about them and images of their paintings. Information stored both ways has two routes back out, which is why pairing pictures with words, or narration with animation, improves recall.

10

Know the viewer's cognitive characteristics

You can't predict every reading, but you can know who you are designing for. Drawing on Evelyn Goldsmith's research on illustration, the book lists characteristics that change how well someone can read a picture. Pick an audience to see which ones matter most.

11

Decide what the graphic is for

Gregory Bateson defined information as "a difference that makes a difference", and in a graphic every element is a potential difference. So start from the purpose. Some graphics only need to be noticed and remembered. Some must extend understanding so the viewer sees new relationships. Some must help someone do a task, and must therefore be unambiguous. Sort these.
