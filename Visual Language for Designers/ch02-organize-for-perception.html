Organize for Perception

The first fraction of a second of looking happens without you. Early vision sorts features, splits textures and groups parts into wholes. Hand it a well-organized picture and comprehension gets a head start.

1

Early vision does the sorting for free

Before you decide to look at anything, your visual system has already scanned the scene, pulled out features and built a rough sketch. This preattentive processing is fast, effortless and driven by the stimulus. Later vision, guided by attention, knowledge and goals, then uses that sketch to decide where to look and what things are. The principle is simple: organize a graphic so early vision does as much of the work as possible. It gives the viewer a head start, like a runner who starts before the gun.

Early vision

Preattentive, parallel

  • Driven by the stimulus
  • Vast arrays of neurons in parallel
  • Under about 10 ms per item
  • Detects primitive features, segregates textures, groups parts
  • Output: a rough mental sketch
Later vision

Attentive, serial

  • Driven by knowledge, expectation and goals
  • Item by item
  • Slower and effortful
  • Binds features into objects, recognizes, identifies
  • Output: meaning

William Winn put the stakes plainly: comprehension succeeds or fails depending on whether what preattentive processes organize can be fitted to existing schemas. A good structure at the start has a domino effect: less load on working memory, easier interpretation, fewer chances to misread.

2

Pop-out versus serial search

Find the target and tap it. In feature search the target is the only red item, so it pops out no matter how many distractors there are. In conjunction search the target is the only red circle, but red squares and blue circles share one feature each with it, so you have to check items one by one. Run a few rounds of each at different crowd sizes and watch the times.

Target: the red circle

Typically the blue dots stay flat as the crowd grows while the orange dots climb. That flat line is parallel processing; the climbing line is serial search. The book's example is a pair of AIDS awareness posters built from symbols: a few red figures among black ones are found instantly, while red female symbols among symbols that share their color take real effort.

3

Features that pop out

Research on preattentive vision lists the features that pop out and then pull later attention: color, motion, orientation, size, depth, tilt, shape, line ends, closure, topology (like a dot inside a circle) and curvature. Any one can be emphasized to make something prominent. Pick a feature.

4

Make the difference big enough

To pop out, a feature has to be discriminated from everything around it. Stephen Kosslyn explains that a difference registers as a change in brain-cell activity; if the change is small it gets lost in the brain's natural noise. So don't ask viewers for subtle discriminations. Drag the slider and notice where the odd item stops hiding.

5

Texture segregation

Among our first responses to a scene is to split it into regions of texture, the optical grain of a surface. An abrupt change in texture reads as an edge: here one thing ends and another begins. The textured region usually becomes the figure, the plain one the ground, which is the first step to recognizing objects. Textures built from a simple feature, like line orientation, segregate easily; complex textures on busy backgrounds don't.

Texture gradients make depth

Texture also carries space. Where its pattern looks denser and finer, a surface seems to recede; coarser and sparser, it seems close. Toggle the gradient.

Type as textureRepeated, layered or bent type creates texture even when it can't be read. The words carry a literal message and the grain carries a second, designed one. It suits themes about books, poetry and language.
6

Grouping parts into wholes

Early vision also decides which parts belong together. The Gestalt psychologists showed that wholes often win over parts; newer research added more grouping cues. Same twelve dots each time. Switch the cue and watch the groups change.

Grouping pays off three ways: viewers know clustered items are related, search is faster because related information sits in one place, and new features can emerge from the group, as lines radiating from a point become a sun. A square is seen as one shape, not four lines meeting at right angles.

7

Which tool for which job?

Quick recognition calls for one strong primitive feature against few distractors. Aesthetic depth calls for texture, which also offloads work to perception. Related information calls for grouping, which also gives emphasis, balance and unity. Match each brief.
