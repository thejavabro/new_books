Make the Abstract Concrete

Charts, diagrams, maps and timelines turn ideas into space. Because we already understand space, the right layout lets people see relationships they could not hold in their heads.

1

Visuals help us think

We sketch maps to give directions and draw diagrams to express ideas. Donald Norman put it bluntly: the unaided mind is overrated. Two classics from the history of graphs make the point. In 1854 Dr. John Snow plotted cholera deaths in London and traced them to a contaminated water pump. During the Crimean War, Florence Nightingale invented a chart showing soldiers dying far more from preventable disease than from wounds, which made her case for sanitation hard to ignore.

Fifty cases. Is there a pattern?

Diagrams, charts, graphs, visualizations, maps and timelines go by many names: abstract, nonrepresentational, logical, arbitrary graphics. They share one purpose: to make abstract ideas concrete. They were once the domain of statisticians and cartographers; now designers and illustrators make them for editorial, scientific, business, educational and promotional work.

2

How abstract graphics work

Abstract graphics look objective, so people trust them like photographs. Yet each one is the product of many subjective choices: what fits and what's left out, symbol or illustration, which colors, which conventions to keep. Two traits set them apart from pictorial images. First, every element has a one-to-one correspondence with what it stands for: the picnic-table icon on a map means "picnic area" and nothing else. Second, they depict relationships. Each form specializes.

3

Space conveys meaning

We understand abstract graphics quickly because we already understand space. On a map the relationship is analogous: west on the map is west in the world. In charts and diagrams it is metaphorical: up means more, the top or left of a hierarchy means most important, distance on a timeline means time apart. These readings are "cognitively natural", so they need fewer mental transformations than text. Break the metaphor and see what happens.

4

Design for early vision, and mean every difference

Complex abstract graphics can be tuned for preattentive processing (chapter 2), shifting work from working memory to perception. Make similar things the same color or shape so nobody has to discriminate needlessly, group related entities, and let size carry value: half the value, half the size. Then make sure every visible difference is intentional, because viewers will assume it means something. Add some accidental differences to this diagram and read what a viewer would infer.

Text, color, representationTitles, legends, captions and labels add a second channel; keep them legible, brief and consistent. Color can code categories (road types, income bands) and is stored in memory with what it codes. And choose the form of each feature carefully: an illustrated diagram of two people on phones explains internet calling better than boxes and lines, and changes the tone too. Follow notation conventions unless surprise is the point.
5

Big-picture views: diagrams

Here a diagram means a visual explanation of a system, not of numbers: its parts and their relationships, which can show structure, process, transformation, cycle or function. Arrows do much of the work, and they change what a diagram means. In one study, people described a device diagram without arrows in terms of its structure, and the same diagram with arrows in terms of cause, effect and function. Toggle the arrows.

Each arrow style says something different. Tap to see what.

Enter at the global levelA diagram's pattern (cycle, hierarchy, tree, flow) shapes how it is stored in memory: a cyclical diagram tends to be remembered as a cycle. Viewers can enter at the global pattern or at a local detail, and where they enter is where they start searching. Most diagrams should invite the global view first, so make elements big enough to show the pattern, but not so big that one element and its detail hog the eye.
6

Data displays: pick the form for the task

Howard Wainer observed that a child can see that a third of a pie is bigger than a quarter long before they can compare 1/3 and 1/4. Graphs make quantities concrete, yet people find them the hardest abstract graphics: they must decode marks, recall graph schemas, read labels and scale, and compare. John Tukey said the point of a graph is impact and immediacy: if the message emerges slowly, use another display. Research finds bar graphs better for comparisons, line graphs for trends, and tables for looking up exact values. Choose a task.

Four to seven perceptual units

Stephen Kosslyn's advice for fitting working memory: keep a display to about four to seven perceptual units, make every mark big enough to see and every variation easy to discriminate, and group labels with what they label. Add lines and see when the chart stops being readable.

Also from the chapter: use precalculated values such as percentages and averages so readers don't compute; add variables with color or size rather than extra axes; don't make viewers break apart something the eye has grouped, like the points of a line.

7

Visualizing information

Information visualizations make the structure of large data sets visible: more than a million measurements at once, as Colin Ware notes, revealing emergent patterns nobody expected. They are often computer-generated, interactive, sometimes real-time, and insight comes from exploring and rearranging them. A parallel genre of illustrated visualizations works on smaller data: someone's week, the structure of a novel, the shared words in sacred texts. Here is a tiny one: a week of a student's time, one square per hour awake. Sort it different ways.

Good visualizations, computer-made or hand-made, are easy to perceive, structure data in a fresh way, communicate comparisons efficiently, behave intuitively when interactive, and are attractive enough to draw people in.

More than geography

A map is a reduced version of a space and can't hold everything, so it shows what matters for its purpose. There are as many "realities" as purposes, as Lynn Liben writes. A map informs on several levels. Step through them.

9

Snapshots of time

We think of time spatially: usually as a line moving forward, sometimes as a cycle like the seasons, sometimes as a spiral that recurs and advances. Timelines are the most common form, usually running left to right or bottom to top, and they can span a day, a company's history or the deep time of geology. In history, sequence can suggest cause and effect. Same eight years of events, three shapes of time.
