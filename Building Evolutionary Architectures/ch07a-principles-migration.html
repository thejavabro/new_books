Evolvable Architectures: Principles and Migration

Part III brings mechanics and structure together. This first half of Chapter 7 lays out the principles, the three-step method, and how to move an existing system toward something that can evolve.

1

Five principles over mechanics and structure

Most of the ideas in this book are old ideas seen through a new lens: testing aimed at architecture, Continuous Delivery pipelines carrying governance. Over both mechanics and structure sit five principles. Open each one.

2

Decide at the last responsible moment

Decide too early and you guess with little information, so you hedge with general, over-engineered solutions. Decide too late and you miss architectural goals. The sweet spot is the latest point that doesn't hold anything up. Move the decision date.

3

Postel's Law for contracts

Jon Postel's networking maxim, be conservative in what you send and liberal in what you accept, softens coupling points. A billing service needs only a customer's phone number. Choose how the sender and the receiver behave, then change the address format upstream.

4
Three steps to operationalize it

  

    
Identify dimensionsWhich characteristics must survive evolution? Always the technical architecture; usually data, security, scalability and other "-ilities." Involve business, operations and security. Multirole teams help.

    
Define fitness functionsEach dimension may need several: metrics, tests, manual checks. Record the decisions in something lightweight such as a wiki or ADRs.

    
Automate in pipelinesRun them in deployment pipeline stages alongside provisioning and testing. Watch cycle time: the faster new generations appear, the faster the architecture can evolve.

  

  
It never finishesUnknown unknowns mean many fitness functions only reveal themselves when something starts going wrong. Watch for broken nonfunctional requirements and retrofit checks so the problem can't grow.






  
5
Greenfield is easy; retrofitting depends on three things

  
New projects can build a pipeline on day one and choose an evolvable style (microservices is the obvious example). For an existing system, evolvability depends on component coupling, engineering practice maturity and how easily the team can write fitness functions. Rate your system.

  

    
    
    
    

    

  

  






  
6
Migrating: find the right partitions

  
The common journey is from a "share everything" monolith to a "share as little as possible" service-based architecture. Have a better reason than fashion. Then choose how to cut. Too coarse and little changes; too fine and orchestration, chatter and interdependency take over. Pick a partitioning criterion to see where the cuts fall.

  

    

    

    

  

  
Start big, then repeatSam Newman's advice, quoted in the book: build a small number of larger services first. Break off a few big chunks, fix the integration points, add consumer-driven contracts and other fitness functions around the new seams, then iterate.






  
7
What to do with shared classes

  
Domains are usually easy to split; the awkward part is shared helpers like an Address class. LCOM (Lack of Cohesion in Methods, from the Chidamber & Kemerer suite) tells you whether a class was ever really one thing. Below, tick which fields each method uses. Methods that share no fields form separate clusters; more clusters means less cohesion and an easy split.

8
Then: separate the UI, add discovery

  

  
Indirection has a price"All problems in computer science can be solved by another level of indirection, except for the problem of too many indirections." Every proxy makes navigation harder, and every former in-process call is now a network call with latency and availability concerns.






  
9
Metawork is more interesting than work

  
Bored senior developers would often rather build a framework than use one. One client built its own application server, web framework and nearly every other piece of infrastructure. When open source equivalents matured, they kept their own over minor differences. A decade later their best people were full-time plumbers. Architects can fall for the same thing.
