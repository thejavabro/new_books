Evolving Software Architecture

Software never sits still: the ecosystem shifts under it and its own parts slowly erode. This chapter defines an architecture that can change on purpose: guided, incremental change across multiple dimensions.

1

The ground keeps moving

Two forces make software hard to keep healthy. First, a real system has thousands of parts, and no human can police every interaction between them by hand. Second, the ecosystem around the code (languages, tools, frameworks, platforms, practices) is in dynamic equilibrium: it reaches a balance developers can work within, then something new arrives and upsets it until a new balance forms.

The book's image is a unicyclist carrying boxes. They stay upright only by constantly adjusting, and someone keeps tossing more boxes on top. Toss a few and watch.

Why five-year plans stopped workingIf the whole development world shifts within five years, a long-range architecture plan written today is likely to be obsolete before it is finished. The response is not better prediction; it is making change cheap and safe.
2

Big shifts are many small steps

Disruptive changes look sudden, but you can usually trace them through a series of small, unremarkable steps. Containers are the book's example. Step through the chain, then try the thought experiment underneath.

3

Requirements plus characteristics

Structural design splits into two parts. The domain is the problem the software solves: the requirements. The architecture characteristics are everything else that decides success: scale, performance, security, modularity and the rest of the "-ilities." The book uses that term; others say nonfunctional requirements, quality attributes or cross-cutting requirements. They mean the same thing.

The domain already has protection: unit, functional and acceptance tests. Architecture characteristics need the same kind of protection, and they often change for reasons that have nothing to do with the domain (a company-wide database switch, say).

4

Guided, incremental, multidimensional

Everything in the book flows from one short definition: an evolutionary architecture supports guided, incremental change across multiple dimensions. Pick a part.

Who counts as "the architect"?Anyone who makes architectural decisions, whatever their job title. And the important decisions nearly always involve collaborating with other roles.
5

One change, many dimensions

Architects often focus on the technical dimension (frameworks, libraries, languages), but a change ripples through others too. Common dimensions are technical, data, security and operational, plus whichever "-ilities" matter for this system. Each important dimension gets its own fitness functions. Choose a change and see which dimensions it touches.

6

How architectures quietly degrade

Even if the ecosystem stood still, architectures erode from inside. The classic case: a layered system where presentation sits on top and persistence at the bottom. Someone building reports asks to read the database straight from the UI "just for speed." Then someone else does. Each bypass makes sense alone; together they wipe out the reason for having layers.

Evolvability is a meta-characteristicIf you designed for scalability, you want it to survive the next 200 features. Making a system evolvable means wrapping protection around every other characteristic you care about. That is why this work overlaps so heavily with architectural governance.
7

Why "evolutionary"?

Why not call it incremental, continual, agile, reactive, emergent or adaptable architecture? The authors want a word that carries two ideas at once: change happens in small steps, and it is steered toward a goal. They also want real structural change, not patches layered on patches. Test each word.

8

How little design can you afford?

Agile is sometimes misread as "no architecture; it will emerge." The book disagrees. A dog house needs lumber, not blueprints; a 50-story office tower needs design. Software is the same, and agility means removing useless design, not all of it. Emergence also implies you can start from nothing, while architecture is the scaffolding that everything else hangs on. Slide from small to large.

There is no perfect architectureMost problems can be solved successfully in several styles; some just fit with less friction. The key balance is enough structure and governance for long-term goals, without needless formality.
