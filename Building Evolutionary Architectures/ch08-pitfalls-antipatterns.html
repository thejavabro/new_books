Pitfalls and Antipatterns

Some ideas look good and fail slowly (antipatterns); others look good and fail fast (pitfalls). This chapter catalogs the ones that most often stop a system from evolving.

1

Antipattern or pitfall?

People often say "antipattern" to mean "bad". The book is more precise. An antipattern looks like a good idea at first and only reveals itself as a mistake later, and a better alternative usually exists. A pitfall looks superficially attractive but shows itself to be a bad path almost immediately. Because antipatterns are only obvious in hindsight, they are the harder of the two to avoid.

The chapter groups its traps into three areas: technical architecture, incremental change, and business concerns.

2

Antipattern: the Last 10% Trap

Rapid-development tools (4GLs once, low-code and no-code platforms now) make the first 80% of a client's wishes quick and easy. The next 10% is possible only with clever hacks that bend the tool beyond its design. The final 10% simply can't be done. Every project starts as a triumph and ends as a disappointment. Build the same app in both kinds of tool.

Evaluate the hardest problem firstWhen trying such a tool, skip "Hello, World". Easy things will be easy. What you need to learn early is what the tool can't do, so you can plan alternatives for it.
3

Case study: when reuse becomes a bottleneck

PenultimateWidgets built a reusable admin grid component (UI, validation, sensible defaults) so teams could build admin screens quickly. Over time the component team became a silo: swamped with requests, holding several of the best developers, and running on aging web code. Run the simulation, then apply the fix the architects chose.

Two takeawaysWhen a coupling point impedes evolution, break it by forking or duplication. And keep re-evaluating decisions: a choice that was right when made can turn into an antipattern as the ecosystem shifts. Too much coupling is often worse than duplication.
4

Antipattern: Vendor King

Some organizations build their entire architecture around one vendor product, typically an ERP suite, then try to stretch it with plug-ins. The tool dictates persistence, infrastructure and even business processes, and often runs into the Last 10% Trap. A related folk principle: "Let's Stop Working and Call It a Success", where buyer and vendor both quietly declare victory with much of the promise unimplemented. The escape: treat the product as just another integration point, behind an anticorruption layer.

5

Pitfall: leaky abstractions

Joel Spolsky observed that every nontrivial abstraction leaks to some degree. Modern stacks contain many more layers than they used to, so when something misbehaves near the bottom, the failure seeps all the way up and surfaces as a baffling error in the UI. The authors call this primordial abstraction ooze. Tap a low layer in each stack to break it.

6

Pitfall: resume-driven development

Choosing technology to decorate your CV rather than to solve the problem. The task here: a small internal tool to book meeting rooms for 40 people. Tap what you'd use.

7

Antipattern: inappropriate governance

When servers, operating systems and databases were expensive, governance standardized on one shared stack to save money. Cheap, isolated environments changed that balance, but many organizations still follow the old playbook. Forcing everyone onto the stack that suits the most complex project creates inadvertent overcomplication for everyone else. PenultimateWidgets settled on "just enough" governance: three standard stacks, chosen by need.

Forced decouplingAt Wunderlist, Chad Fowler went further: teams had to use different stacks, precisely so nobody could reuse code across services by accident. His judgment was that accidental coupling costs more than reduced developer mobility.
8

Pitfall: lack of speed to release

Cycle time runs from when a developer starts a feature to when it is live in production. (Lead time also counts estimation and prioritization, which makes it a fuzzier engineering metric.) Like fruit flies in genetics labs, fast generations reveal results sooner: an architecture can't evolve faster than its cycle time allows. That makes cycle time a natural process fitness function. Here, a team starts at three hours and sets an alarm at four.

The alarm doesn't decide for you; it creates a decision point (a last responsible moment). Without it, cycle time creeps up unnoticed and the trade-off between more checks and faster delivery is made implicitly.

9

Pitfall: product customization

Sales wants options to sell. Customization comes in three escalating forms: a unique build per customer (branches and tags), permanent feature toggles (including "freemium" tiers), and customization built into the UI. All of them multiply the combinations you must test and protect with fitness functions. That is not a reason to refuse, just a reason to price the cost honestly.

10

Antipattern: reporting atop the system of record

Order entry and monthly analysis want very different data shapes; a schema designed for both is optimized for neither. Report writers often bypass the carefully layered architecture and query the tables directly, since reports need only data and layers add latency. Then nobody can change the schema without breaking reports. The common fix in service architectures: services publish events; separate reporting services consume them into a denormalized store (or, at larger scale, a data mesh).

11

Pitfall: excessively long planning horizons

Long budgeting cycles force decisions early, when you know least. The more effort you pour into research and plans, the more attached you become (the sunk cost fallacy). The authors call the software version irrational artifact attachment: the more time a document cost, the harder you defend it against evidence. Slide the planning horizon.

Remedy: break programs into small early deliverables that test both the architecture and the delivery infrastructure, and avoid technologies needing a big up-front commitment (licenses, support contracts) before users have confirmed the fit.
