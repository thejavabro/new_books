Topologies: Connascence and Quanta

Evolvability comes down to coupling, not to the name of an architecture style. Connascence gives you words for coupling, and the architecture quantum tells you how big the smallest independent, deployable piece really is.

1

Evolvability is about coupling

Nothing about a named architecture style makes it evolvable on its own. What matters is its coupling: how the pieces connect and lean on each other. You can't build complex software without some coupling, so the goal is appropriate coupling: the right dimensions bound together, for maximum benefit and minimum cost. Two older ideas, from object-oriented design and domain-driven design, turn out to say the same thing about it.

2

Connascence: words for kinds of coupling

Meilir Page-Jones (1996) called two components connascent when changing one would force a change in the other to keep the system correct. The value is vocabulary: once a team can name a kind of coupling, it can talk about weakening it (the Sapir–Whorf idea that language shapes thought). Static forms can be seen in source code; dynamic forms (dashed) only show up at runtime. They run from weakest to strongest. Tap each.

Refactor toward weaker connascence

  
Static connascence is preferable because tools can find and fix it. The usual move is to convert something strong into connascence of name, which modern refactoring tools change everywhere in one step. Apply each refactoring.

How much does this coupling hurt?

  
Connascence has three properties. Strength: how hard it is to refactor. Locality: how far apart the coupled pieces sit; strong coupling inside one module is fine, the same coupling across modules or codebases is not. Degree: how many pieces it touches. Codebases grow, so a small degree rarely stays small. Combine them.

Minimize overall connascence by breaking the system into encapsulated elements. Minimize what crosses those boundaries. Maximize connascence within them.

Rule of locality

5
Locality and bounded context say the same thing

  
Domain-driven design's bounded context keeps everything about a domain visible inside and opaque outside. Instead of one company-wide Customer class, each context has its own and reconciles at the edges through contracts. That is the locality rule in different words: let implementation coupling spread wide and the architecture turns brittle, where a small change in one place breaks something unexpected far away. The extreme case: using an application's database schema as an integration point.

  

    

    

    

  






  
6
The architecture quantum

  
Bounded context is a design idea. Architects need its counterpart in technical terms. In physics a quantum is the smallest amount involved in an interaction; here an architecture quantum is an independently deployable artifact with high functional cohesion, high static coupling and synchronous dynamic coupling. In plain words: the smallest thing you can deploy on its own that does a cohesive job, including everything it needs to run (database, broker, operating system). Dynamic coupling: three forces that pull on each other

  
Static coupling describes how things are wired; dynamic coupling describes how quanta call each other at runtime. Three forces interact: communication (synchronous or asynchronous), consistency (atomic or eventual) and coordination (orchestrated or choreographed). You can't pick them independently; each choice pulls the others. Set a combination.

A synchronous call blocks until the receiver answers; an asynchronous call posts a message (often to a queue) and carries on, with any reply arriving later. Cross-service transactions are among the hardest problems in distributed systems, so the usual advice is to avoid them.
