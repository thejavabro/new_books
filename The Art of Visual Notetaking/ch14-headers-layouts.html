Headers, Layouts and Time Blocking

A header tells readers what they are looking at. A layout is the road map. Time blocking keeps you on that map so the page fills just as the talk ends.

1

Headers: the cover of your notes

The header sits at the top of the canvas, where it is read first, and introduces the session. Putting it at the end would be like printing a book's cover on its last page. The author borrows the word from web design. A header combines some of: the presenter's name and title, the talk title, the event name, a portrait, the event logo and the date. Build one.

How much time?No single answer. Beginners should allow extra time; you'll speed up. A header is a creative outlet, but a simple, quick one is fine in a crunch. With the schedule in hand, sketch it before the event.
How much space?Depends on canvas size and talk length. Worried about room? Keep it small and simple. A short talk (under 20 minutes) is a good excuse for a bigger header to balance the light content.

Four steps to a header

ExerciseImagine you're giving a talk. Make three headers for yourself: (1) name, title, talk title, portrait and event; (2) portrait, name and talk title; (3) just your name and the talk title.
3

Layouts: the road map

Decide the layout before you start. A defined space gives you freedom and confidence in the moment; no plan invites indecision and wasted space. Beginners: left to right, top to bottom. And never fill the page randomly, wherever there's white space: people want to know where to begin and when they're done. Tap a layout to watch its path.

4

Choosing the best layout

Not every layout suits every kind of content. Whatever you choose, use hierarchy, arrows and labels to guide the reader, and keep the focus on the information. Match each session to a layout.

Time blocking

"Did you get the notes ahead of time?" Never. But with a schedule, the author knows how long the talk runs and blocks out sections in pencil with time stamps. Steps: (1) find out how long the presenter speaks; (2) choose a layout; (3) divide the talk into increments and pencil them along the layout, leaving room for a header first. She typically uses 8- to 10-minute blocks, and 15 for longer talks. Many presenters cram the most into the end, so give the final block extra space.
