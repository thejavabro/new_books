Tools: Your Notetaking Arsenal

The question everyone asks is "what pen is that?" The honest answer: tools matter far less than practice. Still, a few smart choices make the work easier.

1

The tools do not make the artist

People ask the author "what pen are you using?" far more often than "how did you learn?" Her answer is that regular practice is the only route to effective visual notes. Some of the best she has seen were done with a ballpoint on a legal pad. Use good tools if you like, but the priority is the information, not the aesthetics. Drag the sliders and watch which one actually moves the result.

2

Choosing a surface

Decide how the notes will be used before you choose the surface. Sharing online? Square and horizontal images suit most social platforms, so a tall legal pad is a poor fit. Pick a surface to see its trade-offs.

      Paper: weight and tooth

      Real-life lessonThick, smooth card stock seemed perfect for a client piece. In practice the markers didn't blend well and the slick surface smudged. Test papers with your actual pens before a job.
      3

      Did you catch that? PEN, not pencil

      Beginners worry about mistakes, which is exactly why the author wants you in ink. Ink can't be edited, so it forces failure, and you learn more and faster from failure than from small safe tweaks. Pen also builds confidence and is easier to read than pencil. Run both practice plans.

      Pack your bag

      Beyond pens, a small kit saves the day. Tap each item you would pack for a full-day conference in a dim ballroom with no tables. Then check your bag.

      6

      Three questions for choosing tools

      The book groups its advice into three checks. Tools should meet your audience's needs (how will the notes be shared and seen?), work together (does the ink smudge on this paper? does yellow marker over black pen bleed? does a lefty's hand pick up color?), and suit the environment. Try the environment scenarios.
