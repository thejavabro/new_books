Visual Direction and Finding Information Fast

Readers need to know where to look next, and you need to find things again later. Containers, numbers and arrows guide the eye; icons and an index guide the search.

1

Visual direction: tell the eye where to go

Visual direction covers the elements that control how notes are read. They balance the page so it doesn't overwhelm, separate the path of content, and tell the reader where to look next. Three workhorses: containers, numbers and arrows. Toggle them on a page of notes and feel the difference.

2

Containers

The speaker makes a recommendation, says something profound, shares a quote or adds a side comment. You want it to stand apart: put it in a container. Containers can spotlight big ideas or tuck lesser points away from the main flow, subtle or bold. Pick a container for each moment.

ExerciseCopy the container styles from the book, then invent ten of your own.

Numbers: write them last

"Five ways to grow your business" calls for numbers, and capturing them accurately is harder than it looks. Presenters often forget to say which point they're on, or lose track. Run the same talk with two strategies.

Arrows

Arrows are one of the easiest, clearest ways to direct readers and call attention. They don't have to be plain: an arrow can be a title, a container for text, or a subtle nudge to the next spot. Cycle through styles.

ExerciseDraw ten kinds of arrows, then go back to old notes and use arrows to connect blocks of text and lead the reader to the next point.
5

Icons: a map key for your notes

Why revisit notes at all? For your own retention, even if nobody else sees them, and visual notes are much easier to reread than typed ones. Two tools help you find things fast. The first is icons. A map packs lots of information and uses small icons, explained in a key. Do the same: small, simple, recognizable drawings for recurring concepts (bullet journalers will know the idea). Keep them small so they link information without stealing focus. Tap an icon in the key to find every matching item.

6

Indexing your sketchbooks

After a few years the author had a stack of sketchbooks and no quick way to find anything. Her index system:

  1. When you start a sketchbook, write the date of the first notes (month and year) inside the cover or on the first page.
  2. As you go, list each note's title or event in a single column, with page numbers if the book has them.
  3. When it's full, add the date of the last notes after the first. Now each book has a time span.

Other options: thin sticker labels with date ranges on the spines (front cover for spiral or thin books), or white/metallic pen on black covers. Level up by drawing an icon next to each index entry to show its type. Try it: find the notes from a given talk.
