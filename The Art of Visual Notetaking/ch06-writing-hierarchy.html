Writing, Lettering and Hierarchy

Legible writing is the backbone of visual notes. Then a handful of lettering styles and three clear sizes tell readers what matters most at a glance.

1

Print: draw your letters

Pen to paper at last. If you dislike your handwriting, don't worry: legibility is learnable. Start by practicing a plain, classic print alphabet, even if it isn't how you usually write, and use it in your notes at least once. The trick that helped the author relax: don't write letters, draw them, one deliberate stroke at a time.

Practice pad

Trace over the faint letters with your finger, mouse or stylus. Slow is fine.

Real-life exampleThe author's very first visual notes, from a church sermon, were barely readable. Once she wanted to share them, she rebuilt her handwriting into something clearer. It was slower at first, and it was worth it.
2

Cursive: letters that link

Not everyone learned cursive at school. Practice the alphabet, then whole words, watching how the letters join. The book suggests words that hit awkward joins. Tap one to load it as a tracing guide.

3

Block letters: three ways to go bold

Block letters are a bold version of print, great for titles, headers and anything that needs emphasis. Type a word and switch methods.

4

Serif letters: add feet

Serifs are the little "feet" at the ends of strokes. Compare the three styles on one letter, then on a word.

5

Embellishments: shadows, textures, color

Extras add emphasis and interest. Toggle them on a heading. But mind the warning underneath.

  • Shadow: choose one side, or two adjacent sides, and add a gray or black mirror of each letter behind it. Or run a flat shadow along the baseline.
  • Texture: dots, lines or shapes break up uniform letters; works best on empty (outlined) letters. Try it as a gradient.
  • Color: a few colors that work together, with enough contrast against the paper.
  • Cursive: add a second line on one side of each down-stroke for a hand-lettered look, or use a brush marker.
Get the information firstEmbellishing can stop you listening. The author only embellishes in a lull or afterward. If she wants a flourish, she leaves extra space, jots the idea in pencil and moves on.
6

Hierarchy: three sizes

Hierarchy tells readers how important each piece is, at a glance. Good hierarchy feels balanced: a mix of large and small. Start with three sizes, borrowed from journalism and the web: Headline (largest: title and central ideas; may be block or serif), Subheadline (key phrases, quotes, action items) and Body (plain everyday handwriting; most content). Stay consistent; too many styles and sizes create chaos.
