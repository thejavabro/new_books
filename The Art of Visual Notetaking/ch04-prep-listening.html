Prep Work and Listening

1

Get the best seat

You can't take good notes if you can't hear. Hearing the speaker clearly is the most important thing about a seat. The book lists three sneaky sources of distraction: doorways (people coming and going, light changes, noise), people (talkers, fidgeters, babies and children) and vents (air on you or your pages, temperature swings, noise). The author's trick: arrive early, introduce yourself to the sound booth crew and ask where you'll hear best. Often it is near the booth. Tap a seat.

Tap any seat to score it.

2

Get a schedule, then plan the space

People ask how the author spaces everything so perfectly. Easy: planning ahead. An event schedule gives speaker names, talk titles and time frames, so you can fill those in before the session starts. Once you know the length, sketch your plan: she pencils loose time marks every 5 to 15 minutes across the page and does roughly one notebook page per 30 minutes. (Chapter 15 turns this into full time blocking.)

Visual notes are a listening skill first and a drawing skill second. Throughout the book the author warns against anything that pulls you away from listening: embellishing during the talk, drawing diagrams in detail, editing on a whiteboard. Listening cues: spot the signals

Reconstructed

Speakers signal what matters. Phrases like "the key thing is," numbered lists, repetition, a change of slide, a story or a quotable line all tell you "capture this." Read the transcript and tap every phrase you think is a cue. Then check.
