Drawing: Dots, Lines, Shapes and People

Every drawing is built from dots, lines and shapes. Aim for recognizable, not realistic, then "level up" a little at a time.

1

Recognizable beats realistic

"I can't even draw a stick figure!" The author hears it constantly, and it misses the point. Visual notes are about accurate information, and they work as well with stick figures as with polished art. Your brain recognizes a house whether it is five lines or a detailed sketch. So the rule for this whole chapter: go for recognizable over realistic. Slide through the levels. At which level did you know it was a house?

2

Everything is a dot, a line or a shape

All drawings reduce to three elements. A dot is pen touching paper (many dots make shading and texture; spread them out for a gradient). A line is a dot that travels: straight, curvy or crooked; parallel, perpendicular or overlapping. A shape is a line that returns to its start: curvy or straight, filled or empty. Build a light bulb one element at a time.

3

Imagination and association

Our brains are great at association, finding familiar things in unrelated forms, like a dog in a passing cloud. The book's exercise: draw plain shapes, then add dots, lines and shapes to turn them into something. Pick a shape and cycle through ideas.

Stuck? Search "… clip art"Look up reference images on your phone. Adding "clip art" to the search returns simplified versions that are easy to redraw. Use them to learn, never to copy someone's work and pass it off as yours.
Ask a friendIn workshops, everyone draws the same object differently. Asking someone else to draw it is a fast way to find new ideas.
4

Leveling up a drawing

"Leveling up" is the author's term for improving a drawing. After each one, ask: is there anything I can add to make this more recognizable? If so, add it. If not, fine. Every addition should be intentional, not decoration. Add upgrades to the coffee cup and see which help.

5

Four ways to draw people

Drawing people matters because people mostly talk about people. Our brains are wired for faces; infants recognize them before most objects. The book gives four starting figures, each with ways to level up from left to right.

6

Faces carry the emotion

Often a head is enough; the face tells the story. Faces don't even need to be human: a smiling dog or a sad rain cloud isn't realistic, but it adds clarity. The book's exercise: draw 10 to 15 circles and fill each with an emotion from a long list. Consider intensity, age, angle and extras such as tears or hearts. Stuck? Look at the emoji on your phone.

7

Hands: palm, fingers, thumb

Hands are the most requested demo in the author's workshops. They look hard because they have many moving parts, so break them into three: palm, fingers, thumb. Step through four methods, then the leveling-up tips.

ExercisePose your non-drawing hand and draw it: open with fingers apart; open with fingers together; pointing at you; holding a pen; a fist with the thumb over the fingers.
