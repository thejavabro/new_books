Cartoon-Style Faces

Faces carry emotion faster than words. Mix and match a few simple parts and you have a cast of characters, and eventually a style of your own.

1

Why cartoon faces work

The author loves cartoon faces in sketchnotes: they are fun, familiar, and they take away the pressure to be realistic. Anyone who reads comics knows there are endless styles. The book's approach is to practice each feature on its own, then mix and match until a style of your own emerges. The builder below does exactly that.

2

Head first, or face first

Some draw the head before the face, some after. The author does both: sometimes a face inspires the head shape, sometimes she knows the head and invents the face. Try changing the width at one end, front versus side views, adding a chin, and using straight, curvy or crooked parallel lines for necks of different lengths.

3

Feature by feature

The eyes deserve the most practice: they carry most of the emotion. Mouths come next; make faces in a mirror and exaggerate what you see. Noses: keep them quick, and maybe skip nostrils (the author says hers always turn into a pig nose). Ears sit between the tops of the eyes and the bottom of the nose, but cartoons can move them; on a detailed ear she draws only the top ridge (helix) and the bump by the opening (tragus).

4

Adding age, tastefully

Age lives mostly in the eyes and mouth. For eyes: slight bags below, crow's feet at the side, relaxed lids. For mouths: lines from the sides of the nose to the corners of the mouth, short lines at the mouth's sides, small wrinkle lines on the lips. Slide to add them one by one.

5

Putting it all together

The book's final exercise: draw eight head shapes, add eyes, nose, mouth, ears and hair to each, mixing styles. Then write down what you like and dislike about each. Narrowing those preferences is how your own cartooning style forms. Generate a sheet, and star the ones you like.
