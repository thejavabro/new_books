1

A house has a structure and a look

Think of a house. Its architecture is the structure: the outer walls, how many rooms and floors, where the stairs and doors go. Those things are expensive to change once built. Its design is the look and feel: paint, rugs, where the bed goes. You can change those on a weekend.

Software works the same way. A building plan is lines and boxes for walls and rooms. A software architecture diagram is lines and boxes for user interfaces, services, databases and how they talk.

Blue lines are the structure. Orange is decoration. Hide one layer and see which is harder to change.

2

Four dimensions describe any architecture

A room needs three measurements (length, width, height) before you can describe it. A software architecture needs four dimensions. They fit together like puzzle pieces, and every piece has to line up with the other three. Click a piece.

All four pieces meet in the middle. The style must suit the characteristics, and the components must suit the style and the decisions.

3

Characteristics: what matters most?

Architectural characteristics are the capabilities critical to the system's success: scalability, availability, performance, testability and friends. You may know them as non-functional requirements, quality attributes or simply "the -ilities". They are the foundation, because you cannot weigh any choice until you know what you are optimising for.

4

Decisions: rules the whole team builds within

Architectural decisions are choices about structure with long-term impact. One floor or two? Flat or peaked roof? In software: which database, how many services, how they talk. Each decision becomes a constraint that guides the developers. Big systems often have dozens of them.

Decision: "The UI must not talk to the database directly. It goes through services."

Every screen follows the same path. The team knows exactly where data access code belongs.

5

Logical components: the rooms of the system

Rooms are the building blocks of a home. Logical components are the building blocks of a system: each one does one clear job, like taking an order or processing a payment. In code, a component usually shows up as a directory (or namespace) that holds the source for that job. Click a component to find its folder.

Domain vs. functionality. The domain is the problem you are solving (people want to buy shoes online). The system's functionality is how you solve it (Order Placement, Payment Processing, Inventory Management). Domain is the "what". Functionality is the "how".
6

Styles: the overall shape

Houses come in styles like ranch, Tudor or Victorian, and each style tells you a lot about the structure. Software styles do the same. Each one brings its own strengths. The book covers five of them in later chapters.

LayeredSplit by technical layer. Simple and cheap.
Modular monolithOne unit, split by business area.
MicrokernelSmall core plus plug-ins. Great for customising.
MicroservicesMany small services. Scales well and changes fast.
Event-drivenServices react to events. Very fast and scalable.

8

Most decisions sit on a spectrum

Choosing a style is clearly architecture. Moving a field on a screen is clearly design. Most decisions land somewhere in between, and where they land tells you who should make the call: the developers, the architect, or both together. Three questions place a decision on the line.

Strategic or tactical?
Days of planning, many people, long-lived = strategic. A quick, temporary call made alone = tactical.
Effort to build or change?
The harder it is to undo later, the more architectural.
How big are the trade-offs?
Big pros and cons need long analysis. Small ones you just decide.
DesignArchitecture
Decide together

"Software architecture is the stuff that's hard to change."

Martin Fowler's short definition, quoted in the chapter. If a choice is painful to reverse, treat it as architecture.

Nothing is free

Every decision has a trade-off, even ice cream: chocolate means no vanilla today. The question is never "is there a trade-off" but "how big is it".

Recap on one page

Structure vs. appearanceArchitecture is mostly structure. Design is mostly appearance.
CharacteristicsThe capabilities the system must have. Know them first or you cannot weigh trade-offs.
DecisionsStructural rules with long-term impact. They guide and constrain the team.
ComponentsThe functional building blocks, usually a folder or namespace of code.
StyleThe overall shape. Each style favours certain characteristics, so pick it to match.
All four, always"We use microservices" describes one dimension. You need all four to describe an architecture.
The spectrumStrategic, high effort and big trade-offs push a decision toward architecture. That tells you who owns it.

Bonus metaphor: a garden

Neglect the garden and weeds grow. Neglect the architecture and the structure decays.
