Event-Driven Architecture: Asynchronous Adventures

Services that react to things that just happened, all at once, without waiting on each other. Event-driven architecture (EDA) is fast, scalable and easy to extend, and also one of the hardest styles to get right.

One cook or four?

A diner order: grilled cheese, fries, milkshake. If one worker makes each item in turn, you wait for the sum of all three. Hire a cook per item and they work at the same time, so you wait only for the slowest. Customers wait less (responsiveness) and the counter can take more orders (scalability).

That's the core idea of EDA: split the processing into services that each respond to an event (something that just happened) and work simultaneously, talking asynchronously through an event channel.

3

Events are not messages

Event

"Something has happened." Broadcast on a topic to anyone interested. The sender doesn't know who listens and never waits (fire and forget). Example: order placed.

Message

"Please do this" or "tell me that". Sent on a queue to exactly one receiver. The sender may wait for a reply. Example: apply payment for order 42.

The data an event carries is its payload: sometimes the whole order, sometimes just an ID that listeners use to look up the rest.

4

Initiating and derived events

An initiating event comes from the outside world (a customer places an order) and starts a business process. Each service that reacts then announces what it did as a derived event, which other services can react to in turn. Fire the initiating event and watch the chain.

5

Is anyone listening?

Publish a derived event for everything a service does, even when nobody listens yet. That's what makes EDA extensible: new features just subscribe. Der Nile wants to know what time of day most emails go out.

6

Asynchronous vs synchronous

Synchronous is a phone call: you wait for an answer. Asynchronous is a text: send it and get on with your day. Architects draw async as a dotted line and sync as a solid one. Placing an order takes 600 ms; applying the payment takes 1,200 ms.

7

Where does the data live?

Async keeps services decoupled, but data drags coupling back in. Share one database and you're coupled to it; give each service its own and they must call each other for data. Compare the three topologies.
