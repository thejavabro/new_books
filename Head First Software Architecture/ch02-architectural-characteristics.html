Architectural Characteristics: Know Your Capabilities

Characteristics are what your system must be good at, beyond what it does. This chapter shows how to spot them, sort them, find hidden ones, and keep the list short.

4

Why you keep the list short

Every characteristic you commit to adds complexity. Three reasons to pick as few as possible:

Impossible to standardise"Performance" in one team is "responsiveness" in another. Agree on a shared vocabulary inside your organisation.
SynergisticThey pull on each other. More security usually means more encryption, which costs performance.
OverabundantNew ones keep appearing. Cloud elasticity barely existed a few years ago.

Feel the synergy

Push one characteristic up and watch the others react. Then watch the complexity bar.

Complexity

Watch out for resume-driven development. Adding a shiny capability because it is fun to build, not because the system needs it, is how overengineering starts.
5

Explicit and implicit

Explicit characteristics are written in the requirements. Implicit ones are never written down but you know they matter. Mail piling up at a front door tells you nobody is home, even though no one said so. Security is the classic implicit one. So is a clean internal structure: no requirements list ever says "please don't make the code a mess".

8

Solutions in disguise: keep asking "why?"

People often bring you a solution and call it a requirement. Your job is to ask "why?" like a curious toddler until the real need appears. The chapter tells the story of a fighter jet that was "required" to fly at Mach 2.5. Asking why revealed the real need: escape from a fight. The resulting jet was slower on paper but far more agile.

Translate business speak

Business people rarely say "elasticity". They say things like "we need to beat competitors to market". Turning those phrases into measurable characteristics is part of your job.

12

Balance, and the magic number seven

Ask stakeholders "which of these do you want?" and they say "all of them". Too few and you rebuild later. Too many and you overengineer. A handy limit is seven, the number of things people comfortably hold in mind (from George Miller's 1956 paper).

Your worksheet for Lafter

Tap to choose up to seven. Tap a chosen one again to star it as a top-three priority, and a third time to remove it.

Take it or leave it

Everything is a trade-off. For each thing the business says, pick the characteristic you would prioritise.
