Project: Build and Publish a Website You Would Use

The Book 2 capstone: a small real website for an idea of your own, planned like HabitTrail, built in committed steps and published to a public address.

Who is it forSite mapWhich chaptersBuildLaunchQuick check
1

Pick a real person and a reason to visit

HabitTrail kept the book focused on one example. The capstone asks for a small site for an idea of your own, built and published for real. A good first site needs no business behind it: just a specific person, a specific reason someone visits, and two to four pages that do that job. Choose the starting point you can picture finished, and prefer one backed by content you already have (real photos, a real short bio, a real list of skills): invented content is much harder to judge.

2

Plan it the way you planned HabitTrail

Name the one or two visitors and the one action each should complete, sketch the site map, write a sentence of content and one checkable success criterion per page. Tick the pages you'd build. Two to four is enough for a first published site; more than four is a wish list, so publish the rest later, once the first version is live and teaching you something.

Scope cut, from the bookA reader finishing a UX course planned four case studies, a blog and a contact form with file uploads. Writing the site map cut it to a home page and one projects page with three finished pieces, plus a mailto link. The big version became a plan for later.
3

Which Book 2 chapters does your idea need?

Recognising which steps your idea can skip is as useful as the building itself. A reader with a three-client dog-walking round skipped the form chapter entirely: a mailto link and a phone number in the footer did the job. Answer three questions about your site.

4

Build in small, committed increments

Initialise Git before you have anything to lose, commit after each working increment, and if a layout change breaks mobile, step back to the last good commit instead of untangling it by hand. Test each page against its success criterion, on a narrow phone width and by keyboard, not just whatever you click first.

5

Publish, then look with a stranger's eyes

Before going public, check every page for placeholder text, a testimonial you meant to replace, or a photo you don't have rights to: search engines may index it within days. Then open the public address on a device you didn't build on. The real test is the book's last question: could you send this address to the actual person it's for without feeling you need to explain what's missing?
