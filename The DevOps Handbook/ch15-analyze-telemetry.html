Analyze Telemetry to Better Anticipate Problems

Raw metrics are only the start. Simple statistics, good alerting and anomaly detection turn telemetry into early warning of problems that haven't hit customers yet.

Case study: finding sick servers at Netflix (2012)

Netflix ($6.2 billion revenue and 75 million subscribers in 2015) runs huge herds of cloud servers that should all look and act the same. Roy Rapoport's team used simple outlier detection: compute what is "normal" right now across the cluster, flag the nodes that don't fit, and remove them from production automatically, without ever defining "proper" behavior. Since the system is built to survive losing nodes, this is safe. Run it on a cluster.

Each number is a node's error rate. Rule used here (illustrative): flag nodes more than three median absolute deviations from the cluster median.

Rapoport says it massively cut both the effort of finding sick servers and the time to fix them, and helped preserve engineers' sanity and work/life balance.

2

Means and standard deviations

The simplest technique: compute a metric's mean and standard deviation and alert when it strays too far. For a Gaussian (bell-shaped) metric, such as unauthorized login attempts per day, 68%, 95% and 99.7% of data fall within one, two and three standard deviations. Alert at 3σ and only about 0.3% of points trigger. No one had to set a static threshold. Move the threshold.

3

Instrument and alert on undesired outcomes

Tom Limoncelli, formerly an SRE at Google, jokes that ideally we'd delete all alerts, then after each user-visible outage ask which indicators would have predicted it, add those, and repeat, until we only have alerts that prevent outages. In practice: review recent severe incidents and list the precursor signals. Match each signal to its level.

Repeating this with ever weaker failure signals finds problems earlier and earlier, with fewer customer-facing incidents.

4

When the data isn't a bell curve

Many operations metrics aren't Gaussian. Nicole Forsgren notes that many follow a chi-square-like shape; using standard deviations then gives over- or under-alerting, or nonsense such as a negative number of downloads three deviations below the mean. Compare the same alert rule on two data sets.

Under-alerting is just as bad: if completed transactions drop 50% mid-day because a component failed, and that is still within 3σ, customers find the problem first.

5

Case study: predictive auto-scaling at Netflix (2012)

Amazon Auto Scaling adds capacity after load rises. Netflix found three problems: instances took 10 to 45 minutes to start, so capacity came too late for spikes; after outages, demand dropped sharply and too much capacity was removed for the recovery surge; and known daily patterns were ignored. Viewing patterns, though not Gaussian, were very predictable. Scryer threw out spurious points with outlier detection, then used fast Fourier transforms and linear regression to forecast demand and pre-scale. Compare.

Within months of using Scryer in production, Netflix improved viewing experience and availability and reduced EC2 costs.

6

Anomaly detection: smoothing and beyond

When data isn't Gaussian we can still find meaningful variances. Smoothing replaces each point with the average of a trailing window, removing short-term noise and showing trends or cycles. Rally Software's Tarun Reddy advises that Ops work with people who know statistics, often found in marketing or business intelligence, since forecasting is their bread and butter. Change the window.

Case study · advanced anomaly detection (2014)

At Monitorama 2014, Toufic Boubez showed transactions per minute on an e-commerce site with weekly periodicity: low weekends, busy weekdays. A 3σ rule alerted only twice and missed a critical Monday drop-off. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, which compares the shape of one window of data with another and assumes no particular distribution, caught it. "Even saying 'Kolmogorov-Smirnov' is a great way to impress everyone," he joked. Non-parametric techniques are great for operations data.
