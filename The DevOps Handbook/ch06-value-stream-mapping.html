Understanding, Visualizing and Expanding the Value Stream

Before improving the work you must see it. Map how an idea becomes running code, protect a transformation team from the daily grind, give it a clear goal and keep planning horizons short.

1

Value stream mapping finds the real problem

Nordstrom's business wanted a mainframe application rewritten: assigning new salespeople to product lines took too long. Courtney Kissler's team first ran a value stream mapping workshop to check whether the COBOL app really was the problem. Try fixing the process yourself before rewriting anything.

Product line assignment request
Processing time

Once the processing time dropped from days to seconds, the demands to move the application off the mainframe disappeared, and other business leaders started bringing their own experiments. Improvements can start small and cheap once you see where the real constraint is.

2

Identify the teams supporting the value stream

In a value stream of any complexity, no single person knows all the work, all its waits, or what each step needs. So the first step is to gather everyone involved. Tap each role.

3

Draw a value stream map in a few hours

The goal is not to document every step but to find where flow is in danger: where work waits weeks or months (environments, change approvals, security reviews) and where rework is created. A first pass is high-level: five to fifteen process blocks, each with lead time, process time and %C/A as rated by the next step. Tune the blocks; anything slow or low quality is flagged.

Then pick one metric to improve, observe and measure that area in more detail, and build a future-state map as the target the team works toward over a set time, say three to twelve months.

4

Create a dedicated transformation team

Transformations clash with day-to-day operations, which are built to preserve the status quo through specialization, efficiency and procedures. Vijay Govindarajan and Chris Trimble (The Other Side of Innovation) recommend a dedicated team that works outside the performance engine that runs daily operations, and is held to a clear, measurable, system-level result. Toggle each choice to see if it helps the team succeed.

5

Agree on a shared goal, then keep horizons short

Define one measurable goal with a deadline six months to two years out, ambitious but achievable, agreed by executives and known to everyone. Limit how many such initiatives run at once. Which of these are good goals?

Then work in short iterations, two to four weeks, like a startup: more flexibility to replan, a shorter delay between effort and visible improvement (which encourages more investment), faster learning, less activation energy, and less risk that the project is cancelled before it shows results.

6

Reserve 20% for non-functional work

Organizations that only pay interest on debt never reduce the principal, and eventually can't even pay the interest. Technical debt works the same way. Marty Cagan's lesson from eBay's near-death experience: product management gives engineering 20% of capacity off the top to rewrite, re-architect or refactor as it sees fit, more if things are already bad. Try different shares.

7

Case study: Operation InVersion at LinkedIn (2011)

LinkedIn ran on Leo, a monolithic Java application. By 2010 about a hundred services ran outside it, but Leo still deployed only every two weeks, often went down and was hard to troubleshoot. Six months after its 2011 IPO, Kevin Scott, VP of Engineering, stopped all feature development for two months so the whole department could fix the core infrastructure. Step through it.

The lessonInVersion paid down almost a decade of debt, but only by sacrificing two months of features promised to public markets. Paying down debt as part of daily work avoids having to make that kind of "near-death" bet.
8

Make work visible and use tools to reinforce behavior

Everyone should know the current state of work, ideally on up-to-date displays. Christopher Little notes that anthropologists define tools as cultural artifacts: tools shape behavior. So Dev and Ops share one work queue and one backlog, prioritizing improvement globally instead of in silo-specific ticket systems. Chat rooms spread information fast and leave a searchable record, though they can also become a stream of interruptions.
