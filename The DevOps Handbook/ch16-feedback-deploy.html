Enable Feedback So Dev and Ops Can Safely Deploy Code

When developers see what their changes do in production, and share the pager, quality goes up. Self-managed services and readiness reviews keep hand-offs safe.

1

Case study: fear of the deploy button at Right Media (2006)

Nick Galbreath, VP of Engineering at Right Media, ran an ad business where inventory changed by the minute, so Development had to deploy quickly. Developers had complained that Ops was afraid to deploy. Given the button themselves, developers became just as afraid. Galbreath saw the same progression in many teams. Step through it and watch deploy frequency and stability move together.

Galbreath, also responsible for security, liked it too: fixes could reach production quickly. It's not enough to automate deployment; production telemetry must be part of every deployment.

2

Use telemetry to make deployments safer

Whoever deploys watches the feature's metrics during and after the change. If something breaks, they fix forward or roll back. Fixing forward can be dangerous, but it is very safe with automated tests, fast deployment and good telemetry. In the book's Etsy example, a deployment spiked PHP runtime warnings, the developer noticed within minutes, and a fix followed. Deploy and decide.

Optimizing for fast recovery (mean time to repair) rather than for never failing (mean time between failures) is a popular DevOps maxim.

3

Dev shares pager rotation duties with Ops

If developers never hear about production problems, defects in their code get prioritized below new features and keep recurring at 2 a.m. for Ops. In 2009 Facebook's Pedro Canahuati put developers, development managers and architects on the pager rotation. Everyone gets visceral feedback on upstream decisions. Toggle who carries the pager.

4

Have developers follow work downstream

User experience design uses contextual inquiry: watch a customer use the product in their own environment. Developers should do the same with internal customers: watch Ops deploy and run their code. Gene Kim describes 2006 as one of the worst moments of his career: a morning spent watching a Tripwire customer perform a tedious operation by hand, after another customer had described a 1,300-step product setup. An anonymous Ops engineer: "most system administrators lasted only six months… we all felt like the developers personally hated us." Pairing application server clusters took six hours.

What changesDevelopers who watch their work downstream find ways to automate complex manual steps and build in operability, and they gain empathy for the people who run what they build.
5

Developers self-manage their service first

Even with Ops liaisons, there may not be enough Ops engineers for every service, especially in big companies. So product teams run their own services in production first, guided by launch guidance that captures the organization's collective experience. Ops only takes over once the service proves it is supportable. Tick the launch-readiness criteria.

Readiness

Ask about compliance too: does the service drive significant revenue (above 5% of a public US company's revenue counts as "significant" for Sarbanes-Oxley)? Does it carry high traffic or high outage costs? Does it store cardholder or personal data? Those answers bring security and regulatory requirements into view early.

7

Case study: launch and handoff readiness reviews at Google (2010)

Google's site reliability engineers (SREs) are functionally organized: all report to Ben Treynor Sloss's organization, but are embedded in product teams that fund them. SREs are scarce, so they go only to the most important services, or those with regulatory requirements, and only if operational burden is low. A team must have self-managed its service in production for at least six months before it can get an SRE. Two safety checks help:

Launch readiness review (LRR)Required before any new service is public and receives live traffic. Self-reported by the product team, with an SRE assigned to help.
Hand-off readiness review (HRR)When a service moves to Ops-managed, usually months after launch. Similar checklist, but far more stringent standards.

Tom Limoncelli: every launch teaches something, and the checklists are how Google keeps that organizational memory. The teams with the fastest HRR approval were those that worked with SREs earliest, from design through launch, and helping product teams early is a cultural norm Google keeps reinforcing.
