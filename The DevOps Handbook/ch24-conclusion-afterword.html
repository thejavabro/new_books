A call to action: breaking the core, chronic conflict

The book ends where it began. Left unmanaged, Development and Operations have a built-in conflict: one is asked to change fast, the other to keep things stable. That conflict drives a downward spiral of slower delivery, worse quality and more firefighting. DevOps principles and patterns break it. Flip between the two worlds.

2

DevOps needs a coalition

New cultural and management norms, technical practices and architecture need a coalition. It spans business leadership, Product Management, Development, QA, IT Operations, Information Security, and even Marketing, where many technology initiatives start. Together they can build a safe system of work, where small teams develop, validate and safely deploy code on their own. Gather your coalition.

Coalition strength-

The authors' call to action applies to any role: find the people around you who want to change how work is done, share the book, and break out of the spiral together. Better still, sponsor and lead these efforts yourself.

3

An organizational imperative

The authors compare DevOps to Lean, which permanently changed manufacturing in the 1980s. They expect organizations that adopt it to beat those that do not, with energized, continually learning teams that out-innovate competitors. So DevOps is an organizational imperative, not just a technical one. It applies to any organization that must increase the flow of planned work while keeping quality, reliability and security for customers.

The "dirty secret"In several case studies, change agents were promoted after their breakthroughs. Later, new leadership arrived, many of those people left, and their changes were rolled back. The authors ask readers not to be cynical. Those people knew the odds were against them, acted anyway, and showed everyone else what is possible. Innovation needs risk-taking, and if no one in management is upset, you are probably not trying hard enough.

The benefit is shared across Dev, Ops, QA, Infosec, product owners and customers. The authors name more joy in building products, fewer death marches, and fewer missed weekends and holidays.

4

Afterword: Nicole Forsgren on productivity and balance

GitHub's 2020 State of the Octoverse found developers working 4.2 to 4.7 more hours a day across the four time zones studied, and pushing ten to seventeen more commits a day to their main branch during the workweek. Microsoft found that high productivity was masking an exhausted workforce. Forsgren warns against bringing back the old ways, such as long days, brute force and adrenaline-driven schedules, now that we have better tools.

Activity counts like hours or commits do not tell the whole story. Her team's SPACE framework has five dimensions, and she suggests measuring across at least three. Build a dashboard below; the example metrics are hers or simple cases of each dimension.
