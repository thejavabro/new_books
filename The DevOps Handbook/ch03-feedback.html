The Second Way: The Principles of Feedback

Failures in complex systems are inevitable. The Second Way builds fast, loud feedback from right to left so that small problems are seen and swarmed before they turn into big ones.

1

Why we need feedback: complex systems

The First Way moves work left to right. The Second Way moves information right to left, so we can build ever safer systems of work. Technology runs on complex systems: no single person can see the whole, and as Charles Perrow found studying Three Mile Island, failures come from components interacting in ways nobody predicted. Sidney Dekker adds that doing the same thing twice may not give the same result. So we can't design failure out with checklists. Instead, Steven Spear describes what lets organizations work safely anyway.

2

Feedback loops, from seconds to months

Peter Senge describes feedback and feed-forward loops as essential to learning organizations. Elisabeth Hendrickson lists six kinds of feedback in software, each answering a different question and taking a different time. Think of them as concentric circles: the closer to the developer, the faster. Tap a ring.

Contrast this with a waterfall project that writes code for a year and only learns about quality in testing, or worse, at release. At GM's Fremont plant, problems were noticed late and worked around; in high-performing plants, every step got fast feedback and problems were fixed on the spot.

3

Swarm problems: the Andon cord

At Toyota, any worker who sees a problem pulls the Andon cord. The team leader comes at once; if the problem isn't solved within the cycle time (say 55 seconds) the line stops and everyone swarms until it is. This looks like letting a local problem disrupt the whole plant, which is exactly the point: it prevents the problem from travelling downstream, prevents new work from adding new errors, and turns each fix into learning. Run the line both ways.

In software, the Andon cord is a failing build or a failing test in the deployment pipeline: we stop introducing new work and swarm until it's green again. That is what makes continuous integration, the "single-piece flow" of software, possible.

4

Case study: a Slack Andon cord at Excella (2018)

Case study · new in the second edition

Excella's team noticed rising cycle times, a case of the "almost dones": people only raised problems at stand-up. They built a Slack bot: typing andon alerted the whole team, who stopped to find a path forward. At first people barely pulled it, afraid of interrupting colleagues. So they redefined it: pull whenever you're stuck, not when you're really stuck. Pulls went up and cycle time came down. Whenever pulls dropped, cycle time crept back.

Shape of the relationship from the case; values illustrative. The team also reported more psychological safety and more creative solutions.

Keep pushing quality closer to the source

After an incident the instinct is to add more inspection and approvals. In complex systems that often makes things worse: the further the decision is from the work, the worse it gets. Which of these controls are effective? Tap to judge.

Tap each control.

Instead, everyone finds and fixes problems in their own area as part of daily work: peer review, automated quality checks run by the developers themselves, and quality, security and reliability treated as everyone's job. Developers learn far more from fixing their own mistakes quickly than from a report weeks later.

6

Optimize for downstream work centers

Lean names two customers: the external customer who pays, and the internal customer who receives our work next. In the 1980s, "design for manufacturability" put the factory floor's needs into product design. In technology the equivalent is designing for operations: non-functional requirements like architecture, performance, stability, testability, configurability and security get the same priority as user features.
