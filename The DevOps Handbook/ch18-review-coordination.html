Create Review and Coordination Processes to Increase Quality

Distant approvals add delay without adding safety. Peer review by people close to the work, small changes and pairing do a better job of catching problems.

Case study: peer review at GitHub (2011)

At GitHub, a pull request is how an engineer tells others about a change, asks for feedback and, in the end, deploys it. Scott Chacon described the practice as GitHub Flow: review and coordination sit inside daily work rather than in a separate approval step. Step through the five moves.

Nobody waits for a meeting. The reviewers are the people who know the code, and the person who merges is the person who deploys and watches the result.

2

The dangers of change approval processes

Knight Capital lost $440 million in a fifteen-minute deployment error. John Allspaw notes that after such an accident two counterfactual stories appear: there should have been more change control, or there should have been more testing. Both sound right. Yet in low-trust, command-and-control cultures these controls often raise the chance of the failure happening again.

The usual reaction to a failure is to add questions to the form, add another approver (now the CIO as well as the VP), or ask for more lead time. Try stacking them up. The numbers are illustrative; the direction follows the chapter.

What the research saysDORA's 2019 report found that a clear, lightweight change process contributes to high performance, while a heavyweight one (an external board or senior manager signing off significant changes) hurts it. The 2014 State of DevOps Report found that high performers relied more on peer review than on external approval.

Change advisory boards still have a coordinating role, and ITIL does not require them to judge every change by hand. A hundred-word summary or a ticked checklist cannot tell anyone whether a change to a complex system will work. As the Toyota principle puts it, the people closest to the problem usually know the most about it.

3

Case study: from six-eye principle to release at scale at Adidas (2020)

Late in 2020, after five bad outages and just before peak season, Adidas put three VPs in a room to approve every change during the two-month peak. The digital business had grown about tenfold; there were over 550 million lines of code and almost two thousand engineers. One of those VPs, Fernando Cornago, admitted they often did not understand the details they were approving.

They asked how to detect interruptions fast, how to fix them fast, and how to keep them out of production. They tracked revenue lost to outages against net sales. A pre-release spreadsheet came first, and teams found it tedious. It was replaced by an automated release fitness dashboard that checks every release from three angles. Strict guidelines plus automated checks and error budgets now tell any developer whether they can deploy. The system regulates itself, and the three VPs are no longer needed in the room.

4

Enable coordination and scheduling of changes

With a loosely coupled architecture, teams can mostly deploy on their own. Even so, hundreds of independent deploys a day can collide. Small teams can announce changes in chat rooms and let the conflicts surface early. Larger or more tightly coupled organizations may need representatives to deliberately schedule changes and sequence them. Some areas, such as core network switch changes, always carry higher risk and need more technical countermeasures, such as redundancy, failover, comprehensive testing and, ideally, simulation.

5

Enable peer review of changes

Instead of an external body, ask a peer who knows the code to review a change, ideally before it reaches trunk. Small batches matter here too. Randy Shoup notes that risk grows non-linearly with the size of a change: going from ten lines to a hundred raises the risk by more than ten times. Giray Özil joked that a programmer will find ten issues in ten lines of code, but will say five hundred lines look fine. Drag the size of the change.

Case study: code reviews at Google (2010)

In 2016 Google's 25,000 developers committed about 16,000 changes to trunk on a typical day, plus 24,000 more from automated systems. Mandatory reviews make this possible. They cover readability against style guides, ownership of code subtrees, and transparency across teams. A chart from Google, reproduced in the book, shows review lead time growing with the size of the change. Randy Shoup once sent a subject matter expert a review of nearly three thousand lines. It took the reviewer days, and he asked Shoup never to do it again.

7

Dangers of more manual testing and change freezes

When failures pile up, a common reaction is to add manual testing or a change freeze. Both make batches bigger. Manual testing is slower, so teams test less often, and the deployment that follows a freeze carries many more changes, which makes it riskier. Fully integrate testing into daily work instead, as part of the smooth, continual flow into production.

8

Enable pair programming to improve all our changes

In pair programming two engineers work at one workstation. One is the driver who types; the other is the navigator who reviews as the work happens. A common variant has one person write the test and the other make it pass. Jeff Atwood called pairing code review on steroids, because neither half of the pair can opt out of understanding the code.

From Dr. Laurie Williams's 2001 study, as cited in the chapter.

Williams found pairs about 15% slower, but error-free code rose from 70% to 85%. Testing and debugging cost far more than writing code, so that trade is impressive. Pairs also weighed more design options, and 96% of respondents enjoyed their work more. Pairing spreads knowledge too, and it helps when teaching or learning new areas.

9

Case study: pair programming replacing broken code review at Pivotal Labs (2011)

Elisabeth Hendrickson saw two accepted review methods at Pivotal: pairing, or Gerrit, where two designated people had to "+1" every commit. Only senior engineers could approve, and they were busy. Reviews often took a week, and meanwhile everyone else's changes kept landing. Compare the two paths.

Pivotal dismantled the Gerrit process and required pairing, which cut review time from weeks to hours. Hendrickson stresses that code review works fine where the culture values reviewing as highly as writing. Where that culture is missing, pairing is a useful interim step.

10

Evaluating the effectiveness of pull request processes

Ryan Tomayko of GitHub judges a pull request by its context, not by its outcome in production. A bad one had only "Fixing issue #3616 and #3841": no one @mentioned, no explanation, none of the author's thinking. A good one explains why the change is needed, how it was made, its risks and the countermeasures, and then prompts discussion. Build one.

Tomayko's favourite example was a long PR for a database migration that still caused an outage. The author then added an apology, the mistaken assumptions and a list of countermeasures, and the thread kept going. Sampling PRs like this, from everyone or from changes linked to incidents, shows whether review is working.

Fearlessly cut bureaucratic processes

Adrian Cockcroft suggested publishing how many meetings and tickets a release requires, and then relentlessly reducing that number. Capital One's "Got Goo?" team and Disney's "Join the Rebellion" program do this kind of obstacle removal. At Target, Heather Mickman kept asking why about the TEAP-LARB technology approval process. Step through it.

Ownership, not permissionA new engineer asked John Allspaw whether a small HTML change was okay to deploy. He answered with questions. Did you get a review? Do you know who the right reviewer is? Are you confident it works in production? Implementers who own the quality of their changes are a sign of a high-trust, generative culture.
