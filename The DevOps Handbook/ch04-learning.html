The Third Way: Continual Learning and Experimentation

The Third Way turns every success and failure into shared knowledge. It needs a high-trust culture, time reserved for improvement and leaders who coach experiments rather than hand out answers.

1

Learning as part of the work

In plants with chronic safety and quality problems, work is rigidly defined, mistakes are punished and suggestions are ignored. In high performers the system of work is dynamic: line workers experiment in daily work, helped by standardized procedures and documented results. The Third Way brings that into technology. The book cites the State of DevOps research: employees in organizations that practice the Third Way were 2.2 times more likely to recommend their team or organization, with higher job satisfaction and less burnout. McKinsey research similarly links culture, psychological safety and continuous improvement to developer velocity.

2

Three cultures, three ways of handling information

Ron Westrum found that a "generative" culture was one of the best predictors of patient safety in healthcare; the State of DevOps research found the same culture predicts software delivery and organizational performance. Read a situation and decide which culture it describes.

3

Name, blame, shame or learn?

When an accident is blamed on "human error", the usual response is to name, blame and shame the person. Sidney Dekker, who coined just culture, warns that responses seen as unjust impede safety investigations, promote fear, and make people hide problems, so problems stay hidden until a catastrophe. Choose a response to an outage and follow the consequences.

Removing blame removes fearBethany Macri, who led Etsy's Morgue tool for recording post-mortems, put it as a chain: remove blame and you remove fear; remove fear and you enable honesty; honesty enables prevention. Steven Spear notes that organizations then become self-diagnosing and self-improving.
4

Institutionalize the improvement of daily work

If teams only cope with problems through workarounds, the workarounds and technical debt pile up until all the team does is cope. Improving the work is more important than doing the work, so we reserve time for paying down debt, fixing defects and refactoring. Try it: choose how much capacity to reserve for improvement and run a year.

Illustrative model: unplanned work grows with unaddressed debt; improvement work shrinks it.

Alcoa and ever-weaker signalsIn 1987 Alcoa injured about 2% of its 90,000 workers each year. CEO Paul O'Neill set a goal of zero injuries and asked to be told of any injury within 24 hours, not to punish but to learn. Over ten years injuries fell by 95%, so the company could start reporting near misses too. Software teams do the same: post-mortems for customer-impacting incidents first, then for team-impacting incidents and near misses.
5

Turn local discoveries into global improvements

Learning that stays with one team is lost to everyone else. The US Navy's nuclear reactor program has over 5,700 reactor-years without a reactor-related casualty; every crew sets out with the collective knowledge of all previous ones and adds its own. In technology, the equivalents are searchable post-mortems, shared source code repositories, and code libraries that encode best practices. Drag to compare how knowledge spreads.

6

Inject resilience patterns into daily work

Low performers buffer against disruption: more inventory, more machines, more people. High performers improve daily operations and deliberately add tension. At one of Toyota supplier Aisin Seiki's mattress factories, with two lines each making 100 units a day, slow days sent all production to one line to find its weak points, knowing the second line was there as a fallback. Technology equivalents: shrinking deployment lead times on purpose, raising test coverage, game days that turn off whole data centers, and injecting faults in production.

7

Leaders reinforce a learning culture

Greatness doesn't come from leaders making all the right decisions. Jim Womack points out that leaders aren't close enough to the work to solve problems, and frontline workers lack the wider context and authority, so they need each other. Strategic goals cascade into short-term target conditions, each framed as an experiment. Mike Rother's coaching kata gives leaders the questions to ask.

Case study: Bell Labs (1925)

Case study · new in the second edition

Bell Labs, behind sound movies, the transistor, Unix and electronic switching, was built in 1925 to consolidate Bell System research. Mervin Kelly pictured an "institute of creative technology" mixing physicists, chemists, engineers and others. The book links it to Brian Eno's scenius, the genius of a whole scene. The aim was turning new knowledge into new things, and failure was not feared: Kelly knew the odds of any new technology succeeding were stacked against it. The lesson the authors draw: cross-skilled collaboration, psychological safety and continuous improvement are not new, and they produce lasting results.
