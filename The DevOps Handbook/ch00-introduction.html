Imagine a World Where Dev and Ops Become DevOps

Most technology organizations are stuck in a downward spiral of fragile systems, broken promises and slower releases. This opening explains why it happens everywhere, what the alternative looks like, and where DevOps came from.

1

Two worlds: the one we have and the one we could have

The book opens with a thought experiment. Imagine product owners, developers, QA, operations and security all working toward one shared goal. Small teams test ideas against real users, deploy many times a day, and find problems in minutes. Now compare that with what most of us live with: long release cycles, late-night firefighting, and work that feels powerless to change. Flip between the two.

2

Each era made delivery faster, cheaper and less risky

Lean manufacturing raised the bar for factories in the 1980s: plants that cut lead times from about six weeks to under three won, and the rest lost market share. Software is going through the same shift. Each era of technology cut the time and cost of delivering a feature by roughly an order of magnitude, and so changed what a failure costs. Pick an era.

3

The core, chronic conflict

IT must do two things at once: respond quickly to a changing market and provide stable, reliable, secure service. Typically Development is measured on the first and Operations on the second. Eliyahu Goldratt called this kind of setup a core, chronic conflict: each silo's incentives block the global goal. Drag the slider to see what happens when one side "wins".

4

The downward spiral in three acts

The conflict feeds a cycle the authors have seen in organizations of every size. Step through it and watch the spiral tighten.

Why it happens everywhereEvery company is now a technology company, so IT failures become business failures. Projects are how organizations fund change, so every promise lands on the same fragile systems. And people stuck in the spiral feel powerless, which the book calls one of the most damaging things a system can do to people: long hours, weekend work, burnout and lost talent.
5

What the spiral costs the world

The authors estimate the waste with a back-of-envelope calculation for 2011. Adjust their assumptions and see how the figure changes. Their own numbers give roughly $2.6 trillion a year of value that could be created, about the size of a large national economy.

6

Breaking the spiral

In the DevOps ideal, small teams deploy independently, tests catch mistakes within minutes, telemetry shows that features work, and big launches are a toggle flip after weeks of dark launching. People fix problems early, run blameless post-mortems, and even inject faults to learn how the system fails. Tap each practice to see what it buys.

7

The evidence: speed and stability together

From 2013 to 2016 the State of DevOps research surveyed more than 25,000 technology professionals. High performers beat low performers on throughput and reliability at the same time, which is direct evidence that the core conflict can be broken. Pick a group.

The 2015 report also looked at deploys per day per developer. As teams grow, low performers deploy less per person, medium performers stay flat and high performers scale linearly. Drag team size.

8

Where DevOps came from

DevOps is not a single invention. It is the convergence of several movements, each applying lessons from manufacturing, safety and leadership to software.

1980sLean and the Toyota Production System: value stream mapping, kanban, lead time as the best predictor of quality and satisfaction, small batches.
2001The Agile Manifesto: deliver working software frequently, in small increments, with motivated, trusted teams.
2006Continuous delivery is first presented at the Agile conference; Jez Humble and David Farley later define the deployment pipeline.
2008Patrick Debois and Andrew Shafer hold a session on Agile infrastructure at the Agile conference in Toronto.
2009John Allspaw and Paul Hammond's "10 Deploys per Day" talk on Dev and Ops at Flickr; the first DevOpsDays in Ghent coins the word; Mike Rother publishes Toyota Kata.
2014–2019State of DevOps research, led by Nicole Forsgren, measures what drives delivery performance.
