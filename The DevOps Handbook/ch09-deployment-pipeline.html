Create the Foundations of Our Deployment Pipeline

Fast flow needs production-like environments that anyone can create on demand, and everything needed to build them kept in version control.

1

Case study: the enterprise data warehouse (2009)

Em Campbell-Pretty ran a large enterprise data warehouse program: ten waterfall streams, all behind schedule. Only one reached user acceptance testing on time, and it then took six more months. After a year of Agile there were only small gains. In a program-wide retrospective she asked what would double productivity. Top of the list, ahead of the usual complaint about business engagement: improve availability of environments.

A new integration and build team made a startling discovery. Compare the environments.

2

Enable on-demand creation of dev, test and production environments

When test environments take a long time, teams test too little and too late, and the environments are often misconfigured or unlike production. Instead of describing production in a document or wiki, build one common mechanism that creates every environment, from a developer's laptop to production. Choose a technique and provision.

Times illustrative.

Ops benefits as much as Dev: automation enforces consistency and removes tedious, error-prone manual work. Developers can run production-like environments on their own machines and find problems long before integration testing.

3

Create a single repository of truth for the whole system

Version control has long been standard for code. To be able to recreate production predictably, even after a catastrophe, much more must be in it. Select everything that belongs in version control.

Why the environment matters more than the codeThe 2014–2019 State of DevOps research found that version control for all production artifacts predicted software delivery performance, which in turn predicted organizational performance, and that putting environment configuration under version control mattered even more than doing it for code. The reason: environments have orders of magnitude more configurable settings than code. We must also be able to recreate pre-production and the build process itself, including compilers and test tools.
4

Make infrastructure easier to rebuild than to repair

Bill Baker of Microsoft: we used to treat servers like pets, named and nursed back to health. Now they are cattle: numbered, and replaced when sick. Click servers to break them, then try both approaches.

Every production change (configuration, patches, upgrades) must flow through the automated system so it reaches every environment and is recorded. Some organizations disable remote logins to production, or routinely kill and replace instances so manual changes can't survive. At Netflix, the book notes, the average AWS instance was 24 days old, and 60% were under a week old. Taken to its conclusion, this is sometimes called immutable infrastructure: no manual changes to production at all.

5

Case study: $30 billion of hotel revenue in containers (2020)

Dwayne Holmes and a small team of three developers and three infrastructure engineers containerized all the revenue-generating systems of one of the largest hotel companies, supporting over $30 billion in annual revenue and more than 3,000 developers. For him containers deliver three things: abstraction (the "dial tone": pick up the phone and it works), specialization (Ops builds containers developers reuse) and automation (rebuild again and again and it just works). Drag through the years.

The longer the interval between integration and deployment, the worse the surprises. So expand the definition of done: at the end of each iteration, code is integrated, tested, working and potentially shippable, demonstrated in a production-like environment. By the end of the project we've deployed hundreds or thousands of times, with the same monitoring, logging and deployment tools we use in production. Build your team's definition.
