Create Telemetry to Enable Seeing and Solving Problems

You can't fix what you can't see. Build telemetry into applications, environments and the pipeline so that problems are found by data, not by customers or guesswork.

1

Don't just reboot it

Things go wrong in production. Without information, the common rule of thumb is to reboot the server, and if that fails, reboot something else. A 2001 Microsoft Operations Framework study found that organizations with the best service levels rebooted servers twenty times less often and had five times fewer "blue screens of death". They diagnosed and fixed causes, what The Visible Ops Handbook calls a culture of causality. That needs telemetry: an automated communications process by which measurements are collected and sent to receivers for monitoring.

An incident: checkout latency is spiking.

Research backs it up: in the 2015 State of DevOps Report high performers restored service 168 times faster than their peers; in DORA's 2019 report elite performers were 2,604 times faster than low performers, minutes versus weeks.

2

Case study: the Church of Graphs at Etsy (2012)

From 2009 Etsy standardized its whole stack on LAMP, changing critical infrastructure that customers should never notice. To keep that safe, developers added telemetry to their features as part of daily work. Ian Malpass: if engineering at Etsy has a religion, it's the Church of Graphs; if it moves, they track it, and the key is making tracking anything easy.

Production metrics tracked. In 2011 the top thirty business metrics were prominent on the "deploy dashboard".

3

Create a centralized telemetry infrastructure

For decades, Dev logged what developers found interesting and Ops monitored whether environments were up. When something broke, nobody could see why. James Turnbull's The Art of Monitoring describes a modern architecture. Follow an event through it.

Centralized logs can be turned into metrics by counting them: a log line like "child pid exited on signal 11" becomes a segfault count, so it can be graphed, trended and checked for anomalies. The deployment pipeline sends telemetry too: test results, build status, deployment events. Everything should be accessible through APIs and easy to add and query.

4

Application logging that helps production

Every feature should be instrumented: if it was important enough to build, it's important enough to measure. Logging levels tell readers how much to care. Dan North's test for ERROR versus WARN: imagine being woken up at 4 a.m. Low printer toner is not an ERROR. Assign each message a level.

Also log everything significant for security and operations: authentication and authorization decisions, system and data access, privileged changes, data changes, invalid input (possible injection attacks), resource limits, health and availability, startups and shutdowns, faults, circuit-breaker trips, delays, and backup success or failure. Hierarchical categories, such as performance or security, make them easier to interpret.

5

Use telemetry to guide problem solving

When blame surrounds outages, people avoid documenting changes and hide telemetry, which makes problems harder to solve. Telemetry lets us use the scientific method instead. Step through the four questions.

6

Make creating metrics part of daily work

Etsy open-sourced StatsD so that one line of code creates a counter or timer, sent to Graphite for graphing. Overlay deployments on the graphs, since most problems follow changes. The newer OpenTelemetry standard lets collectors talk to any storage and processing back end. Click "log in" to emit a metric.

// one line per event
StatsD.increment("login.successes");
StatsD.increment("login.failures");
StatsD.timing("login.duration_ms", ms);
7

Self-service telemetry and information radiators

Telemetry should be fast and easy for anyone to get, giving everyone a common view of reality. Put it on displays where Dev and Ops work: an information radiator. Alistair Cockburn's two reasons: the team has nothing to hide from visitors, and nothing to hide from itself. Ernest Mueller starts at new organizations by using radiators to show issues and changes; some companies even publish status to customers.

Case study · LinkedIn (2011)

In 2010 LinkedIn had huge volumes of telemetry that engineers couldn't reach: getting CPU usage for all hosts of one service meant filing a ticket. Intern Eric Wong built InGraphs over a summer: self-service graphs, calculations across datasets, week-over-week trends, custom dashboards. Once, LinkedIn spotted a major web-mail provider's problem in its graphs and told them before the provider knew. The real-time graphs ended up on screens throughout engineering offices.

8

Find and fill telemetry gaps at every level

We need enough telemetry, at every level, to see the health of everything the service relies on, and ideally to spot problems before customers do. Rate your coverage at each level.

Business metrics should follow the customer acquisition funnel (sign-ups, add to cart, checkout) and be actionable, not vanity numbers. Infrastructure metrics should be organized by service and linked through service discovery tools (for example ZooKeeper, etcd or Consul) so it is clear which infrastructure supports which service. Pre-production environments need telemetry too, so problems are visible before production.
