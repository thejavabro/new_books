Integrate Hypothesis-Driven Development and A/B Testing

Most ideas fail to improve the metrics they target. So treat each feature as a hypothesis, test it with real users and keep only what works.

1

Should we build it, and why?

Developers often work on features for months or years, across several releases, without ever confirming that the business goals are being met. Even when a feature fails, fixing it loses out to the next new feature. So before building, ask "should we build it, and why?", then run the cheapest, fastest experiment that could confirm, with real users, that the feature will achieve the intended outcome.

2

Case study: hypothesis-driven development at Intuit (2012)

Intuit founder Scott Cook wanted every employee able to run rapid experiments. The TurboTax consumer division built a culture of "rampant innovation" in 2010 and went on to run 165 experiments during the three months of the US tax season. The website's conversion rate rose 50%, and team members loved that their ideas reached the market. Remarkably, they experimented during peak season, when traffic and learning value are highest; waiting until the day after the filing deadline would mean waiting a year. Drag the experiment rate.

Illustrative model: about one in three experiments helps, a third do nothing, a third hurt and are rolled back.

3

A brief history of A/B testing

A/B testing grew out of direct response marketing. Before email, each test meant designing and printing offers, mailing thousands of postcards, and waiting weeks for replies, at tens of thousands of dollars per trial. It paid off when conversion rose, say from 3% to 12%. It has been used in campaign fundraising, internet marketing, the Lean Startup and even by the British government to find which letters best collected overdue tax. Online, visitors are randomly shown a control (A) or a treatment (B), and statistics on their behavior establish a causal link.

4

Two-thirds of good ideas don't work

Ronny Kohavi, who led Microsoft's experimentation group, found that of well-designed experiments meant to improve a key metric, only about one-third succeeded. The rest had negligible or negative effect, and all had seemed like reasonable ideas. Jez Humble jokes that taken to an extreme, the company would have done better giving the team a vacation than building such features. Try guessing.

Outcomes are randomly drawn in Kohavi's one-third proportions: the point is that intuition is a poor predictor.

5

Integrate A/B testing into releases and planning

Fast A/B testing needs on-demand deployment and feature toggles that decide which share of users sees the treatment, plus telemetry on each cohort. Etsy built its own experimentation framework because features too often launched without anyone knowing whether they worked. Then product owners must treat each feature as a hypothesis. Write one in the book's template.

Work is broken into small units and each is validated; if an outcome isn't met, the roadmap changes.

6

Case study: doubling revenue at Yahoo! Answers (2010)

Yahoo! Answers had about 140 million monthly visitors and 20 million active users in over twenty languages, but growth and revenue had flattened and engagement was declining. Jim Stoneham saw it as a social game, and noted that Twitter, Facebook and Zynga ran experiments at least twice a week. If experiments aren't frequent, metrics talk gives way to shipping features. Answers could release only once every four weeks, a feedback loop ten times slower than competitors'. It moved to weekly and then several deployments a week. Compare.
