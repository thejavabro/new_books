Selecting Which Value Stream to Start With

A transformation has to start somewhere. Pick a value stream where success is possible and visible, find the innovators who want to try, and expand the coalition from there.

1

Pick your first battles carefully

Choosing where to start decides not only how hard the transformation is but who gets involved, how teams are organized and how people will see the effort. Michael Rembetsy, who helped lead Etsy's transformation, warned that an organization in trouble doesn't get many shots, so pick improvement projects carefully and protect them.

Case study · Nordstrom (2014–2015)

A 2011 board meeting at Nordstrom concluded that online revenue growth was a strategic priority. Courtney Kissler's team didn't want to upend everything, so they picked three areas with clear business problems. For the mobile app, the need was speed: releases twice a year frustrated customers. They moved to continuous planning, a single prioritized backlog, and testing as part of daily work; they doubled features delivered per month and halved defects. For the in-store Café Bistro systems, the need was cost and quality: eleven restaurant re-concepts in 2013 caused incidents, and 44 were planned for 2014. A business leader suggested tripling the team; Kissler proposed fixing the work instead. Focusing on intake and deployment cut deployment lead times by 60% and production incidents by 60 to 90%.

2

Greenfield or brownfield?

A greenfield project starts from scratch, with no legacy code or processes; it is easy to show early wins but is usually a pilot. A brownfield service already serves customers, often for years, and carries technical debt. Many assume DevOps is for greenfield only, yet over 60% of transformation stories at the 2014 DevOps Enterprise Summit were brownfield. The State of DevOps research found the age of an application and its technology did not predict performance; what mattered was whether it was (or could be) architected for testability and deployability.

Case study: Kessel Run and mid-air refueling (2020)

After a 2015 tragedy in which US forces struck a hospital in Afghanistan, investigators traced part of the cause to outdated, manual planning systems. A group inside the US Air Force formed Kessel Run, named after Han Solo's smuggling route, to "smuggle" modern ways of working into the Department of Defense. Air Operation Centers still planned with whiteboards and spreadsheets; one search found 2.8 million Excel and PowerPoint files on one server. They started with a single process: tanker planning for mid-air refueling. Step through their iterations.

4

Systems of record and systems of engagement

Gartner's "bimodal IT" splits systems into systems of record (ERP, HR, finance: slower change, regulated, "doing it right") and systems of engagement (customer-facing: fast change and experiments, "doing it fast"). The book warns against using this split as an excuse. Both kinds need speed and safety, and because systems depend on each other, the whole is only as fast as its most fragile part. Start with the most sympathetic and innovative groups

Geoffrey Moore's Crossing the Chasm shows how new ideas spread: innovators and early adopters embrace them, conservative groups resist. Early on, don't spend energy converting skeptics. Build successes with the willing and expand from there. Step through the sequence.

Case study: scaling DevOps at American Airlines, part 2 (2020)

By year three, American Airlines realized DevOps was not just an IT thing. To scale "deliver value faster" they used four pillars: delivery excellence (how we work), operating excellence (how we're structured and funded), people excellence (talent, culture, leadership behaviors) and technology excellence (modernization, automation, cloud). Culture rested on passion, selflessness and accountability. They also changed the vocabulary. Tap an old question to see what replaced it.

CEO Doug Parker said what made him proudest was that the champions of the delivery transformation were now business leaders, not IT.

Case study: HMRC's platform and the COVID-19 support schemes (2020)

Case study · new in the second edition

In 2020 the UK tax authority had weeks to build services to distribute pandemic support to millions of people, with nobody able to say how many. All services launched on time, most a week or two early, with a 94% user satisfaction rating. The secret was years of investment in a platform, the Multichannel Digital Tax Platform: about 1,200 microservices, more than 2,000 people in 70 teams, around 100 production deployments a day. It deliberately limits technology choices ("opinionated"), abstracts infrastructure away from teams, and supplies pipelines and reusable components, so teams could "just get on with it". During the crisis a platform engineer was embedded in each COVID-19 team.

Little fish in little pondsThe chapter closes on Peter Drucker's line: little fish learn to be big fish in little ponds. Start where you can win, protect the effort, and earn the right to expand.
