Protecting the Deployment Pipeline

Change management and compliance can be served better by an automated pipeline than by paperwork. Here is how to keep auditors and regulators happy while deploying often.

1

Integrate security and compliance into change approval

Any sizeable IT organization already has change management, and that is the main control for operations and security risk. Compliance officers and auditors want to see it work. ITIL describes three kinds of change. Sort these examples, then check the answers.

StandardLower risk, follows an approved process, can be pre-approved. Deploys can be fully automated but are still logged for traceability.
NormalHigher risk; needs the agreed change authority. Often the CAB, which may not have the expertise to judge the change, and lead times suffer.
UrgentAn emergency, such as a security patch or restoring service. Senior approval; paperwork can follow. DevOps aims to make the normal process fit emergencies too.
2

Recategorize lower-risk changes as standard changes

A reliable pipeline earns a reputation for undramatic deployments. Use it to argue with change management that your deployments are low risk enough to count as standard. The evidence: months or quarters of change history, with the full list of production issues, high change success rates and low mean time to repair (MTTR). Move the sliders to build the case.

Standard changes still go into the change system, such as Remedy or ServiceNow, ideally recorded automatically by the configuration management and deployment tools. Putting planning-tool ticket numbers (JIRA, Rally, LeanKit) in commit comments lets anyone trace a deployment back through version control to features, defects, incidents or user stories, without loading engineers with paperwork.

3

What to do when changes are normal changes

Changes that cannot be standard need approval from at least some of the CAB. Make the request for change (RFC) complete and accurate, ideally generated automatically. Fill one in by switching on the automation.

Many toolchains can do this compliantly by tagging every commit with a ticket number. The CAB then reviews it as usual, but with the evidence in front of them. If all goes well, the change authorities will appreciate how thorough and detailed it is.

4

Case study: automated infrastructure changes as standard changes at Salesforce.com (2012)

Salesforce was releasing less as it grew. It shipped four major customer releases in 2006, and only one in 2007 despite hiring more engineers. Batches grew and deployments got worse. A multiyear transformation began in 2009. By 2013, according to Dave Mangot and Reena Mathew, deployment lead time had fallen from six days to five minutes, and the company could process over a billion transactions a day.

Quality became everyone's job, with automated testing in every stage. The team practised destructive testing, raising load until a service broke, to learn its failure modes. Infosec worked with Quality Engineering from the start. The payoff: change management agreed that infrastructure changes made through Puppet would be standard changes, needing far fewer approvals or none. Manual changes still needed approval, which gave everyone another reason to automate.

5

Implement separation of duty through code review

For decades, separation of duty has been a key control against fraud and mistakes: a code librarian approves the developer's change, then Operations promotes it. Some examples are uncontroversial, such as administrators who can read logs but not delete them. When deployments were annual, handoffs were workable. With frequent, complex deployments, engineers need to see outcomes fast, and handoffs slow that feedback down. Prefer controls such as pair programming, continuous inspection of check-ins and code review. Where separation of duty is required, they can show equivalent outcomes.

6

Case study: PCI compliance and a cautionary tale of separating duties at Etsy (2014)

Bill Massie managed ICHT ("I Can Haz Tokens"), Etsy's payment application that tokenizes card data and talks to the payment processor. PCI DSS scope covers the people, processes and technology that touch cardholder data, plus anything connected. So ICHT was separated physically and logically, with its own team, which kept the rest of Etsy out of scope. Toggle the boundary.

To meet the code review requirement, Massie became the change approver: changes were flagged in JIRA, and he reviewed, approved and deployed them by hand. Etsy got its Report of Compliance. But the team became compartmentalized. Nobody could be a full-stack engineer, and Massie described fear and reluctance about deploying, since no one could see beyond their part of the stack. Compliance is possible with DevOps, but separation of duty can erode the very virtues that made the rest of Etsy work.

7

Case study: ten no-fear releases per day at Capital One (2020)

After years of moving to Agile, insourcing, microservices and the cloud, Capital One still ran its card servicing platform, serving tens of millions of customers, on an ageing mainframe vendor product. Rakesh Goyal said it had been patched until the teams running it were as big as the product. Tap the principles and moves.

The pipeline blocked releases that did not meet regulatory controls, and at the peak twenty-five teams worked on the platform at the same time.

8

Ensure documentation and proof for auditors and compliance officers

Bill Shinn of AWS points out a gap: few auditors read code, and few developers have read NIST 800-37 or the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act. Auditors were trained to take, say, a thousand of ten thousand servers as a sample, with screenshots as evidence. That breaks down when infrastructure is code and auto-scaling servers come and go all the time. His teams design controls with auditors, one control per sprint, and send everything to telemetry such as Splunk or Kibana so auditors can find evidence themselves. Compare the two.

Case study: relying on production telemetry for ATM systems (2013)

A bank's DevOps leader, given the pseudonym Mary Smith, described a developer who planted a backdoor in ATM code that put machines into maintenance mode so cash could be taken. Code review did not catch it. Separation of duty and change approval were in place. Telemetry caught it: in an operations review someone noticed a city's ATMs entering maintenance mode at unscheduled times, before the scheduled cash audit. Run the week and spot it.

Day 0

The lesson: auditors who rely heavily on code review and separation of duty can miss things. Production telemetry is a powerful detective control.
