Designing Organization and Architecture with Conway's Law in Mind

Systems copy the communication structure of the organizations that build them. So design teams and architecture together: small, long-lived, market-oriented teams owning loosely coupled services.

1

Organizations ship their org chart

In 1968 Melvin Conway observed that organizations which design systems are constrained to produce designs that copy their communication structures. Eric Raymond's shorter version: if you have four groups working on a compiler, you'll get a 4-pass compiler. Change the number of teams and watch the architecture follow.

2

Case study: Conway's Law at Etsy (2015)

Etsy had two teams, developers and DBAs. In 2007 it built Sprouter ("stored procedure router") between the PHP front end and the Postgres database to keep them apart. Instead of two layers, every business-logic change now touched three, owned by different teams. Switch between before and after.

3

Functional, matrix and market orientation

Decision science names three structures. Pick one to see how work flows through it.

Functional orientation can work: Etsy, Google and GitHub, and Toyota itself, ran high-performing functional organizations. What they share is a high-trust culture, transparent priorities and enough slack for urgent work. As Mike Rother writes in Toyota Kata, you can't reorganize your way to continuous improvement; what matters is how people act and react. To get market orientation, don't do a big top-down reorg: embed functional engineers and skills (Ops, QA, Infosec) into each service team, or give teams self-service platforms.

4

Testing, operations and security are everyone's job

In high performers, quality, availability and security aren't one department's job. Ticketmaster's CTO Jody Mulkey used to see Dev and Ops as offense and defense on a football field; now he sees Ops as the offensive linemen who make it possible for Dev to move the ball. In 2009, Facebook had painful deployments, so all engineers, managers and architects rotated through on-call for the services they built, giving everyone visceral feedback on upstream decisions.

Generalists over silos

Over-specialized departments behave like "sovereign states" (Steven Spear), so every complex task needs many handoffs. Encourage every engineer to grow broad skills. Pick a shape.

CSG's Scott Prugh notes managers object that "I can hire two server administrators for every multi-skilled operations engineer", but the gains in flow far outweigh it, and cross-training makes work more fun. Valuing people only for their current skills reinforces a fixed mindset; valuing learning builds a growth mindset.

5

Fund services and products, not projects

Project funding assembles a team, ships, then disbands it, so nobody sees the long-term consequences. Funding stable service teams lets them own their strategy and roadmap and measure success by outcomes (revenue, customer lifetime value, adoption) rather than by on-time, on-budget delivery. As one IT leader quipped, every new application is like a free puppy: the upfront cost isn't what gets you.

6

Design team boundaries with loosely coupled architecture

In a tightly coupled system, small changes can cause large failures, so every change needs coordination with everyone else and integration testing with thousands of other changes. Service-oriented architectures with bounded contexts (from Eric Evans's Domain-Driven Design) let a team understand and change its service without knowing the internals of its peers, interacting only through APIs. Randy Shoup notes that organizations like Google and Amazon have incredible flexibility and scalability this way. Toggle the coupling and press "change service B".

7

Keep teams small: the two-pizza rule

From 2002, Amazon kept teams small enough to be fed by two pizzas, about five to ten people. Communication paths grow roughly with the square of team size. Drag to feel it.

8

Team Topologies: four team types

New in this edition: Matthew Skelton and Manuel Pais's Team Topologies. They suggest about eight people per team (with 150, Dunbar's number, as an upper limit for a group), keeping teams together at least a year since trust takes around three months to build, and treating inter-team demands as costly. Tap the four types.

9

Case study: API Enablement at Target (2015)

Case study

Target's core data (inventory, pricing, stores) was locked in legacy systems and mainframes, often with several sources of truth. A new team needed three to six months just to build integrations to get data. In 2012 Heather Mickman led an API Enablement team, hiring engineers rather than contractors, to let teams deliver in days instead of months.

Monthly API calls served. Also: 53 new business capabilities in two years (including Ship to Store and Gift Registry), 90 APIs, about 80 deployments a week, and digital sales up 42% in the 2014 holiday season.
