The First Way: The Principles of Flow

Work moves left to right, from idea to customer. The First Way is about making that movement fast and smooth by seeing the work, doing less of it at once, and fixing whatever slows it most.

1

Make our work visible

In a factory you can see inventory pile up in front of a slow machine. In technology work, a ticket can be reassigned with one click, so work bounces between teams or waits silently. A kanban board spanning the whole value stream makes it visible. Work only counts as done when it is running in production. Click cards on the board to pull them right; the dashed ones are "invisible" work that arrived by email and chat.

2

Limit work in process

Interrupting a technology worker is easy and its cost is invisible, yet every switch means rebuilding context. A WIP limit caps the cards per column: when the test lane already holds three, no new card enters until one leaves. Dominica DeGrandis notes queue size is one of the few leading indicators of lead time. Run the simulation with different numbers of projects per engineer.

3

Reduce batch sizes: the envelope game

The book borrows a simulation from Lean Thinking: ten brochures, four steps each (fold, insert, seal, stamp), ten seconds per step. In the large batch you fold all ten, then insert all ten, and so on. In single-piece flow you finish one envelope before starting the next. Run both.

t = 0 s

In software the equivalent of single-piece flow is continuous deployment: each change committed to version control is integrated, tested and deployed. An annual release is the large batch: huge WIP, massive disruption downstream, and problems found late.

4

Reduce the number of handoffs

A deployment that takes months may involve hundreds of steps across many teams: network, admins, storage, security, release managers. Each handoff needs requesting, specifying, scheduling and verifying, and each is a queue. Some context is lost every time. Add handoffs and watch both the wait and the context leak.

5

Continually identify and elevate the constraint

Goldratt: any improvement not made at the constraint is an illusion. Improve a step before the bottleneck and work piles up in front of it; improve one after and it starves. Click a station to double its capacity and see if throughput moves.

The five focusing steps

  1. Identify the system's constraint.
  2. Decide how to exploit it.
  3. Subordinate everything else to that decision.
  4. Elevate the constraint.
  5. If it has been broken, go back to step one, and don't let inertia become the new constraint.

6

Eliminate hardship and waste

Shigeo Shingo saw waste as the biggest threat to a business. Modern Lean reframes it as reducing hardship and drudgery. Mary and Tom Poppendieck listed software's wastes; Damon Edwards added two more. Sort each everyday example into its category.

7

Case study: flow and constraints in a hospital (2021)

Case study · new in the second edition

Around 2007 a hospital's emergency department was boarding patients for hours or days, and was on ambulance diversion about 60 hours a month. Nurses quit. A flow committee, guided by Goldratt's The Goal, treated the hospital as one system rather than separate departments. Within a year diversion fell to about 45 minutes a month.

The lessons from the doctor who told the story: flow must matter to leaders, who create bandwidth by deciding what can wait; break down silos and look at flow through the whole system; never accept "no" as the final word; measure and reward what serves the overall goal; and remember that how work is set up is a human-made constraint, not a law of physics.
