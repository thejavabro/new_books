Staying technical, without being the bottleneck

Once you manage a whole team, you'll write much less code. You still need to stay technical enough to spot problems, judge estimates and earn respect. The trick is where your code lands: off the critical path. If a feature can't ship until the manager finds time, the manager is now the slowest part of the team.

Meetings and interruptions will always win over your coding time, so the whole release waits on you.

Pick useful work nobody is blocked on: small bugs, tooling, prototypes, reviews. You stay close to the code and the team never waits.

Debugging a dysfunctional team

Treat an unhappy team like a buggy system: find the symptom, form a hypothesis, fix the root cause. Four common symptoms:

Symptom:

Deadlines slide; little gets released. People feel good when they set small goals and hit them.

Look for:

Painful, infrequent releases, manual testing, features too big to ship in pieces. Push to remove those bottlenecks, and roll up your sleeves where the work is slipping.

Symptom:

Gossip, negativity, us-versus-them. Sometimes caused by a "brilliant jerk" everyone thinks is irreplaceable.

Fix:

Act quickly. Give clear, prompt feedback with examples. Sometimes the right outcome is helping the person leave on good terms.

Symptom:

Long hours and burnout.

Fix:

If it's unstable systems, slow the roadmap and measure alerts and incidents until they drop. If it's a real crunch, be there, help, cheer, give explicit rest afterward, then learn so it doesn't recur.

Symptom:

Friction with product, design or another team, or within the team.

Fix:

Regular touch-bases with peers, gather actionable feedback, never undermine peers in front of your team, and create non-work time together.

Why release frequency changes mood

When releases are rare, they become a scarce resource. Everyone crams changes into the one window, tests break, and people fight over it. Toggle the release cadence.

3

The shield

Managers are told to shield the team from the chaos above. Some shielding is good: the team needs focus. But a manager who hides everything leaves the team unprepared and surprised. The aim is a filter, not a wall.

Every rumour and reprioritization lands on the team. Constant distraction and anxiety.

Calm, until a change arrives that they had no hint of. They also never learn how the business works, which stunts their growth.

You absorb the noise but pass on the context that matters, with your take on what it means for them. They stay focused and informed.

How to drive good decisions

Five habits keep a team's decisions grounded. Hover or tap each node.

Create a data-driven culture.

Measure what your systems and products do. Ask "how will we know?" before building, so opinions can be tested.

Flex your product muscles.

Understand the customer and the business case. Engineers who understand why can suggest cheaper ways to get the same value.

Look into the future.

What will this system need in a year? Spot upcoming scaling or maintenance problems and plan for them before they're emergencies.

Review the outcome of decisions.

After a project, check whether it did what you hoped. Did the metric move? This is how judgement improves.

Run retrospectives on process.

Regularly ask what's working and what isn't in how you work day to day, and actually change something.

Conflict avoider vs. conflict tamer

The conflict avoider wants everyone to agree, so issues fester and decisions stall. The conflict tamer doesn't love conflict either, but deals with it openly and builds processes that make disagreement productive. Sort each habit.

Plan a quarter that holds

Some rules of thumb for the bigger picture, built into a calculator. A quarter has 13 weeks, but after holidays, meetings, outages and onboarding, plan on about 10 productive weeks per engineer, fewer in a holiday-heavy quarter. Then reserve 20% for sustaining work: tests, cleanup, upgrades. And for a quick estimate, double your guess.
