Your time: what's important, anyway?

Managing several teams, usually through tech leads or new managers, means you can no longer write code regularly or be in every meeting. Your calendar fills up by itself. The question changes from "how do I do all this?" to "what actually needs me?" Compare a week before and after deliberate delegation.

Everything routes through you. Strategy, hiring and developing leaders get squeezed out by the urgent. And skipping 1-1s to cope is how you miss the person about to quit.

Routine work moves to tech leads and senior engineers. You keep 1-1s, and spend real time on the things only you can do: hiring, strategy, and growing the next layer of leaders.

Strategies for saying no

As you manage more, more requests arrive than your teams can handle. Saying no well is a core skill. Six techniques:

Agree to the goal, add the cost.

"Yes, we can do that, and it means the payments migration slips a month." The other person sees the tradeoff instead of a wall.

Turn repeated asks into rules.

If the same kind of request keeps coming, write a policy (e.g. "code freezes need two weeks' notice"). Then the policy says no, not you.

Make them a partner.

"Help me say yes: what could we drop, or what data would justify the extra people?" Shifts the conversation from conflict to problem solving.

Point to the team's capacity.

Show the plan and the available engineer-weeks. A concrete budget makes the constraint real, not personal.

Decide with your peers.

Bring tough calls to your peer managers or leadership group, so the no is a shared decision with a shared story.

Say it clearly and early.

A vague "maybe later" that is really a no wastes everyone's time and burns trust. A quick, kind no is better.

A sales lead asks you, for the fourth time this quarter, to build a one-off feature for a big client. Which move fits best?

Measuring the health of a team

Your job now includes technical elements beyond code: tools, processes and systems that let teams move. Three signals show a team's health from its systems, without asking anyone. Pick a team.

Releases / week
Check-ins / dev / day
Incidents / week

Fictional teams. Frequent releases signal small, well-broken-down work and good tooling. Frequent check-ins signal people aren't stuck. Incidents show whether speed is costing stability.

Us versus them, or team player?

Rallying your team against a common enemy (another team, product, "management") builds loyalty fast. It also poisons the organization. Your peers are your team too.

Us versus them

  • Blames other teams in front of their own
  • Hoards information and headcount
  • Wins locally, damages the whole
  • Team learns to distrust everyone else

Team player

  • Treats peer managers as their first team
  • Raises disagreements privately with peers
  • Shares people and credit where it helps the company
  • Models trust across team boundaries
