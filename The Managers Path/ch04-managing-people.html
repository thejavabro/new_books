Starting a reporting relationship right

The first weeks with a new report set the tone for everything after. Build trust and rapport, give them a plan, and tell them how you work. A 30/60/90-day plan gives a new hire clear, growing goals. Click each milestone.

First month: get set up and learn.

Dev environment working, first small change shipped, meet the people they'll work with, and read the key systems docs. Update the onboarding docs with anything that was wrong.

Second month: deliver something real.

Take a meaningful piece of a project from start to finish with support. Participate in on-call or code review in earnest.

Third month: add their perspective.

Contribute to planning and design, suggest improvements, and have a picture of where they want to grow.

Regular 1-1s: what they cost and what they buy

Weekly 1-1s are the default. They cost real time, which is why managers skip them, and skipping is how you miss the signs that someone is about to quit. See what your schedule costs.

3

Five styles of 1-1

There is no one right format. Each style suits different people and moments. Most good 1-1 relationships mix several.

Work through a shared list.

Both sides add items to a running doc. Efficient and leaves a record. Risk: it becomes purely transactional, so leave a few minutes for the human side.

They bring whatever is on their mind.

Unstructured, good for venting, ideas and things that don't fit a list. Some people need prompting to open up; ask open questions.

Set time aside for feedback and growth.

Every so often, step back from day to day work. Discuss career goals, give coaching feedback, and ask for feedback on yourself.

Status of the work.

Appropriate when the report is a manager or lead running a project you oversee. For most ICs, status belongs in writing.

Learn who they are.

Especially early: what do they enjoy, how do they like praise, what are their goals, what has worked with past managers? This is the foundation of trust.

4

Micromanager or delegator?

The micromanager digs into every detail. The pure delegator hands work off and disappears until it's late. Good managers adjust how deep they go to the stage of the project and use the team's goals to decide which details matter. Pick a stage, then set your involvement.

More practical advice for delegating well
  • Use the team's goals to decide which details you need. If a detail doesn't affect a goal you own, let it go.
  • Check the systems before the people. Look at tickets, commits and dashboards first so your questions are informed, not interruptions.
  • Establish standards for code and systems so that you don't have to review every decision personally.
  • Treat bad news neutrally or positively. If people get punished for sharing problems, they'll stop sharing them, and you'll be pushed into micromanaging to find out.

6

Writing and delivering a performance review

Give yourself time.

A good review can't be written the night before. Block time weeks ahead and look back over the whole period, not just the last month.

Gather input.

Read their self-review and peer feedback. Pull in the projects they shipped and the notes you kept from 1-1s.

Write with specific examples.

Recognize accomplishments concretely. Pick a small number of growth areas, each with examples and a sense of what "better" looks like.

Deliver it with nothing new.

If you've been giving continuous feedback, the review is a summary. Share it ahead of the conversation so they can digest it, then talk about the future.

7

Cultivating careers, and firing when you must

Growing people means advocating for them, giving stretch work and helping them build a case for promotion. The hard counterpart is dealing with someone who isn't performing. Most managers wait far too long, and the whole team pays for it.

Clear feedback, a documented plan, then follow through.

Say plainly what's wrong and what good looks like. If it doesn't change, put a written improvement plan in place with HR. If that fails, let them go respectfully. Morale dips briefly, then recovers: the team sees that standards mean something.

Avoidance compounds.

Others pick up the slack and resent it. Your best people notice that low performance has no consequence. By the time you act, the damage is spread across the team.
