One more layer of abstraction

Managing managers looks like managing several teams, only bigger. It isn't. Now everything reaches you through another person, often after the fact, and you can no longer know every engineer well. Many good team managers struggle here and fall back to what's comfortable: coding, or doing their managers' project management for them. The fix is discipline: follow up on the little things until you learn which ones you can stop following up on.

Information is filtered twice before it reaches you. Problems surface late, often through someone quitting.

Skip-level meetings give you a direct read on how teams are doing and how their managers are doing. Formats vary: periodic 1-1s with a rotating set of engineers, small group lunches, or open Q&A. Ask about the team, what's getting in the way, and what they'd change.

Manager accountability and the people pleaser

Your managers are accountable for two things: their team's results and their team's health. Your job is to hold them to it, even when that means uncomfortable conversations. The people pleaser can't.

People pleaser

  • Says yes to everyone above and beside them
  • Avoids telling their own managers hard truths
  • Teams get overcommitted and whiplashed
  • Everyone likes them, nobody trusts their commitments

Accountable leader

  • Says no when the team can't deliver
  • Gives managers clear, honest feedback
  • Holds managers to their goals and their team's health
  • Is trusted because their yes means yes
3

New managers, experienced managers, hiring managers

Teach the basics, explicitly.

They need help with 1-1s, feedback, delegation and saying no. Expect them to fall back to coding under stress. Coach closely, and give them room to make small mistakes.

Learn their style, then hold them to outcomes.

They'll have their own way of working. Don't impose yours, but be clear about expectations, and watch for habits from other company cultures that don't fit yours.

Hiring managers is hard to evaluate.

Interviews can't show you how someone leads. Look at how they think about people problems and team building, check references carefully, and make sure the team gets a voice in the process.

Debugging a dysfunctional organization

Debug an org like a system: hypothesis, data, observation. Follow a fictional case: Team Delta has missed its last three deadlines, and its manager says everything is fine.

Have a hypothesis.

Your gut says the work is under-scoped and the team is afraid to say so. Write it down so you can test it rather than just confirm it.

Clue: the last three projects all slipped in the final two weeks.

Check the data.

Tickets, release history, incident logs, commit activity. What do the systems say?

Clue: tickets keep getting added after the deadline is set. Releases are fine.

Observe the team.

Sit in on standups and planning. Watch how they behave, not what they report.

Clue: in planning, the product manager sets the date before the engineers estimate.

Ask questions.

Open, non-leading ones, in skip-levels and with the manager: "What gets in the way?" "How do you decide dates?"

Clue: engineers say "we just accept the dates; pushing back doesn't go anywhere."

Check the team dynamics.

How do the manager, tech lead and product manager work together? Who holds power in the room?

Clue: the manager avoids conflict with a forceful product partner.

Jump in to help.

Get involved directly for a while: coach the manager on saying no, join the next planning session, and help reset expectations.

Stay curious.

Your first hypothesis was close but incomplete: the real issue was a conflict-avoidant manager, not just scoping. Keep asking why instead of stopping at the first plausible story.

Roadmap uncertainty

You must set expectations and deliver on schedule while the business keeps changing its mind. The further out you plan, the less certain it is. Slide out in time.

Be realistic about changeIf plans always change mid-year, don't promise your team anything that depends on continuity past that point.
Break big projects into deliverablesSo you get value even if the grand vision is cut.
Don't promise tech projects "later"Later never comes. Schedule important work now, or be honest it's backlog.
Reserve 20% for sustaining workIn every planning session. Big rewrites still need their own plan.
Treat tech projects like product projectsHow big? How important? What value can you explain? What does success mean?
