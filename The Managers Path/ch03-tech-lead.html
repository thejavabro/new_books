The one weird trick: step away from the code

The tech lead is often not the best coder on the team. It's the engineer willing to take on leadership: representing the team, planning work, keeping things moving. A common definition is a leader of a development team who still spends at least about 30% of their time writing code. The trick is learning when to stop coding so the team as a whole moves faster. Three hats at once

A tech lead wears three hats, and the job is the overlap. Click a circle.

The overlap is the job.

No single hat is enough. A great architect who ignores the plan, or a planner who never codes, isn't leading the team. Click a circle for each hat.

Systems architect and business analyst

Understand the architecture well enough to make sound design calls, and understand the business need well enough to know what to build and what to simplify.

Project planner

Break work into chunks, find the unknowns, sequence it so the team can work in parallel, and keep everyone moving toward the goal.

Software developer and team leader

Keep writing code so you stay credible and in touch, but also coach, unblock and communicate. The team's output matters more than yours.

Managing a project, step by step

Project management is the new technical skill a tech lead learns. Breaking down a project is a lot like designing a system: you decompose until each piece is understandable. Step through it.

1. Break down the work.

Spreadsheet, Gantt chart, whatever you like. Start with the large chunks and keep splitting.

2. Push through the details and the unknowns.

The tedious part. Keep asking what you don't know until the scary question marks are named. This is where most of the value of planning comes from.

3. Run the project and adjust as you go.

The plan is a tool for noticing drift, not a promise. Compare reality to plan regularly and say early when it's slipping.

4. Use what you learned to manage.

Planning reveals dependencies, risks and where to add people. Use those insights to sequence work and set expectations with stakeholders.

5. Revisit the details near the end.

As you approach completion, go back over the unknowns: launch steps, edge cases, what was cut. Endgames are where projects quietly slip.

Decision point: senior IC or manager?

After being a tech lead, many engineers face a fork. Both paths look different from the inside than from the outside. Flip each card from imagined to real.

Neither is a promotion over the other.Pick the one whose real day you'd enjoy, not whose imagined day sounds best.

Good manager, bad manager: the process czar

The process czar believes the right process will fix every problem. But process only works when the team understands and shares its purpose.

Process czar

  • Imposes a process from a book or last job
  • Measures compliance, not outcomes
  • Adds steps after every mistake
  • Uses process to avoid hard conversations

Good tech lead

  • Lets process grow from the team's real pain
  • Asks what each step is for
  • Removes steps that no longer help
  • Remembers process supports people, not the reverse
