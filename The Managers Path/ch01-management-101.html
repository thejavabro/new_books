The managers you might have met

Many engineers have never had a good manager, so they don't know what to ask for. The best they have seen is benign neglect: a manager who leaves them alone unless they ask for help. That looks fine next to the alternatives, but there is a much better option. Plot managers on two axes: how closely they get involved in your work, and how much they invest in you as a person and in your growth.

Cares about you and helps you grow.

Teaches skills, gives useful feedback, helps you through hard situations, wants you to be able to do their job one day, and above all helps you see what matters so you can focus on it.

Leaves you alone. Mostly harmless.

You already know what to work on, so they stay out of the way. Comfortable, but you get little feedback and little help growing.

Ignores you until it's too late.

Avoids meetings, brushes off concerns, never gives feedback, then tells you out of nowhere that you aren't meeting expectations.

Questions every detail.

Won't let you make decisions on your own. Very involved, but the involvement is about control, not your growth.

Neglect punctuated by anger.

Ignores you until they want to yell at you. Damages the mental health of the whole team.

2

The 1-1 has two jobs

A regular one-on-one meeting is the backbone of a good working relationship. It does two things. It builds human connection, so that when life gets hard you can ask for what you need. And it gives you a private, predictable slot to raise anything at all. Step through what a good one looks like.

It happens on a schedule.

Predictable timing lets you plan what to bring. A manager who keeps cancelling or moving 1-1s makes this impossible. Fewer meetings is fine, none is not.

You share the agenda.

It is not only your manager's job to fill the time. Come with topics you want to discuss.

It is not a status meeting.

For individual contributors, a spoken status report is repetitive. Send status by email or chat and use the time for something that needs a conversation.

A little life outside work.

Not small talk for its own sake. When a death, a new child or a housing crisis hits, it is far easier to ask for time off from someone who knows you. Being an introvert is not a reason to skip this.

Connection becomes trust.

Trust needs some willingness to be vulnerable. That is what lets you bring up the uncomfortable things before they become big.

Feedback and workplace guidance

The second thing to expect is feedback. Good managers praise you, and they also tell you, privately and soon, when something you did did not land. They do it for small things before they become large ones. They also act as a guide to the workplace: who to talk to, how decisions get made, how to handle an awkward situation with a peer.

Your manager noticed you were dismissive of a teammate in a design review. When should they tell you?

Training and career growth: you are the hub

A good manager points you to learning, helps you find projects that stretch you, and helps you get promoted when you're ready. But your manager is one source among many. The most important person for your career is you. Toggle the two mindsets.
