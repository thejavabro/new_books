Why mentoring is your first management job

Mentoring is usually the first time an engineer is responsible for someone else's success. For the person being mentored, it is a fast track: a junior engineer with a mentor gets unstuck sooner, learns the codebase sooner, and learns how the team really works. For the mentor, it is a safe place to practise listening, explaining and giving feedback, all skills a manager needs later.

An illustration, not data: the point is that a mentor shortens the flat start of the curve.

2

Mentoring an intern in three moves

An internship is short. The mentor's job is to make those weeks count, both for the intern and for the company, which is trying to see whether to hire this person. Step through the summer.

Prepare for their arrival.

Know the start date. Have a desk, a working machine and accounts ready. A first day spent waiting for access tells the intern nobody expected them.

Have a project ready.

The best internships have a clearly defined project that is useful but not critical, small enough to finish, and with room to stretch if they move fast.

Stay available.

Interns rarely know which questions are "allowed". Make it easy to ask. Check in often, but let them do the work.

Plan for them to present the work.

A final presentation gives the project a deadline, lets the intern practise communicating, and shows the team what they did. It also gives you material for the hiring decision.

3

New hires bring fresh eyes

A new hire sees everything that the team has stopped noticing: the setup step nobody wrote down, the confusing name, the doc that's two years out of date. A good mentor helps them get productive and also captures what they notice before familiarity sets in. A common habit is to have each new hire fix the onboarding doc as they go.

Each lap makes the next new hire's start smoother, and gives the newcomer a quick, real contribution.

Technical mentoring vs. career mentoring

Not all mentoring is the same. Some is about craft: code, design, debugging. Some is about the career: how to grow, how to handle a situation. They need different things from the mentor.

Good manager, bad manager: the alpha geek

The alpha geek is the engineer who has to be the smartest person in the room. When they become a mentor or a lead, everyone else stops growing.

Alpha geek

  • Answers before the question is finished
  • Rewrites the mentee's code instead of explaining
  • Treats every disagreement as a contest
  • Holds knowledge as status

Good mentor-lead

  • Asks what the mentee has tried
  • Explains the why so the mentee can decide next time
  • Is happy to be proven wrong
  • Gives credit and shares context freely

Three habits of a great mentor

Click each habit. The network on the right shows what making connections does: a mentor links the mentee into the wider organization.

The mentee may have a better idea than you. Fresh questions can expose things the team takes for granted. Ask before telling.

Listen for what they are really asking, which is often not the literal question. Then explain in terms they already know, not the jargon you are used to.

You don't need to know everything. Introduce them to the people who do. Those links outlast the mentoring itself.

Tips for the manager of a mentor

Count it as real workMentoring takes time away from code. Plan for that in the schedule instead of hoping it fits.
Be clear on the goalIs this about ramping a new hire, growing a junior, or testing a future lead? Say so.
Reward itRecognize good mentoring in reviews and promotions, or people will stop doing it.
