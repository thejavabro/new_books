Structure arrives whether you plan it or not

Early startups love to avoid structure: no titles, no process, no levels. The chapter draws on Jo Freeman's essay "The Tyranny of Structurelessness": pretending there's no structure just creates a hidden one, run by whoever has the most informal influence. A structureless group can work, but only under specific conditions. Tick the ones that are true for your team.

Creating and applying core values

Culture is what people do when nobody tells them what to do. Core values make it explicit, and they only matter if they're used. Click each place values should show up.

Define them.

Map company values onto your team, and add a couple that matter to engineering, like a learning culture. Each sub-team will layer its own flavour.

Reward them out loud.

Shoutouts at all-hands for people who lived a value. It can feel awkward; do it anyway. Shared stories bind a community.

Use them in reviews.

Call out when and how people showed the values. It reinforces behaviour and shows who embodies them and who doesn't.

Hire and fire by them.

Ask interview questions that test for them. And be willing to act when someone consistently works against them, however productive.

The same goes for cultural policy: write down the few practices you care about (how decisions are made, how people are promoted) instead of leaving them to whoever has the most influence.

Writing a career ladder

A ladder says what's expected at each level so promotions and pay aren't decided by who argues best. A good one shows how scope grows from level to level across several dimensions. Pick a level to see a sketch (illustrative, not the book's ladder).

Involve the teamSenior engineers and managers draft and critique the parts they know best.
Borrow examplesOther companies' ladders help, especially for very senior levels.
Be detailed, and fit your sizeA director at a 50-person startup isn't one at a multinational.
Short and long formsA side-by-side grid for progression, plus prose for what each level looks like in practice.
Mind pay and recognitionLevels map to salary bands. Don't add levels just to give frequent promotions.

Cross-functional teams

Put everyone a product needs into one team (engineers, product, design, analytics) and "us versus them" turns into "us". This follows Conway's Law: your systems will mirror your communication structure, so choose the communication you most want. The tradeoff: you may accept some inefficiency in the technology to get faster product iteration.

Each function optimizes for itself. Work crosses many hand-offs, and blame crosses with it.

Each pod owns a product area with all the skills it needs. Engineers still report to engineering managers for growth and technical guidance. A small infrastructure group keeps core systems healthy.

Depersonalize decision making

As teams grow, three processes help decisions stop depending on who's loudest or most senior. Each works best with clear behavioural expectations, not just mechanics.

PurposeMostly socialization: several people know the changed code. Tests catch bugs; reviews catch missing docs, related changes and thin testing. DoPut style in an automatic linter so it's never debated. Watch the review backlog so reviews don't block work. Set expectations for respectful comments. AvoidUsing review to criticize colleagues or enforce unrealistic standards.
PurposeLearning, not finding who to blame. Many now call it a "learning review". DoUnderstand the circumstances: missing tests, tools, alerts. Pick the one or two truly high-risk fixes and openly let the rest go. AvoidFinger-pointing ("why did you run that command?"), which makes people hide mistakes. And laundry lists that end with nothing done.
PurposeSocialize big system or tool changes and make their risks clear. ScopeNew languages, frameworks, storage systems, developer tooling. Not every feature design: that's too slow and too common. AskWho knows this tech? Are there production standards? How will we roll out and train? What new operational load does it add? Who reviewsThe people most affected, not a fixed panel of gurus. Nothing demoralizes like a veto from someone unrelated. Much of the value comes from preparing for the review.
