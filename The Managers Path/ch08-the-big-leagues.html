VP of Engineering and CTO

Senior titles mean different things at different companies. A useful model: the CTO is the strategic technical executive the company needs at its current stage, an executive first and a technologist second. The VP of Engineering is usually the top of the management ladder: the person with the "ground game" who makes execution, hiring and teams work. The CTO is not simply the best engineer, nor the top of the technical ladder.

Strategic technical executive.

Understands the business deeply and shapes strategy through technology. Finds the biggest technical opportunities and risks. Protects engineering from becoming a pure execution arm. Needs some reporting power, or influence fades.

Master of operations.

Tracks many initiatives at once and drops into detail when needed. Aligns the roadmap with the hiring plan, runs hiring, coaches the management team, and makes sure business goals become achievable engineering goals.

Both sit at the executive table.

Both translate between business and technology, both are judged by organizational outcomes, and both need trust from engineers and executives alike. At small companies one person may play both roles.

"Why aren't you working on the top priority?"

A senior leader has a new idea and declares it the top priority. Weeks later, they're frustrated nothing has moved. The teams had full queues; nobody said what to stop. Saying something is a priority is different from making the tradeoffs that free people up.

Leaders removed from daily schedules forget that teams have long plans. Announcing a priority changes nothing on the ground.

Go through what's in flight, decide what stops, and communicate it clearly down every level. If your own teams don't know the top priority, ask why, and fix the communication.

3

Setting the strategy

A technology strategy is a plan for how technology will support where the business is going. Step through how to build one.

Do a lot of research.

Understand the business plan and where it's heading. Inventory your systems: what's fragile, what will need to scale. Look at your team's skills and gaps. Talk to peers in other functions.

Combine research with your ideas.

Turn findings into a point of view: where technology can enable the business, what must change, and what you'll deliberately not do.

Draft the strategy.

Write it down. Share early drafts with trusted people, take the critique, and keep iterating. It's hard work, and it's the job.

Consider the board's communication style.

Some boards want data and detail, others a crisp narrative. Shape the same strategy to how your audience listens.

Delivering bad news

Layoffs, a team disbanded, an unpopular policy. As a senior leader, you'll deliver hard messages to large groups. Sort these approaches.

Do
Don't
Announce it in an email to the whole org
Talk to the most affected people one-on-one first
Gather everyone in one room before anyone else hears
Be honest about what's likely to happen next
Ask a fellow exec to help deliver news you strongly disagree with

5

Your first team is your peers

At the top, your most important team isn't the one that reports to you. It's the leadership group of peers in other functions: product, sales, finance, marketing. Your organization becomes your second team.

6

The echo

At the top, everyone watches you. A throwaway comment can change an entire team's focus. Step through how a passing thought echoes down the organization.

A passing thought.

You muse aloud in a meeting you dropped into.

It becomes a wish.

People seek your approval and avoid your criticism, so they take it as direction.

It becomes a mandate.

By the time it reaches a team, a project has changed direction because of a one-off brainstorm.

Detach and be deliberate.

Step back socially (avoid closing down the bar with your org), avoid favourites, and be clear about when you're deciding versus wondering. Your presence alone changes a meeting.

7

Ruling with fear vs. guiding with trust

Fear can get short-term results. The cost is that bad news stops flowing up. Toggle the culture and watch the information.

Public blame, harsh reactions to mistakes, unpredictable anger. People hide problems and avoid risk.

Correcting a culture of fear starts with your own reactions: reward people for raising problems, handle mistakes as learning, and be consistent.

8

True North

Every function has core principles it must keep in sight even when it's inconvenient: its True North. Others will push to bend them, and it's your job to be the one holding the line for yours, while respecting theirs. Pick a function.
