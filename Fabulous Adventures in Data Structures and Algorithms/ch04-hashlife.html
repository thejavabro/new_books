Memoizing Quadtrees for a Better Life

Optimizing Conway's Game of Life the usual way buys constant factors. Re-imagining the grid as an immutable, memoized quadtree buys astronomical speedups: Gosper's HashLife.

1

Conway's Game of Life

An infinite grid of cells, each alive or dead, ticks forward in lockstep. A cell with three live neighbours is alive next tick; with two it stays as it is; anything else, it dies. No players, just a starting pattern. Out of these rules come still lifes, oscillators, spaceships, guns that fire spaceships, and even full Turing-complete computers. Click cells to draw, or pick a pattern.

2

Two arrays and a profiler

The standard first attempt: a fixed array with a ring of permanently dead cells around it (so no bounds checks), a second array to write the next tick into, swap, repeat. Every tick examines every cell: O(n) per tick. Bytes turned out slightly faster than bools; measurement beats guessing.

From there, two ways to go faster. You can tune the same algorithm, or you can challenge the frame and exploit something special about the problem. Compare the options.

Relative speed, log scale. The first three only shrink the constant; HashLife changes what the cost depends on.

Change trackingA region whose cells are all dead or still, with a dead border, will not change next tick. 3

An immutable quadtree, deduplicated

An n-quad is a square of side 2ⁿ. A 0-quad is a single cell; an n-quad is four (n−1)-quads, NW, NE, SW, SE. At first this looks absurd: a reference per cell is far bigger than a bit. But there are only two possible 0-quads, sixteen 1-quads, and of the 65,536 possible 2-quads a real pattern uses few. If the factory is memoized so that equal quads are always the same object, a huge grid shrinks to the size of its irregularity. Draw on this 4-quad and compare object counts.

"A cache with a bad policy is another name for a memory leak"The memoizer is a dictionary from arguments to results, which works for any pure function. A function of several arguments can take one tuple. But the dictionary grows forever unless something evicts entries, so the book's memoizer can report its size and be cleared.
4

A 2-quad knows its center's future

A 4×4 square has exactly enough information to compute its central 2×2 one tick later; the outer ring's own future depends on cells we can't see. That is the base case. For a bigger square, take nine overlapping half-size sub-squares, step each, regroup the nine results into four, and take their centers: you get the center half of the original, one tick later. Step through it on an 8×8 example.

5

Don't take the centers: step them again

In that last stage, instead of just taking the centers of the four regrouped squares, step them too. Now an 8×8 gives its center 4×4 two ticks ahead. Apply the same trick at every level: an n-quad yields its central (n−1)-quad 2ⁿ⁻² ticks in the future, using 13 recursive calls. Memoize that Step function and the futures of common patterns are computed once and reused everywhere.

The grid never shrinksStepping returns a smaller quad, so before each step the grid is "embiggened": placed at the center of a larger, otherwise-empty quad. Empty space is nearly free (all those empty quads are the same object), so we can always add more, and spaceships never hit the edge.
