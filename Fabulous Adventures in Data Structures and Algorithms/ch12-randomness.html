A Better Abstraction for Randomness

Random.Next is a poor vocabulary for probability. Treat a distribution as an object you can sample, project and filter, and building weighted dice becomes clear, and fast with the alias method.

1

Two ways to read a probability

"Red has a 70% chance of beating Blue." What does that mean? A frequentist says a probability is the long-run fraction of outcomes when an experiment is repeated. A Bayesian says it is a degree of confidence, the odds you would accept for a bet, which changes when new evidence arrives. The game is played only once, so only the Bayesian reading really fits. The math is the same either way. The book checks its code the frequentist way (sample 100,000 times and count) and reasons the Bayesian way.

A discrete distribution is described by its probability mass function (PMF): a probability for each member of its support, the set of possible outcomes. A bag of 5 red, 3 blue and 2 green marbles gives P(red) = 0.5, P(blue) = 0.3, P(green) = 0.2.

2

Treat distributions as values

Random.Next(6) is a one-shot tool for one fair die. Just as IEnumerable<T> freed us from "get the next item" loops, an IDistribution<T> with a Sample() method lets code deal in distributions of anything. The discrete version adds Support() and Weight(t). Weights are integers rather than doubles, so they always add up exactly. Each weight list is reduced to lowest terms with Euclid's GCD: weights 30:70 are stored as 3:7.

3

Projection: a fair die with coloured faces

Paint a fair die with 1 green, 2 red and 3 blue faces and you have an unfair three-sided die. A projected distribution samples the underlying one and maps the result. To get its weights, map the whole support, group equal results and add up their weights. That is a categorical distribution: finitely many categories of any type with any weights, the most general finite discrete distribution.

4

Four ways to sample a categorical distribution

Categories 0…n−1 with integer weights (total W). Any other type can be put on top with a projection. Pick the weights, pick an algorithm, and throw darts.

5

Building the alias table, step by step

Make a board with W columns and one row per nonzero category, giving each row weight × n squares. The total is n × W, a perfect rectangle in area but ragged in shape. While some row is too short, take squares from any too-long row to fill it exactly. Each move finishes at least one row, so there are at most n moves. Every row then holds one or two categories: a singleton or a projected Bernoulli. To sample, pick a row uniformly, then sample that row. Two cheap draws, O(1), and no rejections.

Why integers againWith double weights, rounding can label a just-right row as slightly short, and then the loop breaks. Integer weights avoid the whole problem. The alias method also handles zero-weight categories without fuss, which the binary-search ladder must take care over.
