Unification and Anti-unification

Unification asks what values make two trees equal; it powers type inference and logic programming. Anti-unification asks the reverse: what is the most specific pattern both trees fit? It finds clones and suggests fixes.

1

Fill the holes to make two things equal

Unification: given two things containing holes, find what to put in the holes so they become equal, as in A² + 6 = 5 × A (A is 2 or 3). Here the "things" are trees. Anti-unification runs the other way: given two different trees, keep what they share and turn every difference into a hole. One goes from general to specific, the other from specific to general.

HoleA named gap, written as a capital letter: X, Y. (Logicians say "variable".)
ConstantA value with no children: 2, EmptyStack, Alice.
SymbolA name with a left and right child term: ImStack(X, Y).

A substitution maps holes to terms (X → 2). A hole that is a key is bound, otherwise free. Substituting must keep going until no bound holes remain, because a term filled into a hole may itself contain bound holes. Keep that in mind: it is where everything goes wrong.

2

Robinson's unification algorithm

John Robinson's 1965 algorithm, slightly simplified. Start with an empty substitution, then: if either term is a bound hole, replace it by its value and start again. If the terms are equal, succeed. If one is a free hole H, fail if H occurs in the other term (looking through bound holes too), else record H → other. If both are symbols with the same name, unify left children, then right. Otherwise fail. Pick a problem, then try switching the safety checks off.

3

A terrible worst case, a fine typical case

Stack n holes in towers, all on the left in one term and on the right in the other. The substitution stays small, but substituting it out builds a full binary tree: 2ⁿ nodes. Space is a lower bound on time, so this is exponential time too. Equality checks and repeated occurs checks add more cost.

What to do about itMemoize the term factories as in HashLife: equality becomes a pointer check and the exponential tree collapses to a linear DAG. With such tricks the worst case drops to about O(n³), and linear-time algorithms exist. In practice, people still use Robinson's algorithm: worst cases are rare. Use the simplest algorithm with acceptable results and performance.
4

Type inference is unification

Generic type parameters are holes. For List<B> Frob<A, B>(A a, Func<A, B> f) called as var v = Frob(1.5, x => [x]), write the declaration and the call as terms (A, B, T, U, V and X as holes) and unify. Choose the last preset above to run it: T → double, U → List<double>, X → double, so v is a List<List<double>>. (C#'s real inference differs in detail; this is closer to F# or OCaml.)

5

Unification + backtracking = Prolog

Facts: who likes what. Rule: if likes(X, Y) and likes(Z, Y) then compatible(X, Z). To answer compatible(Bob, Q): unify the goal with the rule's conclusion (X → Bob, Z → Q), then solve the conditions one by one, unifying with each fact and backtracking over the alternatives. That combination is the heart of Prolog (1972) and of early, rigorous AI.

6

Anti-unification: the most specific generalization

(1+2) × ((1+2)/5) and 6 × (6/5) differ, but both are a product whose right side divides by 5 and whose two left operands match. Mult(X, Div(Y, Z)) covers both but forgets two facts. The most specific generalization is Mult(A, Div(A, 5)). The Reynolds–Plotkin recursion: equal terms stay as they are; same-named symbols stay and recurse; otherwise reuse the hole already standing for this exact pair, or make a new one.

Naively this is O(n²): an O(n) equality test and a scan of existing holes at each of O(n) calls. Memoized term factories and a memoized AU make it O(n). Holes stand for terms, never for symbol names, so Add(1,2) vs Mult(1,2) is just a hole.

7

Learning bug-fix patterns from history

Anti-unification finds clones: duplicated code where one fix should apply everywhere, or plagiarized homework with renamed variables. At Facebook the author's team went further. They took every code change that fixed a null-dereference defect, turned each into a term Change(before, after), and anti-unified them. A dozen common fix patterns emerged. Then, given new buggy code, unify it with a pattern's "before" and apply the substitution to its "after" to suggest a fix.
