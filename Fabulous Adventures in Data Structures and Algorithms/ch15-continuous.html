Sampling Continuous Distributions

Real-valued randomness needs its own tools: getting a fair uniform double, Box-Muller for the normal curve, inverting a CDF, and the throw-darts-and-reject approach with its traps.

1

A density is an area you throw darts at

A continuous distribution produces real numbers, such as a part's diameter or a probability that something is a bot. It is described by a probability density function (PDF): a non-negative curve with finite area beneath it. To sample, throw a dart uniformly at the area under the curve and take its x coordinate. The chance a sample lands in a range is that range's area divided by the total area. If the total is 1 the PDF is normalized, but in practice you often aren't, and it doesn't matter.

The curve is x0.5(1 − x)1.5 on [0, 1], a beta distribution, which is handy for modelling an unknown probability.

For a tiny width e, P(s ≤ X ≤ s + e) ≈ P(s) · e / total area: a thin rectangle. The next chapter depends on that.

2

Special-purpose samplers: uniform and Box–Muller

The standard uniform on [0, 1] wraps the built-in generator. The normal (Gaussian) distribution is the bell curve, set by a mean and a standard deviation: widgets meant to be 60.0 mm wide with standard deviation 0.1 mm. Its non-normalized PDF is e−x²/2. The Box–Muller trick uses two uniform samples to pick a random radius √(−2 ln u₁) and angle 2πu₂. The x coordinate of that polar point is standard normal, which you then scale and shift.

3

The inverse transform: climb the CDF backwards

This is the continuous version of chapter 12's ladder. Integrate the normalized PDF to get the cumulative distribution function F(x) = P(X ≤ x), an S-shaped curve from 0 to 1 that never decreases. Its inverse is the quantile function Q. Feed Q a uniform random probability and out comes a sample. The Cauchy distribution has F(x) = ½ + arctan(x)/π, so Q(p) = tan(π(p − ½)): a bell with far fatter tails than the normal.

4

Rejection sampling with a helper

What if all you have is a non-normalized PDF you can evaluate? You need a helper distribution you can already sample, with at least the target's support, and a scale factor that makes the scaled helper lie above the target everywhere. Sample x from the helper, pick a height uniformly under the scaled helper, and keep x if the dart lands under the target. The target here is e−x² + e−10(x−1)², two humps. The helper is the standard normal. Try scales below 2.5.

5

Clamping: rejection with scale 1

A very bad car factory: each car's chance of failing within a year is normal around 50% with standard deviation 20%. Probabilities below 0% or above 100% make no sense, so clamp the distribution to [0, 1]. The helper is the original distribution, the scale is 1, and anything outside the range is rejected. That is an ideal job for rejection sampling.

Two problems remainRejections can be arbitrarily many. And nothing automates choosing the helper and scale: the book's 2.5 was found by trial and error, and fat-tailed targets need fat-tailed helpers such as Cauchy. We would like to sample any distribution, posteriors included. That is the next chapter.
