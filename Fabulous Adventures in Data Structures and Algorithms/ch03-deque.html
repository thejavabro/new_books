An Immutable Deque

A double-ended queue that never mutates, pushes and pops at both ends in amortized constant time, and concatenates cheaply. The trick is a finger tree: a recursive type whose middle is a deque of bigger things.

A deque: a stack at both ends

A double-ended queue, or deque, lets you push, peek and pop at both ends. As always with immutable types, the "mutating" operations return a new deque. We'd like every one of them to be cheap, ideally without the O(n) hiccup of the two-stack queue from chapter 2.

interface IDeque<T> : IEnumerable<T> {
  IDeque<T> PushLeft(T item);   IDeque<T> PushRight(T item);
  IDeque<T> PopLeft();          IDeque<T> PopRight();
  T Left();  T Right();         bool IsEmpty { get; }
}
2

The tempting, terrible first attempt

"A deque is empty, a single item, or a left item, a middle deque, and a right item." It reads like a linked list linked on both sides, and the logic works. The performance does not: pushing onto the right means the old right item must go into the middle, whose right item must go into its middle, and so on all the way down. Every push rebuilds every level. Nothing is shared.

Worse than it looksO(n) allocations per push and pop, no persistence, and a naive recursive enumerator that copies O(n) items up an O(n)-deep call stack: O(n²) time to list it. The lesson: an immutable structure that works is not automatically efficient.
3

Step one: a tiny deque of 1 to 4 items

A useful design trick: solve a heavily restricted version of the problem in the dumbest possible way, then build on it. A mini-deque holds exactly one, two, three or four items, implemented as four little record types One, Two, Three, Four. Popping a One or pushing a Four is an error; there is no empty mini. Boring, explicit, obviously correct.

Now redefine: a deque is empty, a single item, or a left mini, a middle deque of minis, and a right mini. The middle stores chunks. When a side mini is full, three of its items are bundled into a Three and pushed into the middle as a single element.

4

Play with a finger tree deque

This is a working implementation. Each row is one level; level 1's minis hold characters, level 2's minis hold Threes of characters, level 3's hold Threes of Threes. Orange rows were touched by the last operation.

Where the "finger" name comes fromA finger tree picks some nodes, the fingers, and keeps them near the root; the farther a node is from a finger, the deeper it sits. Here the fingers are the two ends, so the ends are always at the top and the middle of the sequence sinks deeper. The same idea, with extra bookkeeping such as sizes and splitting, gives persistent priority queues and interval trees.
5

Why it's amortized O(1) and worst-case O(log n)

Two of every three pushes only grow the top mini. One in three overflows into level 2, and two thirds of those stop there; and so on. So 2 of 3 pushes touch one level, 2 of 9 touch two, 2 of 27 touch three. Buffers get exponentially bigger as you go down (level 2 minis hold 3 to 12 characters, level 3 hold 9 to 36), so the tree deepens only logarithmically, without any balancing rules like a red-black tree.

Are we abusing the type system?

A linked list's tail has the same type as the list. Here the middle has a bigger type: a deque of chars has a middle of type deque-of-minis-of-chars, whose middle is deque-of-minis-of-minis-of-chars, and so on. The type grows with every level. Slide to see it.


    

  

  
Generics vs. templatesA C++ compiler expands each template instantiation into fresh code, so an unbounded family of types like this can blow it up. C# and Java generics are genuinely generic: the runtime builds new instantiations on demand and shares code between them. And depth is O(log₃ n), so even a billion items need only about 19 levels.

Concatenation in logarithmic time

  
The Hughes list "concatenated" in O(1) by postponing the work. The finger tree does it for real in O(log n), after relaxing one invariant: the middle may hold Twos as well as Threes. To join deque A to deque B, keep A's left and B's right as they are. Take A's right mini and B's left mini (2 to 8 items), regroup them into Twos and Threes, push those onto A's middle, and then recursively concatenate that with B's middle.
