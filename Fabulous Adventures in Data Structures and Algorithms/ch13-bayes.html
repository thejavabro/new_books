Conditional Probability with Bayes' Theorem

Bayes' theorem turns "how likely is the evidence, given the cause?" into "how likely is the cause, given the evidence?". Expressed with distribution objects, a posterior is just a prior pushed through a likelihood.

Ten thousand people, two questions

Notice that P(C & S) = P(C) · P(S | C) = 10% × 86%, and also = P(S) · P(C | S) = 11.3% × 76.1%. Set the two equal and you have Bayes' theorem: P(C | S) = P(C) · P(S | C) / P(S). The bar "|" means if. P(C) is the prior, belief before evidence. P(C | S) is the posterior, belief after seeing a sneeze: 10% jumps to 76%.

2

Priors change; likelihoods hold steady

Colds are seasonal, so P(C) drifts. But "86% of cold sufferers sneeze, 3% of others do" is a fact about colds, which plausibly stays the same. That is a likelihood function: given a cause, it returns a distribution of effects, a Func<Cold, IDiscreteDistribution<Sneezed>>. Combine any prior with it and you get the joint distribution over (cause, effect) pairs. With integer weights, multiply each prior weight by the LCM of the likelihoods' total weights so the division always comes out exact.

Posterior = joint, filtered, projectedBuild the joint, filter out pairs that disagree with the observation (that is the division by P(S)), then project back to the cause. The result is a function from evidence to a distribution over causes. For one sneeze: Yes 86 : No 27.
3

Positive on a 99.5% accurate test

A rare condition affects 1 person in 30,000. A cheap test is right 99.5% of the time for both the sick and the healthy. You test positive. How sure should you be? Make a guess before you look, then use the controls.

Everyone who tests positive:

The test is wrong 1 time in 200, but you started out at 1 in 30,000. A positive result makes the condition about 200 times more believable, which is still under 1%. In a population of 6 million there are about 200 true cases, 199 of whom test positive, along with 29,999 false alarms. A negative result, by contrast, is extremely reassuring. Distrust any claimed posterior that never mentions the prior.

4

999 fair coins and one trick coin

Draw one coin from a bag of 999 fair coins and 1 double-headed coin. It comes up heads k times in a row. Is it the trick coin? Posterior weights: fair is 999 × 1 and trick is 1 × 2k, over the same denominator 2k. At k = 10 that is 999 : 1024, just over even. That makes sense: drawing the trick coin and getting ten fair heads are both roughly 1-in-1,000 events. The same reasoning tells you whether an account showing ten human-like behaviours is really human.

5

Unconditional likelihoods: rolling with advantage

If the "likelihood" ignores its input, the two quantities are independent, and the same machinery still builds their joint distribution. Roll a d20 twice and keep the higher roll: take the joint of two d20s (400 pairs), then project each pair to its max. The resulting weight for k is 2k − 1, so a 20 is 39 times as likely as a 1. Combining "joint then project" into one helper turns out to matter a great deal in the next chapter.
