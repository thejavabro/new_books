Markov Processes and Metropolis

A Markov process picks its next state from its current one alone. That makes nonsense prose generators, and, with the Metropolis rule, a way to sample distributions you can only evaluate up to a constant.

1

Random sequences with no memory

Wander a strange city, choosing a direction at random at each corner. Gamble chips until you are broke or bored. Autocomplete one word after another. These are all Markov processes: start in a state drawn from an initial distribution, then repeatedly draw the next state from a transition function that sees only the current state. When the transition returns the empty distribution, the sequence stops. A sample is an entire sequence. The transition function is just a likelihood function from chapter 13.

Gambler's ruin

Start with 4 to 7 chips. Each coin flip wins or loses one chip. Stop at 0 (broke) or 10 (happy).

Markov models are everywhere: Brownian motion, DNA mutation, the weather, prices. A work queue is a good one to simulate: jobs arrive, workers finish them, and running the chain many times shows how long queues get and how long clients wait.

2

A very small language model

Let the state be a word. The initial distribution is the set of first words of sentences; the transition from a word is the distribution of the words that followed it in the source text, with a sentence-ending word mapping to empty. The book trains on Shakespeare. This page trains on a tiny corpus of sentences written for it, so you can see every count. Tap a word to follow the chain yourself.

Large language models add better tokens, far more context as the state (at a cost that grows with length), vastly more text, learned semantics and human correction. The basic loop is unchanged: pick the next word at random based on what came before. There is no plan and no backtracking. Any intent to seem meaningful belongs to the people who built the model, not to the random process.

3

The Metropolis algorithm

Rejection sampling needed a hand-fitted helper. Metropolis needs only the (unnormalized) PDF, an initial distribution inside the support, and an unbiased proposal distribution centred on the current point. Each step: propose a nearby x′; compute the ratio w(x′) / w(x); accept x′ with that probability (always if it is uphill), otherwise repeat x. The sequence of samples is itself a Markov process. Only the ratio matters, so normalization never comes up, and each step does a fixed amount of work with no loops.

4

Finally: sampling a continuous posterior

Bayes for densities: posterior(a) ∝ prior(a) · likelihood(a).Weight(evidence). The divisor P(evidence) is just a constant, which an unnormalized PDF can ignore, and Metropolis can sample from what remains. Use the prior as the initial distribution, and a normal around the current point, with a sensible width, as the proposal. (The likelihoods must be consistently weighted with one another.)

A factory makes bent coins whose chance of heads follows the clamped normal from chapter 15, N(0.5, 0.2) on [0, 1]. Flip one coin 10 times. The likelihood is the binomial: C(10, k) · pk(1 − p)10−k. How fair is this coin?

Replace "coin fairness" with "probability this account is a hostile bot" and "heads" with observed behaviours, and this is a real tool. Programs increasingly make decisions under uncertainty at scale. Doing it with principled methods like this one is a responsibility.
