Designing a Video-Processing Pipeline

Turn one uploaded master file into dozens of renditions that play smoothly on any screen and network, then deliver them worldwide.

1

From studio master to every screen

Studios upload huge source files (no size limit, resumable uploads). The system must make each title playable on every device, at a quality that fits each viewer's bandwidth, as soon as possible, without losing anything. Availability of the upload side is less critical: a studio can retry later. The focus is video on demand; live streaming comes later. The book's sizing:

90% movies, 10% series of 10 episodes; 90-minute videos. Encoding shrinks a 50 GB master about a thousandfold, to ~50 MB per rendition. Masters dominate storage, so they belong in cheaper S3 tiers once encoded.

2

An asynchronous pipeline

Every step is stateless and idempotent and runs under an orchestrator, like the crawler in Chapter 15. The principle throughout: fail fast, validating after every step so a bad file never reaches viewers.

3

Chunk it, encode in parallel

A codec (H.264/AVC, HEVC, VP9, AV1) compresses; a container (.mp4, .mov) wraps the result. Each title needs many codec × resolution × bitrate combinations, so the source is split into chunks that encode in parallel. A failed chunk is retried alone instead of re-encoding a 90-minute film. Chunk boundaries follow shots, not a fixed 30 seconds, which also lets each shot get its own settings (shot-based encoding).

Each chunk takes 1 time unit; one worker alone would need 48.

4

The ladder and adaptive streaming

Renditions form a bitrate ladder: from 4K at a high bitrate down to 144p. The player downloads a few seconds at a time and picks the rung its measured bandwidth can sustain, protecting its buffer (HLS on Apple devices, MPEG-DASH elsewhere). Netflix tunes the ladder per title, then per shot, from a complexity analysis: a cartoon needs fewer bits than a rainy battle. Drag the network.

6

Getting bits close to viewers

Encoded chunks are DRM-packaged and stored in S3, with a manifest and chunk locations in a metadata store (Cassandra at Netflix, DynamoDB works). Hit play: the play service checks device and network, the steering service picks caches and returns URLs, and the client streams from them. Netflix puts Open Connect Appliances inside ISPs; CloudFront offers edge locations, regional edge caches, Origin Shield and embedded PoPs in ISPs. Watch how layers cut origin load.

8

Day N: own the encoding brain

Step Functions scales fine but bills per state transition; MediaConvert scales but decides encoding for you. At Netflix scale the steps become separate microservices on EKS (separate clusters or namespaces per team), started by EventBridge when a master lands in S3, coordinated by an orchestrator (Conductor, Maestro or Step Functions). Large outputs pass between steps as S3 URLs.
