System Design Trade-offs and Guidelines

Every large system is a pile of choices between good things you cannot have all at once. This chapter names the qualities we want, how to measure them, and the trade-offs that connect them.

The qualities every system is judged on

A large system is many small servers talking over a network. Abstraction lets us ignore the wiring and reason about a handful of qualities instead: how servers communicate, whether they agree on the data (consistency), whether they answer at all (availability), whether they keep doing the right thing over time (reliability), whether they grow with demand (scalability), whether people can change them (maintainability), and whether they survive breakage (fault tolerance). 2

Synchronous or asynchronous communication

Synchronous calls work like a phone call: the caller waits, blocked, until the reply arrives. Asynchronous calls work like email: the caller hands off the message and carries on, picking up the reply later. Async decouples services and absorbs bursts, but makes the flow harder to follow and to debug.

3

Strong versus eventual consistency

With replicas, "consistent" means every copy agrees. The book places models on a consistency spectrum: at one end strong (every read sees the latest write, as if there were one copy), at the other eventual (replicas converge if writes stop, but a read may be stale for a while). In between sit guarantees such as monotonic reads (you never go back in time) and monotonic writes (your writes apply in order). Try it: a client writes x = 2 to the leader, then another client reads from a replica.

Stricter costs moreStrong consistency needs coordination: synchronous replication, blocking and consensus. That adds latency and makes failures harder to handle, especially across regions. Eventual consistency is cheaper and faster, at the price of stale reads.
4

Counting nines

Availability is the share of time the system answers: (total time − downtime) / total time. It is quoted in "nines". Each extra nine cuts allowed downtime by ten, and usually costs far more than the last one. Pick a target.

Components combine. In sequence (a request must pass through all of them) availabilities multiply, so the chain is weaker than its weakest link. In parallel (any one can serve) only the chance that all fail at once counts: 1 − (1−A₁)(1−A₂).

5

Failover and replication patterns

High availability rests on two complementary patterns. Failover switches traffic to a spare when something dies. Replication keeps extra copies of the data so the spare has something to serve. Toggle the failover style and then knock out the primary.

Single-leader replication

One node takes all writes and streams them to read replicas. Simple and conflict-free, but the leader is a write bottleneck, and if it dies before replicating, recent writes can be lost. More replicas also means more replication lag.

Multileader replication

Several nodes accept writes and sync with each other. Writes survive a leader loss and can happen near users, but concurrent updates to the same item conflict and need a resolution rule (for example, last write wins).

6

Reliability: MTBF, MTTR, RPO and RTO

Reliability is doing the intended job, correctly, over time. Two averages describe it: MTBF, mean time between failures, and MTTR, mean time to repair. A system can be available but unreliable (it answers, wrongly) or reliable but unavailable (correct when up, but often down). Long MTBF and short MTTR together give steady-state availability of roughly MTBF / (MTBF + MTTR).

Fault tolerance adds two recovery levers. Replication keeps live copies. Checkpointing saves state periodically (synchronously, pausing writes, or asynchronously in the background) so you can restore. Checkpoints set your RPO, how much recent data you can afford to lose, while your restore process sets the RTO, how long you can afford to be down.

7

Scaling up and scaling out

Scalability means performance keeps up as load grows. Vertical scaling buys a bigger machine: easy, no code changes, but capped by the largest box and still a single point of failure. Horizontal scaling adds more machines behind a load balancer: nearly unlimited and more resilient, but you must handle distribution, state and coordination. A common path: scale up early, scale out when you hit the ceiling.

The classic trade-offs

Latency is how long a request waits; processing time is how long it takes once picked up; response time is the sum the user feels. Throughput is how much work completes per second, bounded by bandwidth. Batching raises throughput but adds wait. Slide the batch size.

10

CAP and PACELC

The CAP theorem: a distributed store cannot guarantee consistency, availability and partition tolerance all at once. Since partitions happen whether you like it or not, the real choice is what to do during one: refuse (stay consistent) or answer (stay available). PACELC adds the everyday case: Else, when there is no partition, you still trade Latency against Consistency. Walk the decision.
