1

A stockbroker, not an exchange

A broker (Zerodha, Robinhood, Groww) sits between investors and exchanges (NSE, Nasdaq, NYSE). Users search stocks, watch live prices and candlestick charts, place buy and sell orders during market hours, and track profit and loss. Nonfunctional: high availability, no lost order messages, strong consistency for orders, and order handling well under 100 ms. The book starts from Zerodha's numbers.

Two trades per trading user per day. The listed universe is small and stable (NSE ~2,300, Nasdaq ~3,300, NYSE ~2,800 companies), so every price fits in memory; the hard part is fan-out to millions of screens.

2

How much of the order book do you buy?

Exchanges sell market data at several depths. Bid = the most a buyer will pay; ask = the least a seller will take. Level 1 is the best bid and ask; level 2, the best five of each; level 3, the best twenty; tick-by-tick (TBT) is every order. Brokers subscribe through a cloud endpoint, a vendor feed, or racks colocated in the exchange (often over FIX); Zerodha turns TBT into level 3 for its users.

3

Live tick fan-out

A thin stock tick consumer service (STCS) takes the exchange feed and publishes to Kafka (ordered per partition). The stock live ticker service (SLTS) keeps prices in a shared Redis (with a hot copy in local memory) and, on each server, a table of stock → connection IDs; each server holds around 100,000 WebSocket connections on lightweight threads. Step through one tick.

Order matters: a stale price shown after a fresh one is wrong. Kafka keeps order within a partition, and the client also drops any event whose sequence number is older than the last one it applied. Shuffle the network.

4

Candles from a time-series store

A second Kafka consumer, the historical stock tick service, writes every tick to a time-series database (Amazon Timestream; InfluxDB or ClickHouse also fit). Most queries ask for the last day or week, so recent data stays in Timestream's fast memory store and older data moves automatically to the cheaper magnetic store, with a retention limit. Candles are aggregations of ticks per interval.

5

The order management system

The OMS turns intent into an order and sends it to the exchange, which matches buyers with sellers. First a separate risk management system (RMS) runs compliance and risk checks. The broker records an internal order with a unique ID first (idempotency: no duplicate executions), then maps it to the exchange's order ID; acknowledgments and fills arrive asynchronously. Rules live in versioned storage edited through an operations portal, are tested in staging and rolled out gradually, and every order records the rule version that approved it, for audit. Order data needs ACID and is write-heavy: the hot path runs in memory (Redis), persisted in parallel to PostgreSQL or DynamoDB, partitioned by time.

Shaving milliseconds

  
A broker is not a high-frequency trader (HFTs chase microseconds with colocated hardware, shared-memory messaging such as Aeron or Chronicle Queue, single-threaded loops, C++ or garbage-free Java, even FPGAs). 7
Profit and loss

  
Holdings settle at the end of the day, so P&L is computed off-hours in batches (Zerodha uses AWS Batch) and stored as derived data. During the day the client recalculates it from live ticks. DynamoDB layout: partition key userId, sort key PL for the total and S#{stock} per holding; old items move to Standard-IA or S3.
