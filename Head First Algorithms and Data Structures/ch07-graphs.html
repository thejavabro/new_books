Graphs and Graph Traversal

Road maps, friendships, links and dependencies are all graphs. Once you can store a graph and walk it systematically, a whole family of problems opens up, starting with the fewest-hops route.

1

It's all about relationships

Arrays and hash tables answer "what is at this index?" or "what does this key map to?". Many problems are really about connections: roads between places, links between pages, friendships, dependencies. A graph models them with vertices (the things) and edges (the connections). The book's town of Algorithmville is a graph of places joined by roads. Tap any two places to see a path between them.

2

Pete the courier rides in cycles

Pete's standard route leaves City Hall, loops through the Museum, Pizza Shack and Airport, and comes back. A path that starts and ends at the same vertex is a cycle; a simple cycle repeats no vertex on the way. In a simple undirected graph the smallest possible cycle has three edges. Step through every simple cycle in the map; notice that dead-end places like the Library can never be on one.

3

Adjacency list or adjacency matrix

Computers need dots and lines in a precise form. An adjacency list gives each vertex a little notebook of its neighbors (in Python, a dict of lists): space only for edges that exist. An adjacency matrix is a V × V grid with a 1 (or a weight) wherever an edge exists: instant "is there an edge from A to B?" checks, but V² cells even when most are 0. Tap a vertex to see where it lives in each.

The matrix of an undirected graph is symmetric: a 1 at (i, j) means a 1 at (j, i).

4

Breadth-first traversal: ripple outward

At Algorithm Land you could explore by visiting every ride one step from the Ticket Counter, then every ride two steps away, and so on. That is breadth-first traversal. A queue keeps the order: dequeue a vertex, visit it, enqueue its unvisited neighbors. A visited set stops you going round in circles. Neighbor order comes from the adjacency list.

5

Breadth-first search finds the fewest-hops route

Because BFS reaches everything one edge away before anything two edges away, the first time it reaches the goal it has found a path with the fewest edges. To report the route, enqueue (vertex, path-so-far) pairs instead of bare vertices. Storm clouds over the park? Pick where you are and the exit.

Fewest edges is not the same as least time. Once edges carry weights, you need Dijkstra's algorithm, which is chapter 8.

6

Depth-first traversal: follow one trail to the end

Depth-first traversal dives down one path as far as it can, then backs up to the most recent fork and tries the next branch. Swap BFS's queue for a stack and you have it; or let recursion's call stack do the job. It visits everything, but makes no promise about shortest paths. Compare the two visiting orders from the same start.

Call stack (top on the right)
Visited order
def dfs(graph, v, visited=None):
    visited = visited or set()
    visited.add(v)
    for n in graph[v]:
        if n not in visited:
            dfs(graph, n, visited)
    return visited
Which one?BFS for fewest hops and "who is near me". DFS for "is there any path", exploring mazes, detecting cycles, ordering dependencies. Both run in O(V + E) with an adjacency list.
7

Big O depends on vertices and edges

Traversal visits each vertex once and examines each edge once: O(V + E) with an adjacency list. With a matrix, finding a vertex's neighbors means scanning its whole row of V cells, so traversal costs O(V²) however sparse the graph is. Extra space is O(V) either way: the visited set, the queue or stack, the path. Slide density and compare.
