Big O Notation

Your search is fast today. Will it still be fast at a million items? Big O is the shared shorthand for answering that, without a stopwatch and without caring which computer you own.

Say hello to Big O

Chapter 1 described growth in words: "linear", "logarithmic". Big O is the shorthand that lets everyone say the same thing in a few characters. It does not care about your processor, your RAM or your language. It describes how the amount of work grows as the input n gets large, and it describes the worst case, an upper bound. Tap each part of the notation.

Say it out loud as "O of n squared" or "order n squared". O(log n) is "O of log n"; O(1) is "O of one" or "constant time".

2

O(1): work that ignores n

O(1) does not mean exactly one step. It means the work does not depend on n. Reading item 5 of an array costs the same whether the array holds 10 values or 10 million, because the computer jumps straight to that slot. Grow the array and watch the two counters.

1steps for arr[5] (O(1))
12steps to add up every item (O(n))
Primitive operations are the unitComparing two numbers, swapping two items, reading a slot: each is one constant-time step. Saying an algorithm is O(n) really means it does about n of these O(1) steps. Big O then asks only how that count grows: doubling n doubles it (O(n)), quadruples it (O(n²)) or leaves it alone (O(1)).

O(n²): the Experimental Pizza Lab

A loop inside a loop over the same list does n × n steps. The book's pizza lab pairs every topping with every topping, itself included. Toggle toppings and watch the pairing grid fill. Then switch to the round-robin tournament, where each pair plays once and nobody plays themself: about half the grid, n(n−1)/2, which is still O(n²) because halving is just a constant.

Drop the constants, keep the biggest term

  
Big O keeps only what dominates as n grows. Constant factors go (5n is O(n)), constant additions go (n + 100 is O(n)), and smaller terms go (n² + 3n is O(n²)). A nested loop is not automatically O(n²): if the inner loop always runs 5 times, the total is 5n, which is O(n). Simplify each expression.

O(2ⁿ): the ACME supercomputer

  
Exponential time puts n in the exponent: each extra item doubles the work. The book hands you a breakthrough physics algorithm that runs in O(2ⁿ) on a machine doing 1,000 operations per second, with n = 30. Should the team wait? Try it, then crank the machine's speed and see how little that helps.

Exponential growth usually means brute force through every possibility. You don't fix it with more RAM or a faster chip; you need a better idea, or inputs that are guaranteed to stay small.






  
6
O(n!): every possible order

  
Factorial is worse still. n! = n × (n−1) × ... × 1 counts every ordering of n things, so it shows up whenever an algorithm tries every arrangement: every seating plan, every delivery route. Add riders and watch the orderings pile up.

Recap

  

    
O(1)Work does not depend on n. Array access by index.

    
O(log n)Halve each step. Binary search.

    
O(n)One pass over the data. Double n, double the work.

    
O(n²)Nested loops over the same data. Double n, four times the work.

    
O(2ⁿ)Each extra item doubles the work. Brute-forcing subsets.

    
O(n!)Every ordering. Impractical beyond tiny n.

    
SimplifyDrop constants and lower-order terms; Big O is the worst-case trend.
