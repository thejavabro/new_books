Greedy Algorithms

Sometimes the smartest move is to grab what looks best right now. For some problems that is provably optimal; for others it is a fast, decent guess. Knowing which is which is the skill.

What it means to be greedy

Give a customer 67¢ in change and you don't weigh every combination. You grab the biggest coin that fits, then the next biggest, and so on: 25, 25, 10, 5, 1, 1. That is a greedy algorithm: make the locally best choice at each step, never look back, and hope it adds up to the best overall answer. With US coins it always does. Now switch to a country with only 1¢, 3¢ and 4¢ coins and ask for 6¢.

Greedy algorithms always find a solution quickly. Whether it is the best solution depends on the problem. For some problems we can prove greed is optimal; for others it is a fast, decent guess.

2

Halloweek: when fewest edges isn't fastest

It's Halloweek at Algorithm Land. Crowds clog some paths, so every edge now has a difficulty rating: how long it takes to cross right now. You want to get from the Ticket Counter to the Exponential Drop in the least time. Breadth-first search from chapter 7 finds the path with the fewest edges, then stops. Compare it with the cheapest path.

3

Dijkstra's algorithm: cheapest first

Dijkstra's algorithm is breadth-first search with one change: swap the FIFO queue for a priority queue that always hands back the entry with the lowest total cost so far. That one greedy choice is enough. When a vertex comes off the queue, no cheaper route to it can exist, because every other route already costs at least as much. A vertex can sit in the queue twice with different costs; the cheaper copy pops first and the stale one is skipped.

One rule: no negative weightsDijkstra's guarantee relies on costs only ever growing along a path. A negative edge could make a "settled" vertex cheaper later. GPS travel times are never negative, which is why this algorithm sits inside route planners.
4

Inside the priority queue: a min-heap

A priority queue is usually built on a heap. A heap is a tree (a graph with no cycles, one root at the top), specifically a complete binary tree: every node has at most two children and levels fill left to right with no gaps. It keeps one rule: every parent is less than or equal to its children, so the smallest item is always at the root. Python's heapq stores the tree in a plain list: the children of index i are at 2i+1 and 2i+2.

A push goes at the end and bubbles up; a pop moves the last item to the root and sinks it down. Either way it travels at most the height of the tree, about log₂ n levels, so both are O(log n), and peeking at the minimum is O(1).

5

How fast is Dijkstra's algorithm?

Count the expensive operations. Each vertex is settled once: up to V pops at O(log V) each. Each edge can cause at most one push: up to E pushes at O(log V) each. Total: O((V + E) log V) time and O(V + E) space for the visited set and the queue. With a million intersections, log V is only about 20.

6

Greedy sorting: selection sort

Greed shows up in sorting too. Selection sort scans for the smallest remaining item and swaps it into the next position, which is then final forever. The greedy choice "grab the smallest left" happens to give the right global answer. It is O(n²) (a loop inside a loop), uses O(1) extra space, and makes at most one swap per pass.

7

Grab the dragon's loot

The dragon is out, but circling back. Your backpack holds 50 pounds, and you must take each item whole or leave it. Which items maximize the value? Try packing by hand, then let the greedy rule (best value per pound first) pack, then reveal the true best.

Brute force checks every subset: each item is in or out, so 2ⁿ combinations. Greedy computes ratios (O(n)), sorts (O(n log n)) and makes one pass (O(n)): O(n log n) overall. The catch: greedy grabbed scrolls and goblets for their ratios and left no room for the crown. A slightly worse early choice would have opened a better combination, and greedy never looks ahead.

If you could cut items into fractions, greedy by ratio would be optimal. For whole items, the reliable fix is dynamic programming, next chapter.
