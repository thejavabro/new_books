The AI-Native Engineer

AI can now write much of the code. That does not make engineers obsolete; it moves the job toward the parts that always mattered most: deciding what to build, setting the boundaries and proving it works.

1

The vibe-coding trap

Vibe coding means describing roughly what you want, accepting the model's changes without reading them closely, pasting errors back in and letting it fill the gaps. For a throwaway experiment it is brilliant. For software that has to last, it fails in predictable ways. The trick is knowing which mode you are in.

What are you building?

Three ways it fails in production. Tap each.

Fluency is not understanding. Using AI to speed up implementation is a superpower. Giving up the engineering judgment that makes implementation reliable is the trap. Keep the first, refuse the second.

3

From implementer to orchestrator

The job used to be mostly translation: turn a requirement into code. When an agent drafts a CRUD endpoint in thirty seconds, translation stops being the scarce skill. An orchestrator defines the work precisely, hands it to a capable executor, reviews the result against the intent, keeps what passes and redirects what doesn't.

The proportions are an illustration of the four shifts the chapter describes, not measured data.

"Every engineer is a manager now."
Not in the org-chart sense, but in directing, reviewing and being accountable for work other capable entities produce.
4

Why the old workflow stops scaling

The classic flow assumed two things: implementation speed is the bottleneck, and you author every line. Neither holds now. The constraint moves upstream to clear requirements and downstream to rigorous verification. And the old habit of discovering the design while coding breaks, because the agent does not accumulate understanding as it goes; it builds new decisions on top of its first wrong guess.

The later a misunderstanding is found, the more it costs

1×

Barry Boehm's studies of large 1970s projects found a requirements defect caught in operation could cost up to around a hundred times more than one caught early. With AI, generation is so fast that a lot of misaligned code can pile up before anyone notices, so the slope gets steeper. The cure is the same: more time before implementation, less correcting after.

5

The new center of gravity

Almost every AI-native decision comes back to three ideas. They map to old friends (requirements, non-functional requirements, QA), but now they decide how much value you get from the model.

Pin down the degrees of freedom

Every gap in your request is a decision the model makes from its training-data averages. Switch between the two requests and watch the open assumptions close.

Sort the constraints

The most common failure is the agent breaking a constraint it never heard about. Constraints come in three kinds.

6

Human in the loop, by design

HITL means a person explicitly approves decisions above a certain level of consequence or novelty before the system goes on. The agent reasons and proposes; the engineer reviews and decides. It is not distrust of AI. It keeps the reliable parts fast and the risky parts safe.

Calibrate the review to the risk

Skipping review to go faster is backwards. The cheapest checkpoint is the spec review, before anything exists. Ten minutes there can save hours of untangling a misaligned implementation.
7

Iterate on purpose, not at random

When the first output is wrong, there are two responses. Rephrase the prompt and hope, or diagnose the gap (missing case? unclear constraint? missing context?), fix the spec, and run again. One is a random walk, the other a straight line.

● rephrase and hope● fix the spec gap

The target is a correct, reviewed output.

Before

Write first: behavior, constraints, edge cases, acceptance criteria. Maintain rules files and conventions as infrastructure the agent loads every time.

During

Check output against intent, constraints and the existing system. Run tests aimed at agent-typical misses: unspecified edge cases, untested errors, silent boundaries.

Throughout

Own every commit. Stay close on high-stakes work. Keep improving the context system, so month three is easier than month one.

8

The eight-skill stack

Technical depth is now the floor, not the ceiling. What sets engineers apart sits above it: judgment, communication and owning the system. Rate yourself on each skill and see your shape. Tap a name for what it means.

9

Agent = model + harness

The model is what the vendor ships. The harness is everything you build around it: guides that steer the agent before it acts (feed-forward) and sensors that let it see its own mistakes after (feedback). You need both. Rules without feedback mean the agent obeys but never learns whether it worked.

OpenAI's Codex team reported a three-person team shipping roughly a million lines in five months with agent-driven work, and concluded the hard part is now designing environments, feedback loops and control systems. Every harness improvement makes every future task more reliable.

10

Tasks shrink, purpose grows

Coding is one task of a software engineer; solving problems is the purpose. When a resource gets cheaper to use, people find many more uses for it. That is the Jevons paradox, first noticed with coal and steam engines in 1865. Drag the cost of writing software down and watch what becomes worth building.

100%

Why it becomes leadership

Deciding what to build, how it should behave and whether it meets the bar is leadership work. Radiology is a precedent: AI reads scans well, yet demand for radiologists who interpret them in context grew.

If you are junior

  • Pick the harder problem on purpose.
  • Learn tools one layer deeper than the people around you.
  • Read agent code until you could have written it.
  • Practise problem breakdown, first-principles reasoning, clear writing.
  • Find a team where a senior pairs with you on the agent.
