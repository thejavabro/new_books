The story so far

Plot, in brief

The Thanksgiving e-mail works too well: web traffic keeps knocking the site over, and on Black Friday stores are mobbed by customers holding printouts of promotions for items that are already gone. The team spends the weekend building fixes. By Monday, sales are breaking records and the company looks set for its first profitable quarter in over a year. Unicorn now deploys weekly and is trying daily.

Sarah brings bad news: a competitor offers build-to-order kits with its manufacturers. Doing the same needs changes to an old manufacturing system that was outsourced years ago, and the outsourcer quotes eighteen months. Bill proposes bringing it back in-house; Steve agrees and puts Sarah on notice. In January, with Sev 1 calls rare, Steve offers Bill a two-year fast track through the business toward chief operating officer, with Erik as mentor. Erik has one request: write a book.

Design for operations: turn features off without a deploy

During the surge, a developer argues there's no point recommending more products if customers can't check out, so the recommendations should be switched off. Everyone agrees, but changing and deploying the code takes two hours. Afterwards the team makes it a configuration setting, so next time it takes minutes. They also tune database queries and move large images to a content delivery network. Try the surge.

Load is normal.

3

From a release every nine months to experiments every day

Chris reports Unicorn has moved from deploying every two weeks to every week, with daily deployments being trialled, and A/B tests running all the time. Bill wants every new internal application built this way, and in some cases lets developers deploy their own code. Compare the cadences.

Log scale. Every deployment is a chance to learn something from customers, so the bar length is how many chances a year each cadence gives you.

4

When the constraint is outside the building

The build-to-order idea runs into a constraint Bill can't manage: an outsourcer whose contract covers keeping the old system alive, not changing it. Their proposal: six months of requirements, nine months of development, and no people free until June, so eighteen months at least, plus $50,000 just to study it. A few people are assigned to the account part-time. Bill's idea is to take the work back, and the engineers with it.

Steve calls IT a core competency, assigns one of Dick's best people and the company's lawyer to the transition, and tells Sarah that she works for him, not the chairman.

5

The Third Way: break things on purpose

With fires rare, Bill's team spends its time on prevention: a month at 15% of time on preventive infrastructure work, monitoring gaps closed, the top ten fragile systems replaced or refactored. Then comes Project Narwhal: a "chaos monkey" that randomly kills processes and servers, as Netflix famously did. The first week everything falls over; over the following weeks Development and Operations make the systems resilient. John adds an "Evil Chaos Monkey" that attacks constantly. Bill's reasoning: practice and repetition are how you make failure routine. Inject failures below.

Improvement as a two-week habit

Bill now coaches his managers through two-week improvement cycles, following Mike Rother's Improvement Kata. "Kata" means a routine practised until it becomes habit. Tap around the loop.
