The story so far

Plot, in brief

An analyst's note opens the book: Parts Unlimited's stock has dropped by half from its peak, the chairman and CEO roles are being split, and the board has given CEO Steve Masters six months. The rescue plan, a retail and e-commerce programme called Phoenix, is years late.

Bill Palmer runs a small, well-drilled midrange team and likes it that way. One Tuesday his bosses are pushed out and Steve talks him into becoming VP of IT Operations. His first assignment arrives within the hour: the payroll run has lost every hourly employee's data, and the window closes at 5 p.m.

The trouble with IT as plumbing

Steve tells Bill he wants IT to be like a toilet: something he uses without thinking about it. It's a fair wish for the result. The trap is that it also makes IT's work invisible. Leaders see the few requests that reach them. Everything that keeps those requests possible sits below the waterline, where nobody counts it, plans it or protects time for it.

4

Anatomy of the payroll failure

Bill does something useful first: before touching any servers, he asks Finance to walk him through how payroll data actually flows. That picture changes which questions are worth asking. Step through it.

Why the SAN theory was suspiciousIf shared storage had failed badly enough to corrupt payroll, many systems should have been failing. Instead, only one kind of record was wrong in one way: salaried pay was fine and every hourly record was zero. A narrow, tidy symptom usually points to a narrow cause, like a change to one upstream system, not a broad infrastructure failure.
5

The first question in any outage: what changed?

Ann in Finance says nothing was scheduled to change. In the operations centre, nobody can say for sure either, and the storage team's attempt to roll back a firmware upgrade has made the disk array stop serving data altogether. When changes aren't recorded, every outage becomes a hunt. Pick the clues Bill would chase first and watch the clock.
