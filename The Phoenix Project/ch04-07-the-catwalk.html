How a launch date gets set without the people who must deliver it

The Phoenix date is decided in a meeting where Development commits to deploying and the business commits to marketing, but the people who will install, run and support the software were never asked. Bill argues that a rushed deployment will break things that matter more than Phoenix, such as point-of-sale systems. He loses. Notice the shape of the decision: the work lands on IT Operations, but the trade-off is made upstream.

Brent at 100 percentWhen Kirsten, the project manager, asks why tasks are late, the answer is a person: Brent is fully booked. A resource that is always fully booked can't absorb anything new without something else waiting. The book returns to this again and again; here it's just a symptom.
3

Why the change process died, and the smallest one that works

The old process failed because it cost more than it seemed to give back. Engineers were made to fill in long forms in a tool, the networking and server teams rebelled, and the CAB meeting turned into an empty room. Bill's reboot starts small: show up, and say what you're changing. Compare the two.

Old change requestRequester✓Business justification✓Risk category (12 options)✓Affected CIs✓Rollback plan document✓Approver chain✓…plus dozens more fields✓Felt like paperwork. People stopped using it.
Index cardWho is changing✓What and where✓When✓Who you talked to✓Enough to answer "what changed?" in an outage, and to spot two changes that collide.

Illustrative: the more a change record costs to fill in, the fewer changes get recorded at all, and an unrecorded change is invisible in the next outage.

Erik's catwalk: the job release desk and the bottleneck

Erik shows Bill a plant that once drowned in inventory. A supervisor used to release a new job whenever the first work centre was free. But the job then had to pass a slower station, a heat-treat oven, and work piled up in front of it. Eliyahu Goldratt's Theory of Constraints, popularised in his novel The Goal, says improvements anywhere except the bottleneck are an illusion. The fix was to release work at the pace of the bottleneck. Run both policies.

The riddle: four types of work

Bill says Phoenix is work. Erik agrees: that's one type, business projects. There are three more, and Erik won't say what they are. He means kinds of work at the level of the whole organisation, not "e-mails" or "meetings". Pick the four you think he means, then check. Bill takes until chapter 15 to get them all.

A first glimpse of the Three Ways

Before driving Bill back, Erik names the principles the rest of the book unpacks. They're stated briefly here and get a page each later.

First WayFast flow of work from Development into IT Operations and on to the customer.
Second WayShort, fast feedback loops so problems are fixed where they're created and rework is avoided.
Third WayA culture of experimentation and of learning from failure, with repetition and practice as the route to mastery.
