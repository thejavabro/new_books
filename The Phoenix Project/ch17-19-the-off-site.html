The story so far

Plot, in brief

Bill spends four days at home, sleeping well for the first time in weeks. His team calls about a new outage; he doesn't go in. Then Steve calls Paige, and then Bill, to apologise: he concedes he hasn't been fair, that he now sees IT as a competency the company must build, and asks for ninety days. Bill agrees to come back.

At Steve's off-site, with Erik in the room, Steve opens by talking about his own failures and asks every leader to share something personal, citing Patrick Lencioni's idea that trust needs vulnerability. It's excruciating and it works. The team then names its real problem: far more work has been accepted than IT can do. Bill proposes something nobody expects: freeze every project except Phoenix.

Why the book spends a chapter on feelings

A novel about IT operations pauses for a team-building exercise because nothing else on the following pages works without it. Bill's later changes depend on people telling each other the truth: that a project will fail, that a date is impossible, that their team caused the outage. Erik compares the CEO and CIO to a dysfunctional marriage, where both sides feel powerless and held hostage by the other.

What a leadership team without trust does with bad news

3

How a plant accepts an order, and why IT couldn't

Bill reports what he learned from the plant: before accepting an order, a scheduler looks at the bill of materials and the routing, checks the load on each work centre the job will pass through, and only then decides whether taking it would jeopardise existing commitments. IT had no such check. Work was accepted because someone asked. Try it with IT's work centres.

4

Technical debt: shortcuts with interest

Bill describes the cycle: no one analyses capacity before accepting work, so teams scramble and take shortcuts, so production gets more fragile, so there's more firefighting later. Erik names it: technical debt that isn't being paid down. Like financial debt, it charges interest, and the interest is paid in unplanned work. Left alone, he warns, the only work that gets done is unplanned work.

5

The project freeze: why doing less finishes more

Bill's proposal: for two weeks, accept no new projects and stop all IT Operations work except Phoenix. Development keeps coding but deploys nothing else. Steve thinks it's mad; John objects that his audit work can't wait. Erik asks Steve what happens to plant WIP if you keep releasing work into a jammed system. The answer is the whole argument. See it with four projects of equal size.

What the freeze also didIt made the remaining work visible and stopped people deciding priorities on the fly. Steve sends the memo to every manager. The team also agrees to identify the biggest areas of technical debt for Development to tackle.
