The story so far

Plot, in brief

Patty is ready to give up on the change process: since the reboot, about sixty percent of scheduled changes never get done, and the unfinished ones roll forward. Many are stuck waiting for Brent, partly because Bill told him to work only on Phoenix. Bill looks at the growing pile of cards and remembers Erik's mountains of inventory.

Phoenix deploys on Friday afternoon. Testing can't keep up, the release isn't finished when the stores open, and the point-of-sale systems go down. By Monday it's in the news. Stores take orders on paper and Finance types them in by hand. Then John spots card security codes written on the scanned order slips: holding them breaks the payment-card (PCI) rules, with PCI auditors due soon.

The Bates Motel of changes

Patty's numbers: of the changes scheduled each day, only about 40% get done. With 240 left over this week and 400 new requests next week, next week's board holds 640. She is already tracking 942 open changes. Bill calls it the Bates Motel: changes check in but never check out. Erik called work in process the silent killer. Change the inputs and watch the pile.

Little's law, in one lineAverage time a piece of work spends in the system = work in process ÷ throughput. If the pile doubles and the completion rate doesn't change, every change waits twice as long. More WIP never makes work go faster; it only makes it wait longer.
3

If Brent is the oven, who is Mark?

Bill runs the analogy forward. On the plant floor, Mark at the release desk let work in without regard for the bottleneck, so it piled up in front of the oven. In IT Operations, work piles up in front of Brent. So who lets it in? The uncomfortable answer: the CAB, which approved every change without asking whether there was capacity to do it. Tap a plant item to see its IT twin.

Bill's next move isn't a fix but a measurement: mark which change cards need Brent, so they can see how much work is heading his way. You can't control the flow until you can see it.
