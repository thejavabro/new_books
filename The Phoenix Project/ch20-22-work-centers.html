The story so far

Plot, in brief

The freeze works: Phoenix tasks start getting done and fewer fires break out. But business managers keep asking for exceptions, and Patty worries about what happens when the freeze lifts. Bill goes back to the catwalk, where Erik corrects him: the constraint isn't a person, it's the set of work centres that only that person can run.

At a long audit meeting, the business managers show the external auditors that their own procedures, not IT controls, catch financial errors. The feared material weaknesses evaporate. John, who spent years on those IT controls, is crushed, and Erik tells him bluntly that he has been protecting the wrong things. The next week Patty unveils a kanban board.

Brent is a worker, not a work centre

When Erik asks for the constraints, Bill confidently says "Brent". Erik scoffs: is Brent a heat-treat oven? Bill gets there: Brent is a worker who staffs far too many work centres. Imagine a plant where a quarter of the stations could only be run by one man. Every work centre has four parts. Tap them; Patty later maps them onto configuring a server.

That reframing gives Bill his rule for lifting the freeze: a project is safe to release if it doesn't route through any work centre that needs Brent. Finding out which projects those are means building something IT never had: a bill of materials for its work, listing the steps, tools and people each recurring job needs.

Which projects are safe to release?

Each line is a project's route through IT work centres. Red centres currently need Brent. Projects that avoid them can be released without adding to his queue.

Busy people make work wait: the wait-time curve

Erik then points at the spaces between the work centres, where work waits. His rule of thumb: wait time grows with % busy ÷ % idle. At 50% busy, a job waits one unit of time; at 90% busy, nine; at 99%, ninety-nine. This is why a "fully utilized" Brent makes everything late. Drag the slider.

4

Improving daily work beats doing daily work

Bill's second question: can the monitoring project start? It doesn't need Brent, and better monitoring would mean fewer outages escalated to him. So it elevates the constraint: it's the most important project they have. Erik links it to Total Productive Maintenance, which protects machine availability by treating maintenance as a priority, and to Mike Rother's Improvement Kata: improve something in short, repeated cycles, because a system that isn't improving is getting worse.

And John's long list of security projects? Erik asks three questions: do they increase flow of project work, add capacity at the constraint, or reduce WIP? No, no and no. That doesn't make security unimportant; it means these particular projects compete with the constraint instead of helping it.

5

The audit: risk is managed by the whole system, not just IT

John's IT findings looked catastrophic in isolation. The audit turned on a different question: where does the company actually rely on controls? If a business process downstream would catch an erroneous or fraudulent entry anyway, the IT control upstream isn't what stands between the company and a misstatement. Follow a bad order through the layers.

Erik and JohnErik tells John he's been like a commissar walking onto the plant floor and demanding changes without understanding the work, and like a QA manager writing endless tests for features nobody uses. It's harsh, and it sends John away for days. When he returns, he has changed. The lesson the book draws: protect what matters to the business, and find out what that is first.
6

Patty's kanban board

Patty visits the plant herself and comes back with a board: Ready, Doing, Done, with limits on how much can be in Doing. She documents the most common service requests step by step, starting with laptop replacements, and can now promise delivery dates that hold. She colour-codes the cards so the mix of work is visible at a glance, and reserves about 20% of capacity for improvement work, as Erik recommended. Pull cards across; the board enforces a WIP limit of 3.
