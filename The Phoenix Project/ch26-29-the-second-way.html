The story so far

Plot, in brief

Six weeks in, IT Operations is unrecognisable: outages are handled calmly, drills and retrospectives are routine, work in progress is down, and people know what to work on. John has cut unneeded security projects, and Dick has told Steve he's pleased with IT. Then John finds that Sarah has quietly hired outside vendors, including one to mine customer data, against the company's own privacy promises.

The second Phoenix deployment, on Halloween weekend, still goes badly: a database migration step fails because Brent had changed the production database weeks earlier, for one of Sarah's projects, and the change never reached the test environments. At Monday's meeting Steve shuts down Sarah's side projects, and Erik tells the team they've learned the First Way; now they need the Second.

Why the second deployment still broke: drift

The migration tested fine, because the test environments didn't match production. A single direct change by Brent to the production database, made before the kanban existed, meant every rehearsal was a rehearsal against the wrong system. Bill: there should be no way for Development and QA environments to differ from production. Toggle the scenarios.

4

The Second Way: shrink the batch to speed the feedback

Chris wants to slow Phoenix to a release every other month; releases are so painful that his developers can't afford to do them often. Erik says the opposite. The ideal is single-piece flow, reached by continually shrinking batch sizes. Long release cycles mean huge batches, slow feedback and wild variation between releases. "You'll never hit the target," he says, "if you can fire the cannon only once every nine months." Think anti-aircraft guns.

Erik ties it back to Dick: with long cycles, once labour is counted, projects never hit their internal rate of return. Faster cycle times are a business requirement, not an engineering preference.

5

Work should have no reverse gear

Erik asks Wes about the dragster motorcycle on the boardroom wall. Six gears, no reverse. So why does IT's flow of work have one? Steve answers like the plant manager he was: work flowing backward means waste, from defects, unclear specifications or rework. Every time something bounces back from Operations to Development, the whole line pays. Set the bounce-back rate.

6

Put Herbie at the front of the line

In The Goal, the hero takes his son's scout troop on a hike and finds the slowest boy, Herbie, sets the pace for everyone; he moves Herbie to the front. Erik's instruction: put Brent at the front of the development process, working on environments and deployment at the earliest stage, instead of mopping up at the end. Then, if Phoenix can't deliver what the business needs fast enough, deliver it some other way.
