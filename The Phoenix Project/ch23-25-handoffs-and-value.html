The story so far

Plot, in brief

Brent is a week late on a Phoenix task he said would take forty-five minutes. Patty digs in: the "task" is really twenty-odd steps across six teams. Bill draws the wait-time curve on a whiteboard and the room goes quiet. They decide to standardise recurring work and give someone the job of moving tasks between people.

On a quiet Saturday night John calls Bill, drunk, from a bar; he has a trailer hitched to his car and says he's leaving. Bill sees him home. John reappears days later with a shaved head and a calm manner, and books a meeting with Dick. Instead of pitching security, he asks Dick what a good day looks like. Dick shows them the measures he really manages the company by.

2

Thirty seconds of work, weeks of waiting

Wes jokes that a firewall change takes thirty seconds of typing and four weeks of calendar time. That gap between touch time and lead time is what's eating Phoenix. Bill applies Erik's rule: with seven handoffs and every resource 90% busy, each step queues for about nine times the base wait. Nine hours times seven steps is sixty-three hours in queues alone, before anyone does any work. Adjust the numbers.

Everybody needs slackPatty's reading of the curve: if nobody has idle time, work gets stuck in queues. Utilisation is not efficiency. A team running at 100% is a team where everything waits.

Standardise the recurring work, and add a water spider

Find the hidden projectsAbout a third of IT's "tasks" on the Phoenix plan were really multi-step, multi-team jobs. They get corrected in the project plan.
Standardise the top 20Patty and Wes's team document the twenty most frequent recurring jobs, each with its own kanban lane, as they did for laptops.
Kill the snowflakesStandard builds mean production configurations stop being one-of-a-kind. Brent became Brent by being the only person who knew each snowflake.
A water spiderBorrowed from the plant: someone who carries work between stations so it never sits waiting for a handoff, until the kanbans can do the signalling.
Deployment is final assemblyPatty notices every flow of work passes through deployment, like final assembly in a plant. That's where the kanban belongs.
4

John's turn: from controls to outcomes

John's arc is the book's clearest picture of someone changing how they see their job. He doesn't lower his standards; he changes what he measures himself against.

Before
  • Carries a binder of findings everywhere.
  • Measures success by controls implemented.
  • Pushes projects onto IT regardless of capacity.
  • Feels like the only one who cares.
After
  • Starts with a blank notebook and questions.
  • Asks the business what it relies on and what it fears.
  • Later cuts unnecessary security work from IT's queue.
  • Finds allies by protecting business goals.
5

Dick's second slide: what the business actually runs on

John asks Dick what a good day and a bad day look like. Dick shows two slides. The first is the familiar health of the company: market share, profitability, receivables, borrowing costs. The second, which he looks at every day, asks whether the company is competitive and effective. Asked which are at risk, he says: all of them. Bill realises Dick asked him only about Phoenix, when nearly every line depends on IT. Pick a goal to trace what it rests on.

The goals are Dick's. The processes and systems are this companion's illustration of how such a value chain is traced, not a list from the book.

6

Leading indicators: Erik's oil-change metaphor

Erik tells Bill to go to the owners of each of Dick's goals, learn the processes behind them, and list what threatens them, including the invisible IT parts. His metaphor: a delivery company's goal is on-time delivery; breakdowns threaten it; missed oil changes cause breakdowns. So you track a forward-looking measure, the share of trucks with their oil changed, before deliveries start failing. In IT, patches, monitoring and change discipline are the oil changes.

Erik adds one more framing: the wisest auditors say there are only three control objectives: reliable financial reporting, compliance with laws, and efficient and effective operations. Everything else is a slice of those.
