The story so far

Plot, in brief

The SWAT team gets a name the Operations people hate, Unicorn, and a mission: deliver customer recommendations and promotions, fast. It starts from a clean code base decoupled from Phoenix and its own database fed with copies of Phoenix, order-entry and inventory data. Brent and the QA team build automation that creates matching Dev, QA and Production environments.

Then Brent vanishes: Dick's office has whisked him off to a task force planning the company's break-up. Bill calls him back, Sarah complains to the chairman, and Steve backs Bill. The promotion runs are too slow for the hardware budget, so the team rents cloud capacity by the night. A test e-mail to one percent of customers converts better than any campaign the company has run.

Decouple first, so you can move fast safely

Touching Phoenix's production database meant linking to its libraries and winning approval from an architecture board. So Unicorn copies the data it needs into a database of its own. Now it can develop, test and run without putting Phoenix or other critical systems at risk, and without being held up by processes built for them. Bill applauds, while noting he'll need to standardise which databases are allowed, so the company keeps the skills to run them.

3

Environments on demand: the end of snowflakes

Within one three-week sprint, perhaps for the first time in memory, every developer runs exactly the same operating system, libraries, database and settings. On Phoenix, a new developer needed three or four weeks just to get a working build; on Unicorn they check out a ready-made virtual machine. QA has a matching environment early in the project. This is Erik's advice in action: Brent working at the front of the line, on the thing every later step depends on.

4

Protecting the constraint, at the top of the company

Brent is quietly reassigned to Project Talon, the plan to split the company. Bill's reasoning when he asks Brent to come back is pure constraint thinking: Unicorn is the last chance to hit the quarter; without Brent, Unicorn stalls; the task force can manage with the next-best engineer, perhaps less accurately. Steve agrees and handles the politics, including Sarah's e-mail to the chairman. Try the trade-off.

5

Rent a thousand servers for an hour

The first promotion report works but runs fifty times slower than planned: over 24 hours for a small test set on the 20 servers budgeted. Buying the thousand-plus servers needed would mean over a million dollars of unbudgeted capital. A developer suggests sending the ready-made compute images to the cloud, starting hundreds of instances each evening and shutting them down when done. Because environments are already automated, it isn't hard. A security engineer lists the risks of putting customer data there, and is asked to propose countermeasures rather than block it.

6

Test on one percent before betting on everyone

Maggie, from retail programme management, demos promotions generated from her own buying history: discounts on items the company has in surplus at high margin. She proposes an e-mail to one percent of customers before going all in on Black Friday. The result: over 20% of recipients visit the site and over 6% buy, roughly five times better than any earlier campaign. A small, reversible experiment turns a guess into evidence.

Security gets faster tooJohn is amazed: with Unicorn's short cycle times, security fixes go in quickly instead of waiting months for the next big release. And the last SOX-404 audit issues are closed. Deployment practice that once happened twice a year now happens constantly.
