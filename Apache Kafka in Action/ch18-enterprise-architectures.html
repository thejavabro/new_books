Where Kafka shines, and where it doesn't

The final chapter steps back to look at Kafka in whole enterprise architectures: as the backbone of a data mesh, for liberating data from core systems, for big data pipelines and the Industrial IoT. And, just as important, it lists what Kafka is not, so you don't misuse it.

From central data team to data mesh

The classic setup: sales lives in a CRM, production in an ERP, and a central data engineering team writes ETL pipelines into a warehouse. Sales renames fields without telling anyone, production exports incomplete data, the data team lacks domain knowledge, pipelines break, reports are late and unreliable, and everybody is frustrated. The lake turns into a data swamp. The data mesh flips responsibility. Try a schema change in both worlds:

    Liberating data from core systems

    Banks, insurers and manufacturers run decades-old core systems on central databases: indispensable, hard to change, already near their load limit. You can't point a mobile app at the core banking system. The idea: get the data out in near real time with minimal changes to the core, and build new services on the liberated copy. Debezium CDC reads the database commit log, but the raw tables are normalized, versioned and cryptic, known to a handful of experts. Don't let other teams consume them raw. How to turn them into usable events?

    Real question from a customer: "I have a thousand-line join across 200 tables. How do I do that in Kafka Streams?" Best answer: probably don't. Liberating data is exciting, but far from simple.

    4

    Big data: where Kafka started

    LinkedIn built Kafka to ship every click, comment and like to Hadoop. The log made that easy: trivial operations give huge throughput, data survives a Hadoop outage, and spikes are buffered. Some companies say Kafka is the cheapest part of their pipeline to operate. Architecturally it's almost mundane: A to B, at scale.

    5

    Kafka in the Industrial IoT

    Factories, vehicles, power plants and wind turbines produce ever more data: for customers (warehouse status), for quality (pharma), for performance and for predictive maintenance. Kafka serves both real-time streams and batch reports.

    Data creates demand: plan self-service access early so the Kafka team isn't buried in trivial requests, and consider keeping (non-personal) history: someone will eventually want it to train models. Permissions exist only per topic, so don't give end users read access to shared topics; enforce fine-grained rights in backend logic or a proxy like Kroxylicious.

    Recap and quick check

    Data meshDomains own data products; platform team enables; Kafka carries them.
    Core systemsCDC out, but reshape raw tables into real events.
    Big data & IIoTBuffer spikes, MQTT at the edge, tiered storage for history.
    ClustersOne central cluster unless isolation, security, geography or scale demand more.
    Not a…relational DB, synchronous API, file store, or fix for bad architecture.
    Small apps<1,000 msg/h, one team, no growth: use PostgreSQL.
