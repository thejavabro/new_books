Getting to Know Kafka as an Architect

Kafka is less a message queue than a replicated, durable log that many systems can read at their own pace. This chapter maps the platform an architect has to reason about, from brokers to Schema Registry, Connect and stream processors.

1

Two ways for services to talk

The classic integration is request-response: when a customer changes their address, the customer service calls the billing service, then shipping, then marketing, and waits for each answer. It is simple and well tooled, but the caller must know every receiver, the slowest call sets the pace, and one failing service breaks the whole chain.

In event-driven architecture (EDA) the customer service just announces "address changed" to an intermediary channel. Any service that cares subscribes and reacts in its own time. The sender does not know, or wait for, the receivers.

There is a second reason teams arrive at events: sheer volume. Clickstreams, IoT readings, logs and metrics are produced continuously by many sources and analyzed later. Synchronous calls are simply not a sensible fit; an event stream is often the only practical design.

2

Field notes: Northwind's Customer 360

Running example

The book follows a fictional mid-sized insurer, Northwind Insurance. Policy, claims and customer service each run on separate ageing platforms. The overnight warehouse batch takes about 14 hours, so business users see yesterday's data in the afternoon. Now the business wants Customer 360, an operational data store (ODS) that consolidates every touchpoint into one current view of each customer, and it wants it in the cloud.

The senior data engineer proposes thinking "events first": source systems publish changes to Kafka, and Customer 360 subscribes and builds whatever projections it needs. The lead architect is sceptical. Every later chapter returns to this team and the decisions they make, so watch for these boxes.

3

Brokers, clients and controllers

Applications never talk to each other directly through Kafka; they talk to a cluster of brokers. A client that writes is a producer, a client that reads is a consumer. Brokers persist messages, replicate them, acknowledge writes and serve reads. Consumers pull data when they are ready, which is why a slow consumer cannot overwhelm itself.

A separate set of KRaft controllers keeps the cluster metadata: which brokers are alive, which topics exist, and which broker leads each partition. If a broker dies, the controller notices and hands its work to others. (Older clusters used ZooKeeper for this; modern Kafka has its own Raft-based quorum.) Tap a component.

6

Operations and deployment options

An architect does not have to tune brokers personally, but must understand what running Kafka costs. The main fork in the road is who operates it.

On-premises
  • Full control over versions and configuration
  • Can be cheapest with a skilled ops team
  • You own hardware, security, monitoring and upgrades
Managed cloud Kafka
  • Provisioning and SLAs handled by the provider
  • Version chosen for you; low-level broker tuning often locked
  • Some ecosystem parts (such as Schema Registry) may be commercial or absent
  • Fewer choices of management tooling
Alternatives
  • Apache Pulsar, Amazon Kinesis, Google Pub/Sub, Azure Event Hubs
  • Several speak the Kafka protocol, so clients can be portable

Chapter 9 returns to this decision in depth, including hybrid setups.

7

Kafka for sending messages, and for storing data

Kafka keeps messages after they are read. How long is a setting, and that one setting moves Kafka between two very different roles.

What sets Kafka apartIt was designed as a distributed commit log, not a queue: an ordered, immutable sequence of events, persisted and replicated across brokers, that can be replayed. That design is why it handles very high throughput, survives broker failures, and can serve both real-time consumers and late readers who want history.

Recap and quick check

Two stylesRequest-response couples caller and callee in time; events decouple them through a channel.
Cluster rolesBrokers store and serve data; KRaft controllers manage metadata; clients produce or consume.
Log, not queueMessages are appended, immutable, replicated and retained, so many consumers can read independently.
EcosystemSchema Registry for contracts, Kafka Connect for no-code pipelines, Streams or Flink for processing.
Two usesShort retention: message delivery. Long or infinite retention: state storage and event sourcing.
