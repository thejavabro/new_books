Creating Consumer Applications

Consumers pull, track their own position, and share partitions within a group. Most production incidents with Kafka are really questions about offsets, timeouts and rebalances.

1

Pulling, and consumer groups

Kafka never pushes. A consumer runs a loop: poll for new records, deserialize, process, then commit its offsets to say how far it got. The broker simply answers fetch requests.

Parallelism and pub-sub both come from one idea, the consumer group (group.id). Inside a group, the topic's partitions are divided so each is read by exactly one member. Different groups are independent: each gets every message. So "a microservice" is a group, and its instances are the members.

2

Group coordinator and group leader

Two roles organize a group. The group coordinator is a broker (different groups can have different ones) that tracks members and drives rebalances. The group leader is one of the consumers, chosen at join time, which computes the partition assignment in the classic protocol. Members never talk to each other.

3

Offsets: the one number that decides loss or duplication

Committed offsets are stored per group in the internal topic __consumer_offsets. After a crash or rebalance, whoever takes the partition resumes from the last committed offset. When you commit relative to processing decides your guarantee. Play it: process some records, commit (or let auto-commit fire), then crash.

Field notes

Customer 360's dashboard must not lose messages, so the team sets enable.auto.commit=false and commits in code after processing. They accept that a crash can replay a few records, which means processing has to be idempotent.

When there is no committed offset

A brand-new group has no offsets, and committed offsets also expire if a group stays inactive longer than offsets.retention.minutes (7 days by default). Then auto.offset.reset decides:

For explicit control, skip groups altogether: assign partitions in code and seek to an offset or timestamp, for example to reload a local cache from a whole topic. Reading a compacted topic uses the same API; just remember you may still see several values for a key if the cleaner has not run yet, and that tombstones arrive as null values.

5

Liveness: heartbeats and poll intervals

The group must notice a dead member and hand its partitions to someone else: a rebalance. Two separate checks exist. A background thread sends heartbeats every heartbeat.interval.ms; no heartbeat for session.timeout.ms means the process is gone. And if the app does not call poll again within max.poll.interval.ms (5 minutes by default), it is considered stuck even though it is alive.

6

Assignment strategies and rebalances

The partition.assignment.strategy decides who gets what, and how much moves when membership changes. Remove a consumer and compare.

The next-generation rebalance protocolClassic groups put the assignment logic in clients, which makes debugging and upgrades awkward and lets one slow member stall everyone. The newer consumer protocol (KIP-848, group.protocol=consumer) moves assignment to the broker-side coordinator, makes rebalances incremental per member, and is configured with broker settings such as group.consumer.assignors. A member that does not give up revoked partitions within max.poll.interval.ms is removed.

Compression and the REST Proxy

Compression shrinks network traffic and disk. Usually the producer compresses whole batches (compression.type = gzip, snappy, lz4 or zstd); the broker stores them as is and the consumer decompresses. The broker-side compression.type defaults to producer, meaning keep whatever the producer chose; set it to a codec and the broker recompresses, costing CPU.

Illustrative relative values for typical JSON batches; measure with your own data. Larger batches compress better.

When no native client exists for your language, the Confluent REST Proxy lets apps produce and consume over HTTP. It translates requests into the Kafka protocol, at the cost of an extra hop, extra infrastructure and less control over client behaviour.
