Adaptation

Release is the beginning of a system's life. Teams and architectures that make change cheap get better returns from every experiment. How to arrange process, organization and systems so they can keep adapting.

1

Change is guaranteed. Survival is not.

The Silicon Valley slogans ("software is eating the world", "move fast and break things") all focus on change: surviving it or, better, causing it. Agile embraced change driven by the business; now software change just as often creates markets, alliances and competitors, sometimes from unrelated industries. In a startup, the competitor may be yesterday's version of your own product. The chapter covers people, process, tools and design, which interrelate and need to be introduced in parallel, bit by bit.

Not all software gains from fast change. Where each release needs costly certification (avionics, implanted medical devices) there's a big transaction cost per release. Exceptions exist (JPL hotfixed the Spirit rover on Mars, and Curiosity only got its ground-operations software after landing), but nobody upgrades a rover's RAM in flight. Rapid adaptation pays when the relationship between effort and return is convex, as it usually is in competitive markets. Compare the shapes.

2

Your speed is the speed of your decision loop

Every change goes round a loop: someone observes a need, someone decides a feature fits and is worth doing (and how fast), someone acts and ships it, then someone checks whether it worked. In a tiny company that loop lives in one or two heads; in a big one, each role may be a committee. Call it Deming/Shewhart (plan-do-check-act), an OODA loop, or a programme of A/B tests: getting round faster makes you more competitive, and if you go faster than rivals you get "inside their decision loop" and they react to you. Agile and lean cut delay from "act"; DevOps cut more and improved "observe". Start the clock at the first observation, not when the story hits the backlog. The next frontier is "decide". Adjust the phases.

3

Thrashing: steering faster than feedback arrives

Shortening cycle time is good, until development changes direction faster than feedback comes back. Pilots call it pilot-induced oscillation, or "porpoising": the plane responds late, so the pilot over-pulls, the nose overshoots, he over-pushes, and the aircraft leaps and dives like a dolphin. In software it's thrashing: shifting priorities, an endless series of crises, half-built work abandoned for the next direction. Play with the delay.

The fix is cadence, not brakes.Aim for a steady rhythm of delivery and feedback. If one side is faster, don't slow it down; spend the spare effort speeding up the other. If development outpaces feedback, don't build more deployment tooling; build an experimentation platform that speeds up observing and deciding.

A platform team, with developers as customers

The old split (developers on apps, operations on racks, networks, databases and OSs) was erased and redrawn before anyone said "DevOps". Virtualization and cloud made infrastructure programmable, open-source tools made operations programmable, and images and containers turned programs into "operating systems". Development and operations are now needed at every layer from Foundations. What was infrastructure becomes a platform, and it needs a team that offers, by API and command line, things like:

Mechanisms, not policies.The platform team builds the monitoring service that lets you install your rules, and the service that builds API gateways for teams, not every rule and gateway itself. A vendor Platform gives a head start but doesn't remove the need for the team. It is measured on the platform's availability; application availability belongs to application teams.
The rule of thumb: if developers use the platform only because it's mandatory, it isn't good enough. In the old world operations was the last line of defence and developers were suspects. Here they're customers.

5

Releases should be as big a deal as a haircut

The Godot release ran from afternoon to the small hours with over twenty people. Hard releases happen rarely, rare releases are each unique, unique releases need more planning, and so they get harder still. Slowing the calendar is like seeing the dentist less because it hurts. Frequent releases force you to get good at them, and a faster feedback loop makes improvements more accurate. The answer is less effort, fewer people, more automation and standardization. Three patterns from Continuous Delivery (Humble and Farley) keep quality up as frequency rises. Pick one and roll it forward.

All three act as governors on a dangerous action, and all three limit how many customers can meet a bug, by time or by headcount.

6

The most important part of evolution is extinction

Natural selection is wasteful and random, but repeated small variations under selection pressure make an ecosystem that persists even as species vanish. Evolutionary architecture tries to capture that: let small units of technology and business capability succeed or fail independently. Example: two ideas for encouraging sign-ups, cross-site tracking to find keen users versus a blanket offer. One big promotions service accumulates complexity faster than two small ones (it must also handle routing and precedence), risks "frameworkitis", and shares a database so every change can disrupt more. Two services, each handling one dimension, split the complexity into pieces that are easier to prune, like a bonsai juniper rather than a hundred-foot oak. The caller (or an application-aware router) decides whom to call. Then one service wins (define "wins" carefully: conversion, or acquisition cost against lifetime value?). What do you do with the laggard?

7

Two-pizza teams are about dependencies

Amazon's two-pizza rule is misunderstood. Fewer people helps communication, but the real point is a team that is self-sufficient all the way to production, so members cover several disciplines. Every external dependency is one of the Lilliputians' ropes on Gulliver: easy alone, crippling by the thousand. Waiting for a DBA from the data architecture team, or for review boards, release reviews, change committees and "the People's Committee for Proper Naming Conventions", adds queueing delay at every step. Add hand-offs and watch the lead time.

Illustrative numbers: two days of work, plus a queue wait for each hand-off.

No coordinated deployments.Needing to deploy a provider and its caller together means they're tightly coupled, and the provider is responsible. Make the change backward-compatible (Handling Versions), or add the new interface as a new route and retire the old one weeks later. Self-sufficient teams need tooling too: firewalls, load balancers and SANs wrapped in APIs, so each team manages its own config. Enabling this is the platform team's job.
8

Beware efficiency

Efficiency goes wrong two ways. First, "efficient" as fully utilized: everyone busy close to 100% looks good if you watch the people, but watch the work and it crawls (the lesson of The Goal, Lean Software Development, Product Development Flow, Lean Enterprise, The DevOps Handbook). Judge a value stream from the work's view: short cycle time, high throughput. Second, a highly efficient stream is specialized to today's job. A carmaker replaced its conveyor with a rig that held and turned each car from inside: faster work, less floor space, but a custom rig per model, so redesigning or switching to vans got harder.

Sailboat or container ship?

Before bolting a platform onto one vendor, use the company grapevine. The book notes that in 2017 many firms were uneasy about depending on AWS and edging toward multiple clouds. Left alone, software tends toward the Big Ball of Mud.

9

Rotate the layers 90 degrees

Petroski's "form follows failure": designs evolve to fix what earlier versions did badly. Ford, Parsons and Kua define an evolutionary architecture as supporting "incremental, guided change" across many dimensions, which the classic layered enterprise app does poorly. Layers give vertical isolation (how often do you really swap the database?) but encourage horizontal coupling around a few giant domain classes. Bad layering shows up as a commit adding Foo, FooController, FooMapper, FooDTO…: the domain's concepts echo through every layer. Each layer should speak its own concepts (tables and rows; forms), translating at the boundary. Or rotate the barriers.

A note on microservices. They're a technical fix for an organizational problem: communication paths and code dependencies both explode with size, classes follow a power law, a few "untouchable" classes block refactoring, and the code rots into a Big Ball of Mud whose ramp-up time can exceed average tenure. Microservices cap the size of any piece, ideally so the code on screen is "smaller than the coder's melon". But they can be orphaned when you downsize, and two services per developer overloads people. Don't adopt them because unicorns do; make sure they solve a problem you'll actually have.
10

Loose clustering: a tree falling in a forest

Losing one instance should matter as little as one tree in a forest. So no instance has a unique role (or any special role exists on several, with leader election), members start and stop in any order, and callers depend only on a DNS name or virtual IP for the whole service. Members shouldn't be configured with each other's identities either, though they may discover each other at runtime for leader election and failure detection. Toggle the wiring, then kill an instance.

11

Explicit context: say who knows

A request carries {"item": "029292934"}. Is that the item or its ID? If an ID, there are only four things you can do with it. Compare a bare ID with a URL.

Inside services too: ActiveRecord's implicit connection makes a second database painful. Global state, configuration included, is the most insidious implicit context; it slows you down whenever "one" collaborator must become "more than one".

12

Create options: flat walls, not curved shells

Try adding a wing to the Sydney Opera House: it's finished, a complete vision with no place to extend. The Winchester "Mystery" House, built round the clock for decades with stairs to ceilings, is incoherent, but every flat wall is a possible door. Modular systems hold more options than monoliths. Baldwin and Clark's Design Rules name six modular operators; each module boundary is an option to apply them later, and the system's economic value rises with the number of options. The running example is a shipping module that works out shipments, cost and arrival. Tap an operator.

Architecture for adaptation, in short
  • Start with loose clusters.
  • Use an evolutionary style (microservices, messages, microkernels, or something that doesn't start with "m"); asynchrony helps, as it does against the stability antipatterns.
  • Make context explicit so services can work with many partners.
  • Create options: leave room for the modular operators.

14

Release is the beginning of true life

Change is what defines software, and that change starts at release. Everything before release is gestation. Systems either grow and adapt to their environment or decay until their costs outweigh their benefits, and die. You make change cheaper and less painful by planning for production releases as an integral part of the software, not by designing for change inside the code while ignoring how that change goes live.
