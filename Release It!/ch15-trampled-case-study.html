Case Study: Trampled by Your Own Customers

Months of load tests said the new site could handle launch day. It fell over within hours. The gap between test scripts and real users, and the session-hungry design that turned customers into a stampede.

1

Launch day: champagne, then a crash

Part IV opens with another war story. A retailer had spent years replacing its whole online store, content management, customer service and order processing, meant to be the backbone for a decade. The team numbered over three hundred, the project was more than a year late, and the author had been in crunch mode for nine months through a Minnesota winter. Six official launch dates, three months of load tests, two management teams, three ever-lower load targets. Then, at 9 a.m., the program manager pressed a literal big red button. Step through the morning.

The button was theatre: it lit an LED in another room, where someone clicked Reload on a browser that bypassed the CDN and went straight to the origin servers. The real switch was a scheduled CDN metadata update at 9:00 that took about eight minutes to spread worldwide.

2

Every website is an integration project

There is no such thing as a website project: each is an enterprise integration project with an HTML face, here a monolithic commerce suite rendering everything on the server. Building the back end alongside the front end could give tighter integration, but more often both sides chase a moving target. That depends on Conway's law (1968): organizations "are constrained to produce designs" that copy their communication structures. An interface only gets built if two people talk about it, and what counts is who actually talks, not the org chart. Toggle a conversation and watch the architecture change.

The author uses the law both ways: prescriptively (set up the communication you want the software to mirror) and descriptively (read the software's structure to discover how people really communicate).

3

Built to pass QA, not to run in production

A brand-new stack without stability patterns is tightly coupled, so the chance the system fails is roughly the chance that any part fails, and nobody knows its capacity or stability. Worse, the teams had built everything to pass testing. Across 15 applications and over 500 integration points, every config file targeted the integration-test environment: hostnames, ports and passwords scattered through thousands of files. Some code assumed the QA topology, though production had extra firewalls and clustered instances where QA had one. Questions like "which repository holds the production configs?" met silence, partly because nobody knew and partly because nobody wanted to be seen answering.

Changing one database password

Ambiguous names hurt.A property called host on a QA box running five apps could mean my hostname, the host allowed to call me, or (the author jokes) the host I use to launder money. You must know which before you can set it for production.
4

Concurrent users can't be counted; sessions can

Marketing required 25,000 concurrent users. But there's no lasting connection to count: just separate requests that the server ties together with an identifier into a session. The user leaves at the start of the dead time, and the server can't tell a user who's gone from one who hasn't clicked yet, so it keeps the session until a timeout after the last click. A session therefore always outlives its user, and counting sessions overstates users. Move the timeout.

Capacity also depends on what users do: if everyone looks at the front page and leaves, you can serve far more than if everyone buys. Active sessions are among the most important numbers for a web system, as long as you don't call them users.

5

Three months on a conference call

Load tests usually run overnight and get analysed the next day. This one needed faster turnaround, so a test manager, a load-test vendor engineer, a dev architect, a DBA and the author sat on a call twelve hours a day for three months. Real traffic can't be reproduced; you use traffic analysis, experience and intuition, then hope the scaling factors hold in a smaller environment. They judged think time, conversion rate, session length and catalog access to matter most, and built a mix they thought was slightly harsher than reality.

Checkout is the costliest path: card verification, address normalization, inventory and available-to-purchase checks, and about 12 pages versus about 7 for a browser.

Capacity in active sessions

The first run locked up every app server at 1,200 users. After 60+ builds, capacity was ten times higher, and over the limit the site went "flaky" instead of crashing. Marketing decided a slow site beat no site, and accepted 12,000 sessions for the quiet season.

6

What really made the sessions

Customers hadn't been told the launch date, so pent-up demand wasn't it. Sessions were. Each one costs RAM, and with replication on, each was serialized and shipped to a backup server after every page, costing CPU and bandwidth too. The load scripts had been polite: real browsers, linked pages, cookies. Production was not. Switch on the real-world sources and compare with the tested capacity.

Sessions created (illustrative)

The numbers in this meter are illustrative, not from the book. The sources are: about 40% of visits came via search engines that sent customers to old URLs, and every old .html request went to an app server, creating a session just to serve a 404. Changed URLs also break links in reviews, blogs and social posts, and cost a lot of search ranking. One spider made up to ten sessions a second because a security mandate put session IDs in query parameters (see Security). Then came about a dozen disguised scrapers that ignored cookies (and robots.txt; the site had none), and "random weird stuff".

7

The testing gap: polite scripts, no safety devices

1. Tests used the app as intended. Request a URL, wait, follow a link from the page, carry cookies. No script hit one URL 100 times a second without cookies, and if one had, someone would probably have called it unrealistic and ignored the crash. Think of the tester joke: orders a beer, 0 beers, 99999 beers, a lizard, -1 beers. Load tests need the same noise and chaos.
2. No circuit breakers.When something went wrong, the app kept sending threads into the danger zone, piling new requests onto hung ones like cars in fog on a freeway. The symptom showed up on the first day of load testing, but it was blamed on the test method rather than seen as a failure to recover from insults. See stability patterns.
8

Aftermath: a gateway page at the edge

In one day, the CDN's engineers used edge scripting to put a gateway page in front of the site with three abilities: send clients that mishandle cookies to a page explaining how to enable them, throttle the share of new sessions let through to the real home page (the rest got a polite "come back later"), and block IPs of bots and floods. For three weeks an engineer watched session counts and pulled back the throttle when needed, because a fully saturated cluster took nearly an hour to recover. By week three it stayed at 100%. Inside the app, more fixes. The home page, served five million times a day, was fully dynamic and needed over 1,000 database transactions and a walk over eighty-odd categories for the menus, yet it was identical every time (personalization was a selling point nobody had used, and most visitors had no ID cookie anyway). A static copy went to unidentified visitors. Lawyers sent cease-and-desist letters to scrapers, which is what those "by viewing this page you agree" terms are for; the bots stopped, but never for long. And session failover was turned off after finding whole shopping carts and up to 500 search results serialized into each session.

9

Nothing is as permanent as a temporary fix

Most emergency fixes lasted years; rolling restarts lasted a decade, through more than a full turnover of the team. And every one cost money. Tap each fix for its price.

The worst part: none of it was necessary. Two years later, the improved software handled over four times the load on fewer servers of the same model. Built that way from the start, the engineers could have joined marketing's party "instead of popping fuses".
