Chaos Engineering

If failures are going to happen anyway, cause them yourself, on your schedule, and learn. From the Simian Army to disaster simulations: how to start injecting chaos safely.

1

Breaking things to make them better

"Hey boss, I'm going to log into production and kill some boxes." That conversation could end with HR, but the idea is radical rather than crazy. The principles of chaos engineering define it as experimenting on a distributed system "to build confidence" that it can withstand turbulent conditions in production. It is empirical: rather than modelling what the system should do, you run experiments to learn what it does. It targets production because staging can't show large-scale behaviour: instance ratios change behaviour qualitatively (see Scaling Effects), congested networks behave differently from quiet ones, and nobody builds a second Facebook as a staging copy of Facebook.

Retry storms, cascading failures, dogpiles, slow responses and single points of failure only show up in the whole system. And like concurrency, safety is not composable. Try the book's example: a client with a 50 ms timeout calls providers averaging 20 ms with a 99.9th percentile of about 30 ms.

The distribution here is a simulated stand-in matching the stated mean and tail, not the book's data. Each provider is safe alone; chained, the client starts to look unreliable. That's an emergent property you can only see in the whole.

2

Antecedents: drift, paradoxes and antifragility

Chaos engineering borrows from cybernetics, complex adaptive systems, high-reliability organizations and especially resilience engineering. In Drift into Failure, Sidney Dekker places a system (people, technology and process together) between three boundaries. Pressure for economic return, plus people's natural reluctance to work at their upper limit, form a gradient that pushes operations toward the safety boundary. His airliner example: jets fly faster higher up, so more trips and revenue, but at that altitude the gap between stall speed and the speed where turbulence starts is narrow. Watch the drift, then add a counter-force.

Distributed systems drift the same way: we push throughput to what machines and network can bear, maximally utilized and profitable until a disruption, and then highly efficient systems tend to break all at once. Chaos engineering is the balancing force: optimize for availability and tolerance of disruption in a hostile world, not throughput in an ideal one. Tap the other threads.

3

The Simian Army

The best-known example is Netflix's Chaos Monkey: every so often it picks an autoscaling cluster and kills an instance. The cluster should recover by itself; if it doesn't, the owning team has a problem to fix. It was born in the move to AWS and microservices, when more components meant more ways to lose availability. Netflix chose "and" rather than "either/or": stability patterns to help instances survive, and a system that survives losing them, because no code stops AWS terminating an instance. Real terminations were common enough to hurt at scale but too rare to test every deployment, so they needed failure to happen more often until it was routine: "if something hurts, do it more often". Each new monkey has improved overall availability. Also, as Heather Nakama noted at the third Chaos Community Day, people really like the word "monkey".

Opt in or opt out?

Before you adopt your own monkey

When Chaos Monkey launched, developers were surprised by how much it found, even in long-running services: subtle config problems, and cluster membership lists that grew forever with old IPs (or worse, IPs that came back as a different service). Before you start, tick off what you have.

Designing the experiment

Good measurements and an A/B system that can tag requests as control or test are in place. It's still not time to kill boxes randomly. Step through the design.

6

Three kinds of injection

Knowing the system's structure, you choose where to kill an instance, add latency or fail a call. Each is an injection. Pick a kind, then tap a target in the call tree for one request.

How FIT works.Netflix's failure injection testing (not the same FIT as the test framework in Handling Versions) tags a request at the edge, at an API gateway say, with a cookie that says "this request will fail when G calls H". At that call site, G reads the cookie and reports failure without even making the call. It works because all of Netflix's outbound calls go through one common framework that propagates the cookie.
Nora Jones on introducing chaos to your neighbours. As the first tools engineer at a new e-commerce startup releasing several times a day (and getting paged at 4 a.m. on Saturdays), she emailed everyone that chaos experiments were starting in QA and owners could opt out "unsafe" services. Hardly anyone replied; after weeks of nagging she took silence as consent. QA went down for a week, and she met everyone in the company. Her moral: a quick way to meet colleagues, "but it's not a great way". Control failures delicately, especially the first time.
7

Targeting: from random shots to search

Random targeting is how Chaos Monkey works, and early on it's as good as anything: most software has so many problems that random shots find something alarming. After the easy fixes, it becomes a search problem: finding faults that lead to failures, when on any given day most faults don't. A Tuesday partner data load that writes bad data, which later makes an API return 500, is unlikely to be found at random. The space grows exponentially with the number of service-to-service calls. Humans narrow the search by reasoning about how a successful request works: knock out one support of its call tree and learn either way. That's why faults that didn't cause failures deserve study too: something stopped them. People still beat computers at this abductive reasoning, but researcher Peter Alvaro (UC Santa Cruz) is building what he calls a "cunning malevolent intelligence": collect traces of normal traffic, infer which services each request type needs as a graph, cut links via an experimentation platform, and record whether a fallback appeared or the link simply wasn't crucial. A few iterations shrink the search space drastically.

8

Automate, repeat, and keep it boring

Chaos should be boring: at best, the system just keeps running. When you do find a weakness, fix that instance, then look for the same class of problem elsewhere and automate a test for it. With automation comes moderation: don't kill the last instance in a cluster, and don't fail G→H while also failing every fallback G uses when H is down. Dedicated chaos teams build platforms that decide how much chaos, when, to whom and which services are off limits, so that one unlucky customer isn't in every experiment. Netflix's is ChAP, the Chaos Automation Platform. Such platforms decide what and when, leave "how" to existing tools (Ansible is popular, needing no agent on target nodes), and report each test to monitoring so effects can be correlated.

9

Disaster simulations: the zombie apocalypse

People are part of the system. They get ill, break bones, have family emergencies, quit without notice; disasters can close a building or a city. What happens when your single point of failure goes home every evening? High-reliability organizations run drills, from company-wide business-continuity exercises down to small ones. The fun version: randomly mark a share of staff as zombies for the day. They needn't eat brains, but they must stay away and not answer messages. Record what can't get done, review it like a postmortem, and fix it with documentation, role changes or automation.

Safety notes from the book. Don't combine fault injection with your first zombie drill. Once a normal day works without people, add an abnormal situation at, say, 20% zombies. And agree a code word meaning "this is not part of the drill", in case a learning opportunity turns into an existential crisis.
Wrapping up. Chaos engineering starts from paradoxes: stable systems become fragile, and dependencies and failure modes creep in whenever you look away. Break things regularly, in a semi-controlled way, so the software and the people who build it become more resilient.
