Stabilize Your System

A stable system keeps processing transactions even when its parts misbehave. This chapter gives you the vocabulary: impulses and stress, longevity, failure modes, crack propagation and the chain from fault to failure.

1

Enterprise software must be cynical

New software is like a fresh graduate: optimistic, and about to meet a world that doesn't follow the lab's script. Cynical software expects bad things and is never surprised by them. It doesn't fully trust other systems, or even itself, so it builds internal barriers. The airline's shared service was not cynical enough.

Instability costs money directly (lost sales; the book's retailer loses a million dollars per hour of downtime off-season) and indirectly (customers who leave for good, damage to the brand). Try the "wasted marketing" arithmetic.

Stability is usually cheapAt many decision points, two designs both pass QA. One leads to hours of downtime a year, the other doesn't, and they often cost the same to build.
2

Transactions, systems, impulses and stress

A transaction is a unit of work the system exists to do ("customer places order"), not a database transaction. A system is everything needed to process transactions for users. A robust system keeps processing them despite transient impulses, persistent stress or component failures. The point is not that servers stay up but that users can still get work done.

The words come from mechanical engineering. An impulse is a sudden shock: a flash mob, a celebrity post, millions of messages dumped in a queue at midnight. Stress is a force applied over time, like a slow payment processor. Stress produces strain, which shows up somewhere else entirely.

3

Longevity: the bugs that need time

A system with longevity keeps working for a long time; a useful definition of "long" is the time between deployments. The big threats are memory leaks and data growth. Neither shows up in development (servers live for an hour) or QA (restarted daily) or load tests (which stop after a few hours). Set a leak rate and a deploy interval and see who wins.

Run your own longevity testSet aside a machine and drive steady, modest traffic for days. Let the script go quiet for a few hours a day to mimic night: that is when connection pool and firewall idle timeouts bite. If you skip this, production becomes your longevity test by default.
4

Failure modes and crumple zones

Under an impulse or strain, some component always fails first. James R. Chiles calls these "cracks in the system", like a microscopic crack in a steel plate that spreads faster and faster until the plate shatters. The trigger, the way the crack spreads and the resulting damage together form a failure mode.

You will have failure modes whether you design them or not. Accept that, and you can choose them: build crumple zones that fail first and protect what matters, "crackstoppers" in Chiles's term. If you don't design your failure modes, you get whatever dangerous ones emerge.

5

Stopping crack propagation at the airline

The airline's crack started at one unhandled exception. It could have been stopped at many levels, from a pool setting to the whole architecture. Turn on crackstoppers and watch how far the crack gets.

The more tightly coupled the architecture, the further an error travels. Loosely coupled designs (message queues where callers know a reply may never come, or callers reading from a shared space that the service keeps populated) act as shock absorbers.

Chain of failure

Every outage is a chain: one event makes the next more likely. Reliability engineers separate the links. A fault is a condition that creates an incorrect internal state (a latent bug, a dead socket). An error is visibly wrong behavior that results. A failure is when the system stops doing its job for users. Each link is a place to break the chain. Step through the airline's chain.

Each incident's exact chain is unique, but patterns recur: brittleness along a certain axis, a tendency to amplify one way. Those recurring shapes are the stability antipatterns, and their antidotes are the stability patterns. The patterns can't prevent cracks; they stop them spreading.
