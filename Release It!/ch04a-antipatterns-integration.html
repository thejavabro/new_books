Stability Antipatterns I: Points of Contact

Most outages are not exotic. They follow a handful of patterns that show up again and again. The first half of the antipatterns: the places where systems touch each other, and the ways that contact turns deadly.

1

Life on the technology frontier

We build software vastly faster than in 1968, yet the "software crisis" never ended, because the challenges grew faster: millions of users, applications made of hundreds of continuously redeployed services, everything integrated with everything. Even five nines leaves thousands of unhappy users a day at that scale.

Borrowing again from James R. Chiles, big systems live at the "technology frontier", where two forces meet. High interactive complexity: so many hidden linkages that operators' mental models are wrong, and their instinctive fixes can make things worse. Tight coupling: a failure in one part shifts load and delay onto its neighbors, making their failure more likely, and so on to collapse. Every antipattern on this page and the next creates, accelerates or multiplies cracks. Faults will happen; what matters is what happens after.

2

Integration points: the number-one killer

Almost every project is an integration project. Context diagrams look like a butterfly (one system, many feeds in and out, about 2N connections) or a spiderweb (many services, up to N² connections). One retailer's site needed a dedicated integration project manager and a database just to list its feeds, and every synchronous feed caused at least one outage. Every socket, process, pipe or remote call can and will hang.

Fast failures and slow failures at the socket

TCP opens a connection with a three-way handshake. If nobody listens on the port, a reset comes back in milliseconds: a fast failure, easy to handle. The trouble is the slow failures. Pick a scenario.

A slow response is worse than no responseA blocked thread can't serve other work. Default connect timeouts are often minutes and default reads can block forever. When every thread is blocked, the server is, for all practical purposes, down.
3

The 5 a.m. problem: a firewall that forgets

A site of about thirty app servers hung at almost exactly 5 a.m. every day, just as East Coast traffic arrived. Thread dumps showed every thread inside the database driver's socket calls; packet captures showed requests leaving and nothing coming back. The database itself was idle and healthy. Step through the chain.

Drill through two layers of abstractionThe fix came from a DBA: the database's dead-connection detection pings each client periodically. It was meant to spot crashed clients; here, the ping simply kept the firewall's "last packet" time fresh. Not every problem can be solved at the layer where it shows up. Packet capture (tcpdump, Wireshark) is how you see what is really on the wire, with permission.
4

HTTP protocols and vendor libraries

JSON over HTTP rides on sockets, so it inherits every problem above, then adds its own. Tap each way a provider can hurt you.

Use a client library with fine-grained control of connect and read timeouts, and treat responses as plain data (maps and lists) until you've checked them, instead of mapping them straight into domain objects. Vendor client libraries deserve extra suspicion: they are ordinary code, often closed, and full of blocking. A classic trap is a library that calls your callback while holding its own lock; if your code takes another lock in a different order, you get a deadlock you cannot see.

// the hidden deadlock in a vendor callback
thread 1: vendor.lock(A) -> yourCallback() -> lock(B)
thread 2: yourCode.lock(B) -> vendor.send()  -> lock(A)
// each holds what the other needs, forever

Chain reactions

A chain reaction happens within one horizontally scaled layer. Several identical servers share the load behind a load balancer. If one fails, its share moves to the survivors, so each works harder. If the failure is load-related (a leak, a race that shows up under concurrency), the extra load makes the next server more likely to fail, and so on down the line. Remove servers and watch.

Each server tips over above 100% load.

Elsewhere the book links this antipattern to blocked threads (which turn a remote failure into a chain reaction) and to Bulkheads as the defense: partition the layer so a chain reaction in one part can't take every caller down.

6

Cascading failures: cracks jump the gap

A cascading failure is a crack in one layer that triggers a crack in the layer that calls it. Problems cascade upward. The gap is usually jumped by a resource pool drained by calls that never return, or by integration points without timeouts. It can also go the other way: a caller that retries every error hammers a sick provider harder and harder. Services with high fan-in spread trouble widely. Integration points are the number-one source of cracks; cascading failures are the number-one accelerator. Make the database sick and choose how callers behave.

7

Users: the seagull on the hood

Users are the reason systems exist, and they have a knack for doing the worst thing at the worst time. Capacity is the throughput you can sustain with acceptable performance; beyond it are hard limits: heap memory, sockets (about 64,000 ports per IP address), even closed sockets lingering in TIME_WAIT. Every in-memory session lives on for its timeout after the user's last click, holding everything put in it.

8

Blocked threads: running but doing nothing

Managed runtimes rarely crash; instead the process runs happily while every request thread waits for something that will never happen. To the business, "hung" and "crashed" are the same: no revenue. That's why internal monitors need a partner: an external synthetic client that runs real transactions, plus business metrics like "successful logins". Blocked threads gather around resource pools, caches, object registries and calls to other systems, and hangs get more likely with concurrency that developers never test. Try a synchronized cache that calls a slow back end while holding its lock.

DefensesUse proven concurrency libraries rather than home-made pools and queues. You can't prove the absence of deadlocks, but you can make sure none lasts forever: always use the version of a call that takes a timeout. Be wary of third-party code you cannot see.
9

Self-denial attacks: your own flash mob

Sometimes the system, or the organization around it, attacks itself. A marketing email to "a select few" reaches millions within minutes on deal-hunting sites. One retailer announced the exact minute console preorders opened, with a deep link that bypassed its content delivery network; the site was gone in sixty seconds. A single rogue server can do it too, by hammering a shared lock manager. Choose the right protection for each situation.
