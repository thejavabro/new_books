Stability Antipatterns II: Scale and Slowness

Some failures only appear at scale or over time: point-to-point links that grow as n squared, a front end that can drown its back end, synchronized retries, automation that amplifies a mistake, and responses that are slow rather than dead.

Scaling effects: why there are no elephant-sized spiders

Weight grows with volume (n³), leg strength with cross-section (n²), so a giant spider collapses. Software has the same trap wherever there is a many-to-one or many-to-few relationship: a database fine with ten callers dies with sixty. Development runs everything on one machine and QA on one or two, so production sizes are where these effects first appear. They can't be tested out; they must be designed out.

Point-to-point communication

If every instance talks directly to every other, connections grow as the square of the instance count. Slide the farm size.

Point-to-point links, n(n−1)/2
Links via a message broker or pub/sub, n

With two servers, point-to-point is perfectly fine (as long as it doesn't block when the other dies). With tens of servers, switch to broadcast, multicast, publish/subscribe or queues, choosing the simplest thing that works.

Shared resources

A cluster manager, lock manager or "common service" that every member of a layer must call becomes a bottleneck and a risk as the layer grows. The most scalable design is shared-nothing, but that can cost you failover (a session must live somewhere other than the server that dies). You can approximate it by reducing fan-in, for example pairing servers to back up each other's sessions instead of all using one store.

2

Unbalanced capacities: the front end can drown the back end

A front end with 3,000 request threads calls a scheduling service sized for its usual trickle of demand. Then marketing offers free installation for one day, and the fraction of threads calling scheduling jumps. Sizing every service for the worst day would leave it 99% idle for years, so callers and providers must both survive the surge. QA hides this because it is usually two servers calling two servers.

Defenses on both sidesCallers use a Circuit Breaker when responses get slow. Providers use Handshaking and Back Pressure to tell callers to slow down, and Bulkheads to reserve capacity for important callers. Test with skewed ratios: take the most the front end could send, double it, and point it at your most expensive transaction.
3

Dogpile: everyone at once

After a blackout, power used to come back and trip off again within seconds: every fridge and air conditioner started at once. Systems do the same. Servers booting together all hit the database with cold caches; cron jobs fire at midnight; config management pushes to every node in the same second; retries with a fixed interval line up into pulses. Compare a synchronized fleet with one using random slew and backoff.

4

Force multiplier: automation that amplifies mistakes

Automation is a lever: small effort, big movement, in either direction. In August 2016 Reddit was down for about ninety minutes and degraded for ninety more after a clash between people and automation. Step through it.

The same shape appears when a service-discovery node is cut off from the network: it can't tell "everything vanished" from "I'm blindfolded", gossips its belief, and soon the whole cluster reports zero services. Control-plane software senses the current state, compares it to a desired state and acts, so a wrong sensor or a wrong goal turns into large, fast damage. Industrial robots have limits on range, speed and expected resistance; build the equivalent.

Arm the safeguards

5

Slow responses: worse than an error

A fast error frees the caller to move on. A slow response ties up resources in caller and provider alike. Slowness comes from excess demand, memory leaks (CPU busy collecting garbage), contention for connections, or chatty protocols that stall. It spreads upward as a gradual cascading failure, and on websites it even creates traffic: people hit reload. A provider that tracks its own response time can fail fast: when the moving average breaks its promise, refuse new work quickly. Drive the load.

Unbounded result sets

This section is missing from the PDFThe "Unbounded Result Sets" section was a loading placeholder in the source file. The note below is a brief stand-in, not a summary of the book's text.

The name says the danger: a query or API call whose result has no upper limit. It returns ten rows in QA and ten million in production after years of data growth, exhausting memory or stalling the caller. The general defense is to never trust the size of what comes back: put limits in queries and protocols, page through results, and stop reading after a sensible maximum.

-- unbounded: fine with test data, deadly after five years
SELECT * FROM order_lines WHERE customer_id = ?;
-- bounded: ask for one page, then the next
SELECT * FROM order_lines WHERE customer_id = ?
  ORDER BY id LIMIT 100;
