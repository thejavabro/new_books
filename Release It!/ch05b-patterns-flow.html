Stability Patterns II: Control the Flow

The second group of patterns is about flow: let servers say "not now", test against a hostile double, decouple in time, reject excess work, push back on producers, and put a speed limit on dangerous automation.

1

Handshaking: let the server say "not now"

Serial lines, modems and TCP all let the receiver signal when it's ready. Application protocols mostly don't. HTTP has "503 Service Unavailable" for temporary conditions, but most clients treat anything unusual as fatal. Handshaking lets a server protect itself by throttling its own workload instead of accepting whatever arrives. The practical version: each instance's health check tells the load balancer when it's too busy, and the balancer stops sending it work.

Build handshaking into any protocol you design. When calling a service that can't handshake, a Circuit Breaker is the stopgap: you can't ask politely, so you call and track whether it works.

2

Test harnesses: a devious stand-in

Integration test environments only show what your system does when dependencies behave within spec, and they lock the whole company into testing one version at a time. In production everything eventually runs out of spec. A test harness is a separate server pretending to be the remote system, written to misbehave at every layer: network, protocol, application. Unlike a mock object, it isn't bound by the interface. A trick from the book: one harness, different ports, different misbehavior. Choose a port to point your client at.

It should leave scarsA good harness is as nasty as the real world. Its job is to make the system under test cynical. It also lets several developers test failure handling from their own machines without a shared environment.
3

Decoupling middleware

Middleware both integrates systems (passing data and events) and decouples them (removing direct knowledge of each other). Synchronous request/reply, whether RPC or JSON over HTTP, forces both sides to be up at the same time and amplifies shocks. Message-oriented middleware decouples in space and time: the sender doesn't wait, so it cannot be dragged into a cascading failure. The cost is logic: what do you do when the credit-card authorization reply comes back as a failure, or never comes? Slide along the spectrum.

This is an architecture decision that ripples everywhere and is hard to reverse, so make it early, at the last responsible moment. Learn several architectural styles and choose; not everything must be three tiers and a relational database.

4

Shed load: you can't out-scale the world

Public services have zero control over demand; more than a billion devices could call at once. TCP copes by refusing connections when its listen queue is full, but services usually fall over earlier, from contention on pooled resources: threads wait, then run slower because of all the other waiting threads, until callers time out. "Really, really slow" and "down" look the same from outside. So do what TCP does: refuse work when you're too busy, ideally judged against your own SLA, or with a simple cap on concurrent requests.

5

Create back pressure

Every performance problem starts with a queue backing up somewhere. An unbounded queue eats memory, and by Little's law its wait time grows with its length toward infinity. So bound your queues, and decide what happens when one is full. Pick a policy and push a burst through a queue of 10.

Inside the boundary: back pressure. At the edge: shed load.Blocking the producer only works when the consumers are a finite set you control. At the system edge, a blocked thread just frustrates a user or triggers retries. The book's example: an API for creating location tags lets 100 calls per server into the storage engine. Request threads should hand the storage call to another pool, time out, and answer 503 (or queue the command and answer 202). When back pressure kicks in, tell monitoring.
6

Governor: a speed limit for automation

Steam engines could spin until the metal tore apart, so engineers added governors to cap the speed. Automation has no judgment and goes wrong fast; by the time a human notices, it's a recovery, not an intervention. After its outage, Reddit's autoscaler was limited to shutting down only a percentage of instances at a time. A governor is stateful (knows what it did recently), time-aware and asymmetric: shutting down, deleting and blocking are the unsafe directions. Safety is often U-shaped: too few instances hurts availability, too many hurts the budget. Ask an autoscaler for a big change and watch the governor resist.

7

"The odds are astronomical"

The chapter closes with arithmetic. A small service doing ten million requests a day for three years has about eleven billion chances for something to go wrong. A Milky Way's worth of stars is only a few dozen times larger. Astronomically unlikely events happen all the time at scale. Try your own service.
