Case Study: Phenomenal Cosmic Powers, Itty-Bitty Living Space

A new commerce platform survives launch, then starts to wobble on Thanksgiving and nearly collapses on Black Friday. The team treats it like an emergency room: vital signs, diagnostic tests, a specialist, and treatment.

Every industry has its own calendar

The Gregorian calendar fixed a drift so errors are cyclic, not cumulative. But businesses live by their own almanacs: open enrollment for insurers, Valentine's Day for florists, and for retailers the "holiday season". Up to half a retailer's revenue arrives in November and December, starting the day after U.S. Thanksgiving, Black Friday. Store traffic can quadruple; online traffic can jump tenfold. That is the real load test, the only one that matters.

2

Taking the pulse

The new store had launched in summer. Going into the holidays, the team was cautiously optimistic: twice the servers, hard data on where latency started to climb from earlier spikes, and unusual visibility into the app servers. The author had written scripts that screen-scraped the app server's web admin pages to sample every server's vital signs (latency, free memory, busy threads, sessions) and could also set properties and call methods on any component. Watching them daily, the team learned the site's normal rhythm, "what noon on a Tuesday in July looks like".

Humans are great pattern matchersMonitoring catches problems when they happen; a person who knows the normal pulse notices when something just looks wrong. Thanksgiving morning, sessions already matched a normal week's peak, and by midnight the site had taken a whole October's worth of orders. It held.
3

Black Friday: all DRPs red

Friday morning latency was a healthy 250 ms. Then, with the author halfway across town buying curry ingredients, the operations center called: the synthetic-customer monitor was red on every page-serving instance (DRP). The site was down, losing about a million dollars an hour. Rolling restarts failed instantly, and each host took ten minutes to start. Below are the vital signs twenty minutes in. Compare treatment options

Then the business sponsor mentioned that a newspaper insert that morning offered free home delivery on every online order until Monday. Demand would not ease. The only answer was to stop calling scheduling so much. Luckily the store used a separate connection pool just for scheduling calls (probably an accident of Conway's law), and the code handled a missing connection by showing a polite "delivery scheduling unavailable" message. Try the fix the way it actually unfolded.

Changing config files and restarting every server would have taken over six hours under that load. Reconfiguring and restarting one component took under five minutes once the team knew what to do. All DRPs were green about ninety seconds later. The new one-command script to reset the pool size was used all weekend: the business wanted delivery scheduling back when load was light and throttled to one connection when heavy.

6

Recovery-oriented computing and the lessons

A Berkeley and Stanford research project, Recovery-Oriented Computing, started from four premises: failures are inevitable in hardware and software; modeling can never be complete; you can't predict every failure mode in advance; and human action is a major source of failures. So aim for survivability, for example by restarting components instead of whole servers. Its ideas, ahead of their time in 2005, now feel natural with microservices and containers.
