Interconnect

Instances only become a system when they can find and reach each other. This chapter tours the layer that joins them: names, load balancers, ways to reject load before it kills you, routes and discovery.

1

Solutions at different scales

Standalone instances are castaways; the interconnect layer joins them into a system through traffic management, load balancing and discovery. It's where high availability is really built. The right tools depend on your size: judge each option by the rate of change it supports, the operational support it needs and how much global knowledge it assumes. Describe your organization.

The balance point keeps moving: big companies open-source powerful platforms (Kubernetes, Consul, Spinnaker), and what once needed a platform team gets easier for small teams. Monitoring, once expensive, is now something every team must have.

2

DNS: simple, global, and easy to neglect

With DNS, "discovery" is a human protocol: find the owners, get the name (perhaps for a six-pack), put it in config. Always call a logical service name, even if it's only an alias, so a move changes one record instead of every consumer. Round-robin DNS hands out several addresses for one name; cheap, but every instance must be routable, DNS knows nothing of health or load, and long-running clients often cache the first address forever. Try it.

Where DNS shines: global server load balancing

Specialized DNS servers at each location track the health of their pools and answer only with addresses of healthy ones, preferring the location nearest the caller. After resolution, DNS is out of the picture; the local load balancer handles the actual traffic. Pick the client's location and a site outage.

DNS availabilityIf nobody can resolve your name, your uptime doesn't matter. Host DNS away from your production infrastructure, use more than one provider in different places, and put your status page on yet another.
3

Active load balancing

An active load balancer sits between callers and instances. It listens on virtual IPs, each mapped to a pool with a policy: algorithm, health checks, stickiness, and what to do when no member is healthy. Clients shouldn't be able to tell it's there; instances must generate URLs with the VIP's name, and log the client from X-Forwarded-For, since the source address is now the proxy. Configure a pool.

Software (reverse proxy)HAProxy, nginx, Apache, Squid. Cheap, easy, can cache responses. In every request path, so it can become the bottleneck.
HardwareLayers 4 to 7, any connection protocol, much higher throughput, can fail a whole site over. Five- to six-figure prices and specialist skills.
Content-based routingSend searches to one pool, signups to another, long queries to their own machines.
4

Demand control: reject work at the edge

The world can crush your system at any time; there's no natural protection. Every failing system starts with a queue backing up: sockets held longer under load (fewer available just when most are needed), full NIC buffers, the listen queue. A single edge request fans out into a tree of internal calls, and once responses pass callers' timeouts, the retries arrive exactly when they hurt most. So shed load as early as possible, ideally at the load balancer, which answers 503 when health checks say every instance is too slow.

Size the listen queue

Response time callers see is residence time: queue wait plus processing. Teams that measure only processing time think all is well while consumers complain. The book's rule of thumb for a starting queue length: (max wait ÷ mean processing time + 1) × threads × 1.5.

Starting listen queue length

When overloaded, a "listen queue purge" (accept and immediately answer a canned 503) beats letting connections age in the queue. For services inside a data center, a shorter TIME_WAIT frees ephemeral sockets faster; TIME_WAIT exists to guard against late stray packets ("bogons").

5

Network routing: consistent errors beat intermittent success

Multihomed hosts pick an interface per destination from a routing table built from advertisements. Usually it just works. But imagine a partner integration carrying personal data over a VPN, while the public network also advertises a route. Depending on the order advertisements arrive, packets go over the VPN, or in cleartext over the internet, and the service appears to work either way.

Another trap: two identical machines that numbered their ports differently, so one sent all its traffic out the admin network. Keep a record of every remote connection (destination name, address, desired route); someone will need it for firewall rules anyway. Static routes or software-defined networking are the usual fixes.

6

Discovering services

Discovery matters when there are too many services to manage by DNS, or instances change constantly (containers do both). It has two halves: instances announce themselves to join pools, and callers look up an address. Discovery is itself a service that can fail, so clients should cache results briefly, and nobody should build their own.

7

Migratory virtual IP addresses

"Virtual IP" means two things. Load balancers use VIPs to put many services on few interfaces. Clusters use a migratory VIP: an address that moves to the standby node when the active one dies (new MAC address, advertised by ARP). Clients connect by the VIP's name and never notice which node holds it, but in-memory state and uncommitted transactions are lost. Some drivers re-run queries; updates can't be blindly replayed. Expect an exception in an odd place, and retry against the new node within a circuit breaker's limits.
