Stability Patterns I: Contain the Damage

The antipatterns have antidotes. The first group of stability patterns stops cracks from spreading: trip a breaker, partition capacity, clean up after yourself, refuse doomed work early, and restart small pieces cleanly.

1

A recovery-oriented mindset

Each antipattern has antidotes. None of these patterns will help you pass QA; they help you sleep after launch. And more patterns is not better: "number of patterns applied" is never a quality metric. Expect failures, and apply patterns where they reduce the damage from one. This page covers the patterns that contain damage; the next covers patterns that control flow.

2

Timeouts

This section is missing from the PDFThe "Timeouts" section, the first stability pattern, was a loading placeholder in the source file. This stand-in draws only on how the rest of the book uses timeouts.

Across the book, timeouts are the partner of almost every other pattern: the antipatterns pages say "defend with Timeouts" for integration points, blocked threads and cascading failures, and Circuit Breaker "works so closely with timeouts" that breakers often count timeouts separately. The idea is simple: never wait forever. Every network call, every pool checkout and every lock acquisition gets an upper bound, so a thread can always come back and do something useful, even if that something is returning an error.

3

Circuit breaker: don't do it if it hurts

Fuses burn out before the house does; circuit breakers do the same and can be reset. In software, wrap a dangerous call in a component that stops making the call when it keeps failing. Unlike a retry, a breaker exists to prevent calls. Closed: calls pass through and failures are counted. Too many failures in a short time: open, calls fail immediately. After a cooling period: half-open, one trial call goes through; success closes the circuit, failure opens it again. Play with it.

4

Bulkheads: save part of the ship

A ship's watertight compartments stop one hole from sinking it. Partition capacity so one failure can't take everything. Two critical systems, Foo and Bar, share a service Baz. Let Foo go rogue.

5

Steady state: no fiddling

Every human login to a production server is a chance for an "ohnosecond": one engineer meant to resync a disk mirror and copied the empty new disk over the good one. Keep people off production; the system should run at least one release cycle without hands-on care. Usually the reason people log in is sludge. For every mechanism that accumulates a resource, another must recycle it. Balance the drains.

Purge data with application logic (it knows the referential rules), get logs off hosts to a central server, rotate by size, and cap every cache. Ask of each cache: is the key space bounded, and do items change? If unbounded, you need a size limit and invalidation; a periodic flush is often enough.

6

Fail fast

If slow responses are bad, a slow failure is the worst: all the waiting, none of the result. If you can tell in advance that a request will fail, say so at once. Like a chef's mise en place, check everything you'll need before you start: validate input, check out connections, look at the circuit breakers on the integration points you'll call. A photo printing company's old renderer "printed" all-black images when an asset was missing; checking every font, image and mask up front, and preallocating memory, dropped software-caused remakes to zero. (The one thing not checked, disk space, was the one thing that later failed late.)

7

Let it crash

Error recovery is hard and unreliable. The cleanest state a program ever has is right after startup, so sometimes the best path to system stability is to give up on component stability: crash the broken piece and start a clean one. This needs three things. Limited granularity: a boundary for the crash, such as an actor in Erlang or Akka, or a whole small service instance. Fast replacement: microseconds for actors, milliseconds for a container, but not minutes for an old monolith on a VM. Supervision: a supervisor (not the caller) decides whether to restart one child, all children, or crash itself and escalate, and it backs off if restarts come too densely.
