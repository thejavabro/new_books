Fix Early and Deploy a Quick Fix

Bugs get more expensive with age, so fix them while the context is fresh. And when the clock wins, a revert or a workaround buys time, as long as you do not stop there.

1

A bug gets more expensive every week

Fix bugs as soon as the schedule allows. Right after you touched a system you still hold its context in your head and may guess the cause from the description alone; months later you, or your successor, must rebuild that context from scratch. Meanwhile other code, assets and even users' saved data start to depend on the buggy behaviour, testers trip over it again and again, and every release ships it to more people. Drag the slider.

2

Assign reasonable priorities

You cannot fix everything instantly, so the team needs a bug process. Blockers (users or developers who cannot continue) beat everything. For the rest, weigh severity against how many people it hits and how often, and remember the hidden costs: a bug that keeps tripping QA wastes their time daily and may mask other bugs behind it, and visible annoyances hurt the product's reputation. Interrupting your current task has a price too, so batch small bugs sensibly. Tap a bug to place it.

3

Context walks out of the door

People leave projects, change teams or simply get busy, and they take their context with them. Spread knowledge on purpose: several people on each essential system, design discussions in stand-ups, regular tech talks, rotating responsibilities, and thorough written and spoken handovers when someone leaves. The bus factor is how many people could disappear before a system has nobody who understands it. Toggle who knows what, then remove someone.

4

When other things depend on the bug

The longer a bug lives, the more things are built to compensate for it. Runtime logic fixes itself when the root cause goes; assets that only look right because of the bug must be repaired separately; and persistent data (saved files, documents, users' data in formats you published) may need migration even after the fix. Keep transient and persistent data clearly apart.

One project had accumulated two input dead-zone implementations stacked on top of each other. A colleague spent days tuning before realising one layer simply had to go.

And sometimes users love the bug. Combos in a famous fighting game began as an accident and became a genre staple. Keeping a bug as a feature is risky, though: unintended behaviour is unstable and can break with any change, so if you keep it, re-implement it on purpose.

Take ownershipIf you built the system, help prioritise its bugs even when someone else fixes them, and watch the tracker and the hallway for complaints about it. Claim a bug you overheard as soon as it is reported. Reserving a fixed share of your time for bug fixing keeps them from piling up into technical debt.
5

When the clock wins: deploy a quick fix

Sometimes users are blocked right now and the proper fix is days away. A quick fix restores service while you keep working. Two families: undo the offending change, or work around the symptom. Pick the situation to see the fitting move.

Undo first, ask laterA colleague's asset-conversion tool gained a "clean the target folder" feature. If the tool was run from the wrong place, it cleaned the user's Documents folder instead. The only sensible move: revert the commit, rebuild, redistribute, and warn everyone. Likewise, if a colleague's last commit before a holiday breaks things, reverting and waiting for them is usually faster than decoding it alone.
6

The workaround toolbox

A workaround masks symptoms without fixing the root cause. That can backfire: users meet strange disabled buttons or cryptic errors and file new bugs. Comment every workaround clearly. Options, roughly from gentle to brutal:
