Ask Colleagues, Search All Files, Collect Crash Dumps

Somebody may already know the answer, the text on screen is probably somewhere in your files, and a crash from a user's machine can still be inspected later if the program saved a dump.

1

When to ask a colleague

Big projects are too specialised for anyone to know everything. When your bug touches someone else's area, a few minutes of their time can save you days. Investigate a little first, because what you learn makes your questions sharper and saves them from retracing your steps. But do not wait too long: the book's rule of thumb is at least 20 minutes on your own, then, if you are completely stuck, ask. Asking too early beats asking too late.

Making progress?
Two stories from the bookA graduate who asked questions constantly became one of the team's strongest programmers fast. And a junior spent a day or two digging through a 3D renderer for a depth-sorting bug while the renderer's author, working nearby, had already fixed it locally. Open communication catches mismatches like that.
2

How much to tell them

Say too little and they have nothing to work with. Say everything, theories included, and you may overwhelm them or pull them into your own wrong assumptions. People differ; learn what your colleagues prefer. When the conversation stalls, trickle out information: facts you are sure of first, then guesses and suspicions, until they have something to contribute.

Be approachable in return. Helping is part of the job, not a distraction from it, and if people keep asking about the same part of your system, that tells you what to make clearer next time.

3

Asking outside your company

Go from the most specific channel to the most general. Then help them help you: a detailed report and, above all, a minimal reproducible example. Expect triage ("have you tried turning it off and on?") and replies that take days or weeks, so this suits non-urgent bugs. Respect NDAs; on public forums consider a pseudonym.

Use your networkVisibility speeds things up: vendors care about prominent products, and public questions add a little pressure. A champion inside the vendor helps most. One team's renderer mangled even simple cubes, only on one GPU brand. A colleague had recently met an engineer from that vendor; a Friday-night email with a minimal repro got a precise answer by Monday.
4

Search everything, cleverly

Text connects the pieces of a program, and the program produces text, so searching for it leads back to the source. Pick the right tool (IDE symbol search, an editor, ack or ag for code, Everything or fzf for file names) and check its configuration: many tools skip large or binary files by default, which may be exactly where the answer is. Regular expressions widen a search when you are not sure of the exact spelling. Try them on this small codebase.

5

The string you see is not the string in the code

Messages are usually assembled from pieces: a format string plus numbers, names, dates and user input. Search for the full text on screen and you find nothing. Search for the fixed fragment instead, leaving out anything that looks generated. Also remember the text may be translated.

6

Search beyond the source code

And make your own code searchable: Pos matches half the codebase, cameraOrbitPivotPos matches one thing. Where a name cannot be unique, add a distinctive comment marker; projects that must mark changes in shared code can use start and end tags with team, author and reason.

7

Crash dumps: debug a crash after the fact

A crash dump is a snapshot written when the program dies, which you can load into a debugger later on any machine. It is nearly as good as catching the crash yourself, and it works for crashes on testers' and users' devices. Most crashes are hardware exceptions you cannot recover from: access violations, illegal instructions and division by zero, usually caused by a pointer holding the wrong address. Programs can also raise exceptions deliberately, for example from an assertion, to force a dump.

Call stack from a user's dump

Shipped builds are stripped of debug information, so keep the matching symbol files for every build you hand out: names and addresses, file and line mapping, type layouts and build details. Third-party libraries need theirs too (watch the size; one framework's plug-in symbols topped 60 GB).

Limits: a dump is not interactive (no stepping back to see how things evolved), and optimised code may not match the source line by line. Windows offers full dumps (all memory) or mini dumps (only what the stack references), with options in between. Some GPUs have their own crash dumps.

8

From one dump to actionable data

Add dump support early, not just before release, so testers can attach dumps to reports. The handler runs after a catastrophe, so it must rely on almost nothing; the more robust design is an out-of-process handler that a separate process runs when the main one dies. Then make dumps easy to get: upload them, group crashes by location, and look at the trends.

Check your platform first (several offer crash reporting, and Windows Error Reporting can collect dumps for signed programs), then open-source libraries such as Breakpad and Crashpad, or commercial services that add dashboards and statistics.
