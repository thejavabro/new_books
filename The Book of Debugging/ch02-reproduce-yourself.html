Bug Reports and Reproducing the Bug Yourself

Every investigation starts by seeing the bug with your own eyes. That begins with a report that asks for the right facts, and continues with you following the steps exactly, then changing them on purpose.

1

What goes into a great bug report

The best possible report walks you straight to the problem: follow the steps in a debugger, watch it happen, spot the faulty line. Most reports are not that good, but you can push them in that direction by telling reporters exactly what you need. End users have little expertise and little patience, so ask them only for essentials; professional QA teams have checklists, but they still need to hear what matters most to your project.

2

Symptoms, not theories

The single most important field asks for what the reporter observed, described objectively. A reporter's guess about why it happened can bias whoever investigates and send them in the wrong direction. Expected behaviour sits right next to it, because it reveals the cases where the program is working as intended and the reporter was simply surprised.

Some programs even fake bugs on purpose, such as a game that simulates screen corruption or a crash screen. If yours does, tell outside testers, especially platform holders whose failure reports can delay a release.

3

Five questions the repro answers

Reproducing the bug yourself is the most direct start: no special tools or setup, just use the program. It also answers questions that no report can settle for you.

Does it really exist?Perhaps a colleague already fixed it, or it came from a misunderstanding.
Is it intentional?Surprising behaviour is not always wrong behaviour.
How exactly does it show?You know the internals; you may notice what the reporter could not.
What triggers it?Which steps and conditions are truly required.
How often?Always, often, rarely, once: this sets how you will test a fix.
Seeing it beats reading about itA report said a network connection dropped when an input device was unplugged. On paper that points at the network code. Using the program showed something else entirely: unplugging the device opened a modal dialog, the dialog blocked the network code from running, and the connection timed out. Obvious on screen, nearly invisible from inside the network code.
4

Emulate the reporter, precisely

Follow the steps as closely as you can and make sure you understand each one. Shortcuts and tools that jump ahead are fine, but if the bug vanishes when you skip a step, that step matters and you need to know why. Then match the reporter's world. Tick off everything you have matched:

Chance your environment matches theirs

If nothing works, start over and do it all in one sitting, from a fresh install, including any waiting the report mentions. If the reporter can still reproduce it easily and you cannot, ask them to show you their procedure, then to do it on your debugging device. They may press buttons in a way you cannot copy, or perform a step they forgot to write down.

5

Introduce variations: find the step that matters

Once the bug shows up, play with it. Skip steps to find the essential ones, reorder them to discover dependencies, change the timing, try the same feature somewhere else, or avoid the trigger entirely and see whether a different symptom appears. The aim is a minimal set of steps, and ideally the one action responsible.

Shrink the repro

A search page sometimes shows no results. Here are the reported steps. Toggle steps off, move them up or down, change the pause, and run. The bug has one hidden rule. Find the shortest sequence that still reproduces it every time.

6

Beware of networking complexity

A bug that needs several connected instances, or several people acting in a particular rhythm, is far harder to reproduce. First confirm the network really is needed. If it is, climb down this ladder as far as you can before asking colleagues for their time.

When you need people, bring a planWrite down which variations of the steps you want to try, which alternative reproductions to fall back on, and, if you are already fixing, which fixes to test. Hand it out with the bug report so nobody's time is wasted.
