The Workflow

Debugging without a plan means poking around until an hour has gone and you are not sure what you ruled out. The book replaces that with a loop of four steps (reproduce, probe, examine, fix) and a list of root causes worth recognising on sight.

1

Why bother fixing bugs at all?

The book uses a deliberately loose definition: a bug is anything that does not behave as intended. That covers crashes, but also slowness, visual glitches, broken audio, data leaks and features that simply feel wrong. With a definition that wide, the question is less "is this a bug?" than "why should it be fixed now?". Six reasons come up again and again. Open each card.

3

Debugging is your job

Whether a bug reaches you from a tester, a user or your own screen, the first move is mental: accept that it is now yours to fix. Blaming the compiler, the user or a colleague is tempting, but code written for one specific project is far more likely to be wrong than a widely used third-party system that thousands of others have already exercised.

The second trap is pushing back: the report is vague, the area is unfamiliar, or "that cannot possibly be broken". If someone saw it, it is probably real. A thin report is a reason to go and gather more information, not to close the ticket.

Where is the bug most likely to be?

A rough intuition, not a measurement: the less a piece of code has been exercised by other people, the more suspicion it deserves.

4

The loop: reproduce, probe, examine, fix

A workflow does three things for you: it makes sure you collect every essential detail, it splits an open-ended problem into steps you can concentrate on one at a time, and it tells you when you are actually done. The book's workflow has four steps. You always start with the first; after that you will move back and forth, and the loop only ends when the bug can no longer be reproduced.

Probe and examine are a pairProbing looks at the big picture to narrow your focus quickly, like finding the loose end of a tangled thread. Examining zooms right in on one spot. In practice you alternate between them in short iterations until the trail ends at the root cause.
5

From symptom to root cause, one link at a time

The root cause is often several hops away from the symptom, and the hops can cross different kinds of material: a UI asset, a function, a data file, a tool that produced that file. If you stop at the first spot where you can make the symptom go away, you have only masked it. Walk the trail below and watch where the "fix" would land at each depth.

How do you know you have arrived? Ideally you understand exactly how the bug comes about and how it produces its symptoms. When there is no time for full understanding, the practical definition is the spot where a change fixes the bug and no obvious deeper cause remains.

6

Handle bugs as a team

QA

Test early, write it all down

A good tester records exactly what they did, cuts the steps down to the minimum, and reports usability, accessibility and spec gaps as well as crashes.

Tracker

One place for everything

Every report goes through the bug tracker, and so does most of the conversation about it, so the bug and its discussion stay together.

History

Reopen, don't refile

When the same bug comes back, reopen the old report. Whoever picks it up inherits the earlier investigation and attempted fixes.

When a fix is out of reach because you lack the context or the skills, the right move is to find the person who should be working on it and hand it over, with everything you learned.

Floating-point drift you can see

One cause in the list is easy to demonstrate. A 32-bit float carries about seven significant digits, so far from the origin, small steps get swallowed. An object at position 10,000,000 that moves by 0.1 per frame may not move at all. Switching to a 64-bit double is often a quick way to buy breathing room.

9

Manage expectations, including your own

Debugging is stressful and hard to estimate. Users expect quick answers, and only trivial bugs or lucky guesses deliver them. The advice: under-promise and over-deliver, find a place where you can concentrate, and when you are stuck, stop and sleep on it. The hopeful part is that computers are deterministic. However tangled the path, every bug has a real cause, connected to its symptoms by a chain you can follow.
