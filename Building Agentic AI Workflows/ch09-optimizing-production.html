Optimizing AI Models for Production

The last mile is all trade-offs: bits per weight against accuracy, a small model against a large one, latency against cost. These are the levers, and the experiments that tell you which to pull.

How much GPU memory does a model need?

Production needs more than accuracy: speed, cost and data governance. The first constraint is usually GPU memory (VRAM). Rule of thumb: parameters × (bits ÷ 8) × 1.2, where the 1.2 is a finger-in-the-wind 20% headroom for activations and runtime overhead. An 8B model at FP16 works out to about 19.2 billion bytes, roughly 17.9 GB.


      

      
Estimated VRAM to load

    

  

  
Running data adds more on top. In the book's measurement, an 8B model at 16 bits peaked around 26 GB for a single query, against about 6 GB once quantized to 4 bits; for a batch of 32, roughly 30 GB against 9 GB.






  
2
Quantization: fewer bits per weight

  
Quantization stores each weight in fewer bits: 32 → 8 cuts memory 75%, and 4 bits halves it again. It isn't just chopping decimals. Algorithms such as NF4 (NormalFloat4, from the QLoRA work that pairs it with LoRA) map weights onto a small set of levels chosen to fit their typical bell-shaped distribution. You need the weights to do it, it's quick, and it runs models on small GPUs or even CPUs (Ollama runs quantized models fully offline). The price: a different model, so some accuracy loss is near-certain, commonly 5–15% on benchmarks, more on maths and reasoning. Fine-tuning can win some back.

Distillation: a small student copies a big teacher

  
Distillation trains a smaller student to reproduce a larger teacher's outputs. Task-agnostic distillation copies general language modelling, giving a lean new base model (BERT → DistilBERT). Task-specific distillation trains on one narrow labelled task. A common recipe chains them: distill a 9B teacher into an ~800M general student, then distill again on core task data. Distilled models can then be quantized as well.

Case study: speculative decoding

  
A small assistant model drafts several tokens the normal way; the big base model checks the whole draft in one forward pass instead of one pass per token. Matching tokens are kept, and at the first mismatch the base model supplies its own token and drafting restarts. The output distribution is unchanged; the speed-up depends on how often the two agree. Disagree enough and it's slower than not bothering.

Alignment is everythingOn policy questions the untuned assistant kept drafting plausible-sounding wrong text that the fine-tuned base kept rejecting, so it ran slower. Distilling the fine-tuned base into its own assistant should help. In practice speculative decoding is mostly used with large foundation models; for a 10–25% gain it can be simpler to add another GPU.

Case study: a phone bot that answers in under a second

  
Audio-to-audio models exist but weren't ready: hard to customise voices, and quality drifts over long calls. So the phone bot grounds in text (Chapter 6): Twilio streams call audio over WebSockets; after a pause of more than 500 ms the buffered audio goes to speech-to-text; the text goes to an LLM (possibly an agent); the reply goes to text-to-speech and streams back to the caller.

  

    
Latency budget for one reply

    

    

      
      
      
    

    

  

  
To pick providers, a script sent test phrases through every TTS and then every STT model, timing each leg (seconds per word) and scoring accuracy as 1 − word error rate on the round trip. Groq's hosted models were fastest on both sides; a distilled Whisper for STT was about 7.5× faster than a small GPT-4o-class transcriber with barely any accuracy change. Voice quality wasn't judged, only speed. The final stack: distil-whisper (STT), Llama 4 Scout at around 750 tokens/s (LLM) and PlayAI TTS, all on Groq.

Case study: Matryoshka embeddings

  
Last optimisation: embeddings. Normally an embedder always outputs the same length. A Matryoshka embedder is trained so the first 64, 128, 256 or 512 dimensions also work on their own, like nested dolls. Truncate when you need speed and memory; use more when you need fidelity.

  

    

      

        
        

        
Disk, 1M float32 vectors
Search cost vs. 1,024

      

      
Recall@10 on held-out questions (schematic shape of the book's result).

    

    
The book measured a 1M-vector database at 64–1,024 dimensions: going from 1,024 to 64 cut disk use by nearly 20× and search latency sharply, for cosine and dot product alike.

  

  
The data: Victor Hugo's Les Misérables (public domain) standing in for company docs. Chunk it, have an LLM write trivia questions each chunk answers, and you have positive pairs. Negatives come free from multiple negatives ranking loss: within a batch, every other pair's passage counts as a negative for this question.

  

    

    
Green: the true pair, pulled together. Grey: in-batch negatives, pushed apart. Orange: a false negative, another passage that also answers the question but gets pushed away anyway. In-batch negatives are also often too easy; hard negatives mined with a second model teach more, but that model can mislabel too.

  

  
MatryoshkaLoss just applies that loss to the full vector and to each truncated prefix (1,024, 512, 256, 128, 64) and sums them, which pushes the most important information into the first dimensions, much like principal components. The base model was BAAI's bge-large-en-v1.5. Two recipes were compared: masked-language-model pre-training on the raw text (15% of tokens masked) then the embedding fine-tune, versus the fine-tune alone. They came out the same. Probably there was no new jargon to learn in a classic novel; in a jargon-heavy or cross-lingual domain the extra step might pay. Not every idea pans out, and that's fine.

Case study N + 1: what comes next

  
The book closes by zooming out. Shrink a model? Quantize. Speed it up? Maybe speculative decoding. More control? Fine-tune on your own data. Privacy? Self-host. A weird edge case nobody else cares about? Bake it in with domain adaptation. Underneath all of it: the field rewards people who try things and measure them.
