Fine-Tuning AI for Calibrated Performance

Fine-tuning bakes your task into the weights. Done well, the result is smaller, cheaper and faster than a prompted giant, and its confidence scores actually mean something.

1

Fine-tuning: teach the weights, not the prompt

Fine-tuning updates a pretrained model's parameters on examples from your application, so it speaks the domain, follows the style and knows new facts without forgetting the old ones. Two reasons to do it: trust (ground the model, reduce hallucination, get honest confidence) and cost at scale (stop paying a giant model to answer what is really a yes/no question). Off-the-shelf fine-tuning is young: Hugging Face only appeared in 2016, building therapy chatbots, hence the name.

Data is split three ways: train to update weights, validation to watch progress during training, test for one final unbiased estimate. The first case study uses app reviews (about 288,000, rated 1–5 stars) with a stratified 60/20/20 split, so each split keeps the same star distribution. That matters because 5-star reviews dominate.

Split sizes are the book's; class shares are schematic, showing the heavy 5-star skew.

2

Classification versus multiple choice

A very common business need: pick one label from a fixed list (sometimes 2 options, sometimes 90). Today people often prompt a generative model instead, which is really multiple choice: nothing forces the output to be one of your labels, and even structured outputs or fine-tuning only make strays less likely. A fine-tuned classifier adds a small head on top of an encoder that can only output probabilities over your classes, and needs no prompt at all.

Six experiments: a small GPT (GPT-4.1-Nano) with a plain prompt, then with few-shot examples; Nano fine-tuned without and with a system prompt; full GPT-4.1 fine-tuned; and ModernBERT, a modern drop-in replacement for BERT (context 512 → 8,192 tokens, modern attention and position embeddings, fresher data; 350M parameters in the large version). Only ModernBERT gives direct access to its weights.

3

Calibration: does 80% sure mean 80% right?

A model is calibrated when its confidence matches its hit rate. If it's overconfident you'll over-trust it; underconfident and you'll waste effort on human fallback. Calibration is what makes a threshold like "act only above 60%" meaningful. Expected calibration error: bucket predictions by confidence, compare each bucket's average confidence with its actual accuracy, weight the gaps by bucket size, and (for multiple classes) average across classes. Lower is better.

ECE

Dots are buckets (size ∝ how many predictions fall in them); the diagonal is perfect calibration. Shapes follow the book's curves; numbers are simulated.

Reading a dotThe book's 5-shot Nano put about 95% confidence on many 1- and 5-star predictions that were right only about 80% of the time: overconfident at the top. At the bottom it was underconfident. Probabilities for closed models come from the API's logprobs (top 20 tokens at the time), which works here because "1" to "5" always made the top 20.
4

Same accuracy, very different bills

Prompting helped: few-shot raised both accuracy and calibration over the bare prompt. But every fine-tuned model reached about 73% on the test set, far ahead of prompting, and all hugged the diagonal more tightly. ModernBERT's ECE was about 1.7× lower than GPT-4.1's, at almost identical accuracy. 2- and 3-star reviews, the rarest classes, were the worst calibrated for every model. And the fine-tuned Nano didn't need its system prompt: same accuracy, cheaper calls. The weights had learned the task.

Calibration when the model writes free text

Ask two strong chat models for the birthday of San Francisco's current mayor, then "how confident are you, 0–100%?" Both gave wrong dates with 70%+ confidence. Self-reported confidence in free text isn't trained against reality the way a classifier's is. The token probabilities tell the truer story: a peaked distribution means "I've seen this many times"; a flat one means guessing.

Schematic: Einstein's correct month gets nearly all the mass; for the mayor the mass is spread over October, August, December and November, with the true month (February) under 7%. High probability means familiar, not necessarily right.

Case study: domain adaptation on policy documents

Chapter 5's agent looked policies up. Could a model simply know them? Domain adaptation fine-tunes a general model on a curated domain corpus. To see where it fits, recall how chat models are made (tap a stage):

Long documents get chunked: fixed maximum token windows that overlap so no idea is cut in half, each prefixed with the policy's title or URL so the model learns where facts came from (and could cite them).

7

Making it fit: LoRA, 4-bit weights and Unsloth

The model is an 8B-class Qwen3 reasoning model on one rented A100 (about $6/hour, 30–60 minutes). Three tricks make that possible. LoRA freezes the original weights and learns small low-rank matrices added to them; in the book's run 1.42B of 9.61B parameters were trainable. Quantization stores weights in 4 bits instead of 32. Unsloth wraps Hugging Face's trainer with hand-optimised maths for big speed and memory savings.

LoRA (2 × d × r)

The chunks are fed as raw text (no chat formatting) with an end-of-text token after each, imitating stage-1 pre-training data: the goal is for the model to absorb the policy, not to respond to it. Adding knowledge is delicate, so learning rates are small, smaller still for the embedding weights that every token passes through first. Normally you'd also mix in conversational data to protect chat skills; here only new raw text was used, and slow training was enough.

Learning-rate schedule

peak 5e-5 · embeddings 5e-6 · warm-up 10% · cosine decay · 2 epochs

There's no test split here: the point isn't to generalise to a task but to remember a corpus, so the signal is training loss falling and flattening. The real test was the Chapter 5 scenarios. On a Superhost question the base model hallucinated (missing that co-hosted listings don't count); the adapted model cited the correct review, response-rate and cancellation thresholds.

Share of answers graded 3/3 by the same rubric as Chapter 5. The base Qwen3 managed 6.5%. The text says the adapted model matched GPT-4.1-Nano with its lookup tool (23%), with no lookup of its own; the figure caption puts it higher, at over half. The bar shows the conservative reading.
