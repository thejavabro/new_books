A Pragmatic Philosophy

Being pragmatic is an attitude before it is a technique: you own your career, you own your mistakes, you notice rot early, and you keep learning and explaining.

1

It's your life: you have agency

The book opens with what the authors call its most important tip. Developers are in one of the most flexible careers there is: skills in demand, work that crosses borders, often the option to work remotely. Yet many sit and wait for things to improve. The pragmatic response to almost any complaint is a question: why can't you change it?

Tip 3You Have Agency

Pick a complaint, see the moves you have

no move is "wait and hope"

Try to fix things where you are first, but not forever. As a well-known quip in the chapter puts it, you can change your organization, or change your organization.

2

The cat ate my source code: options, not excuses

Pragmatic programmers take responsibility for their career, their learning and their daily work, and they admit ignorance or error without drama. Responsibility is something you agree to: once you accept it, you are accountable for the outcome, including the risks you could have planned for. A vendor letting you down is a risk; a disk dying with no backup is your problem.

You also have the right to say no to a responsibility that is impossible, too risky or ethically dodgy. What you can't do is accept it and then blame the language, the vendor, management or the cat.

Tip 4Provide Options, Don't Make Lame Excuses

Rubber-duck test: excuse or option?

Before you deliver bad news, say it to a rubber duck (or a cat) first. Would it sound reasonable or lame? Sort each line.

When you don't knowFollow "I don't know" with "but I'll find out." You admit the gap and take ownership in the same breath.
3

Software entropy and broken windows

Disorder in software grows the way it does everywhere. People call it software rot or, more optimistically, technical debt, implying it will be paid back. The biggest driver is the project's psychology. One unrepaired broken window, a bad design, a wrong decision, some sloppy code, signals that nobody cares, and the next one comes faster. Neglect accelerates rot more than anything else.

Tip 5Don't Live with Broken Windows

Run a codebase for a year

each window is a module
Policy:
week 0

Every week some damage is random. But each broken window that is left alone makes neighbouring breakage more likely, because "everything else here is junk anyway."

If there's no time to fix it properly, board it up: comment out the offending code, show a "not implemented" message, swap in dummy data. Any action that stops further damage and shows you're on top of it. And in a crisis, act like the firefighters in the book's story who laid down a mat before dragging their hoses over a pristine carpet: don't cause collateral damage just because something is on fire.

4

Stone soup: be a catalyst

You know what needs building, but ask permission for the whole thing and you get committees, budgets and blank stares: start-up fatigue. So do what the soldiers in the folk tale did. Ask for something modest, build it well, show it off, and then mention how much better it would be with one more thing. People find it easier to join an ongoing success.

Tip 6Be a Catalyst for Change
5

Boiled frogs: remember the big picture

The villagers' side of the story is a warning. They stared at the stones and missed everything else. Projects rarely blow up in one go; they overrun a day at a time, drift from the spec one feature at a time, and morale erodes through a pile of small things. That's different from broken windows: there people stop caring; here nobody even notices.

Tip 7Remember the Big Picture

Spot the drift

press Notice when you think it's too hot
Day 0 · scope 100% · schedule +0 days

The chapter suggests a situational-awareness habit: without looking, how many exits does the room have? How many lights? Practise really noticing your surroundings, then do the same for your project.

6

Good-enough software

Perfect, bug-free software is not on the menu. You can, though, discipline yourself to write software that is good enough for users, for future maintainers and for your own peace of mind. "Good enough" does not mean sloppy: requirements, performance, privacy and security still have to be met. It means users get a say in when it's good enough for them.

Tip 8Make Quality a Requirements Issue

Where should the quality bar sit?

pick a product, then a ship date

Know when to stop. Like a painter adding layer on layer until the picture drowns in paint, you can spoil a perfectly good program by over-embellishing it. Many users would rather have something with rough edges today, and their early feedback usually leads to a better product anyway.

7

Your knowledge portfolio

Knowledge and experience are your main professional assets, and they expire, like a warehouse full of bananas. Treat them like an investment portfolio, with the same five rules.

Tip 9Invest Regularly in Your Knowledge Portfolio

Ten-year portfolio simulator

hours a week you invest

Practical goals from the chapter: learn a new language every year, read a technical book a month, read non-technical books too, take classes, join meetups and actually participate, try different environments, and keep up with technology outside your project. When someone asks a question you can't answer, take it as a personal challenge to find out.

8

Communicate!

A good idea is an orphan without effective communication. Treat your native language as just another programming language: plan it, keep it DRY, refine it. The same change sounds completely different depending on who you're telling.

Tip 11English is Just Another Programming Language

Same change, four pitches

"we're moving status updates onto a third-party message broker"
Tip 12It's Both What You Say and the Way You Say It

Documentation: build it in

Generate API docs from comments in the code, but don't comment every line mechanically: that's a second copy to keep in sync, which breaks DRY. Comments should say why: trade-offs, rejected alternatives, the goal. The code already says how.

Tip 13Build Documentation In, Don't Bolt It On
# loop over orders
for o in orders:
    # add 1 to retries
    o.retries += 1
# The payment gateway drops ~1% of calls
# under load; three retries cover it without
# risking double charges (see ADR-12).
for o in orders:
    o.retries += 1

Left: comments that restate the code. Right: a comment carrying knowledge nothing else holds.
