Listen to your lizard brain

Instincts are patterns your nonconscious has absorbed from experience. When coding feels wrong (a task that drags, a piece of code that keeps fighting back, a sense of dread before starting) that feeling is information. Your lizard brain is trying to tell you something, even if it can't put it in words.

Tip 61Listen to Your Inner Lizard
Fear of the blank pageTwo common causes: your lizard brain sees a problem you haven't consciously found, or you fear making mistakes. Both are fine. Lower the stakes and start.
The prototyping hackWrite "I'm prototyping" on a sticky note. Remind yourself prototypes fail and get thrown away. Start with whatever you understand best, and listen: when the doubt resolves, you'll know. Then delete the prototype and write the real code.

This isn't only about your own code. Inherited code was built by people with their own instincts and constraints; when it feels off, read it closely and look for the pattern that's bothering you.

2

Programming by coincidence

A soldier pokes the ground of a field with his bayonet: no mines. Pokes again: nothing. Convinced it's safe, he strides forward. It wasn't. Programmers do the same when code "seems to work" and they move on without knowing why. When it breaks, they can't fix it, because they never knew why it worked.

How to program deliberately

  • Always be aware of what you are doing. Could you explain the code in detail to a junior colleague?
  • Don't code in the dark. If you don't know why it works, you won't know why it fails.
  • Proceed from a plan, even one on a napkin.
  • Rely only on reliable, documented things. If unsure, assume the worst.
  • Document your assumptions (contracts help), and test them: write the assertion.
  • Prioritise the hard, fundamental parts over bells and whistles.
  • Don't be a slave to history: existing code shouldn't dictate future code. Be ready to refactor.

Algorithm speed

Pragmatic programmers estimate resources (time, memory, processor) almost daily, often without writing it down. Big-O notation describes how the worst case grows with input size n, ignoring constants. It's a ceiling, not a stopwatch, but it tells you what happens when the data grows a hundred-fold.

Double n and see whether the times grow as predicted. Real machines add caches, garbage collection and JIT warm-up, which is exactly why you measure.

Tip 64Test Your Estimates

Best isn't always best. For small inputs a simple O(n²) insertion sort can beat a clever O(n log n) one, and the fastest algorithm may not be worth its complexity. Also watch for premature optimisation: make sure an algorithm really is the bottleneck before spending time on it.

4

Refactoring

Software is less like building and more like gardening. You plant to a plan, then some things thrive, some need pruning, some get moved to a sunnier spot. Refactoring, in Martin Fowler's definition, is restructuring existing code, changing its internal structure without changing its external behaviour. Two parts matter: it's done in small steps that don't change behaviour, and it's backed by tests. It is not a rewrite.
