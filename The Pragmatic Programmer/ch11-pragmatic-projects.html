Pragmatic teams

A pragmatic team is small (under roughly 10 to 12 people) and stable: members rarely come and go, everyone knows and trusts everyone else. Every idea in the book so far scales up from one person to a team.

Coconuts don't cut it

After the war, islanders in Melanesia built airstrips, control towers and headphones out of coconut shells and palm fronds, hoping the cargo planes would return. Anthropologists call it a cargo cult. Teams do the same when they copy the artifacts of successful companies without the substance: the authors met a "Scrum" team that held its daily stand-up once a week, with four-week sprints that stretched to eight.

Real practice or coconut?

Tip 87Do What Works, Not What's Fashionable

Context matters: are you really in the same market, at the same size, with the same people and constraints as Spotify or Netflix? Even they didn't follow their current processes while growing. How do you know what works? Try it. Pilot with a small team, keep what helps, discard the rest. One size fits no one well; methodologies are menus, not recipes.

The pragmatic starter kit

Three things every project needs, whatever its methodology: version control, regression testing and full automation. Together they make the rest safe.

Version controlDrives everything. A push triggers build, tests and deployment. Release by tagging; build on dedicated machines, not someone's laptop.
Regression testingRuthless and continuous. Unit, integration, validation and verification, performance. Most of it automated.
Full automationNo manual procedures. People forget steps; scripts don't. Builds, deploys, environment setup: all code, under version control.

Test the tests: use saboteurs

Tests are software too, so they're imperfect. Cause a bug on purpose and check that a test complains. Do it systematically (mutation testing) or at system level (kill services at random, as Netflix's Chaos Monkey does).

Mutation game

def discount(total, member):
    if member and total >= 100:
        return total * 0.9
    return total

State coverage, not code coverage

A one-line function a / (a + b) reaches 100% line coverage with a single test. Yet with inputs from 0 to 999 it has a million states, and exactly one crashes. Lines run is not the same as states tested.

4

Delight your users

Users don't really want code. They have a business problem and want it solved within their goals and budget. Their expectations aren't even in the spec. So ask one question: how will you know we've all been successful a month (or a year) after this project is done?

Stated requirement → real expectation

tap a project

Make the expectations clear to the whole team, use them to choose between paths, and critically re-read requirements in their light. Often a "requirement" turns out to be an amateur implementation plan. Don't be afraid to suggest changes that move closer to the real goal. Your title may say developer; your job is problem solver.

Tip 96Delight Users, Don't Just Deliver Code
5

Pride and prejudice

Artisans of earlier ages signed their work. Pragmatic programmers take responsibility for theirs, too. Not with territorial code ownership (fiefdoms where people guard "their" modules and look down on others'), but with pride: "I wrote this, and I stand behind it." Code can be owned communally; anonymity is what breeds sloppiness.

Postface: the moral compass

Twenty years on, software runs the world: pacemakers, cars, banks, elections, baby monitors. Developers are building the future, and that comes with real responsibility. The authors ask two questions of every piece of code delivered.

Question 1

Have I protected the user?

Can that baby monitor get security patches? If the smart thermostat fails, does the owner still have manual control? Am I storing only the data I need, and encrypting anything personal?

Tip 98First, Do No Harm
Question 2

Would I use this myself?

Would I be happy for my details to be shared this way, my movements sold, to be driven by this car? If a project skirts ethics, working on it makes you responsible too.

Tip 99Don't Enable Scumbags
