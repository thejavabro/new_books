The requirements pit

Many books describe requirements as things lying on the ground, waiting to be gathered. They aren't. Clients rarely know exactly what they want; their first statement of need is an invitation to explore, not a specification. The developer's job is to help them discover it.

Tip 76Programmers Help People Understand What They Want

Notice what the developer does: delivers facts and implications, then lets the client decide. That's the pattern. Requirements are discovered in a feedback loop of mock-ups, prototypes and tracer bullets, each one prompting "yes, that's what I meant" or "no, not like that".

Tip 78Work with a User to Think Like a User

Want to know how the system will really be used? Spend a week doing the user's job. Watching a help desk for a day teaches more than a month of interviews, and it builds rapport.

2

Requirements versus policy

"Only an employee's supervisors and HR may view their record." Is that a requirement? Today, maybe. But it's business policy dressed as an absolute, and policies change. Code the general case and treat the policy as data.

Tip 79Policy Is Metadata

  

  

    
Requirements vs realityBrian Eno once described the ultimate mixing desk that could do anything, and got in musicians' way, because it swapped their faders and knobs for keyboards and mice. Successful tools adapt to the hands that use them. Early feedback lets clients say "it does what I want, but not how I want."

    
Requirements are needNot architecture, not design, not UI. The simplest statement that captures the business need is best. Capture semantic invariants as requirements and current work practices as policy. Overspecifying freezes today's habits into the system.

Documenting requirements

  
The best requirements documentation might be working code. Requirements documents aren't really for clients (they rarely read 200 pages); they're for planning. Many teams write user stories: a short description on an index card of something a user wants, plus acceptance criteria. Keep them small so they invite conversation instead of ending it.

  

    
Free shipping thresholdOrders of $50+ before shipping ship free (ground only). 
Done when: threshold is configurable; excludes ebooks.

    
Record accessAuthorised roles can view an employee record. 
Done when: roles editable by HR admin.

    
Export CSVFinance can export last month's invoices. 
Done when: opens in their spreadsheet, UTF-8.

Solving impossible puzzles

  
King Gordius tied a knot no one could untie. Alexander cut it with his sword. A slightly different interpretation of the requirements, that's all. Hard problems are often hard because of constraints that aren't real. The trick isn't thinking outside the box; it's finding where the box actually is.

Working together

  
Conway's Law: organisations design systems that copy their communication structures. Teams that don't talk ship siloed, stove-pipe systems; a team split in two ships a front end and a back end. The book's advice is to put people together, including users, instead of throwing requirements over walls.

  

    
Conway's Law in action
how many teams, and do they talk?

    

    

  

  

    
Pair programmingOne types, one thinks at a higher level and spots problems. Two brains, two sets of experience, and social pressure against taking shortcuts.

    
Mob programmingThree or more people, one keyboard, rotating often. It can include users, testers and sponsors. It's a tight collaboration on live code.

  

  
Coding together isn't for everyone, and that's fine; at least try it. Build the code, not your ego; it's not who's brightest. Start small, criticise the code, not the person, listen, and run frequent retrospectives.

The essence of agility

  
"Agile" is an adjective: it describes how you do something. Yet it's been turned into a noun you can buy in a box, with certifications. The Agile Manifesto's values still say it best: individuals and interactions over processes and tools, working software over comprehensive documentation, customer collaboration over contract negotiation, responding to change over following a plan.

  
Tip 83Agile Is Not a Noun; Agile Is How You Do Things

  
There can never be a single agile process, because agility means responding to the unknown. So the authors offer a recipe, applied recursively at every level:

  
1 · Find out where you are
2 · Take the smallest meaningful step toward where you want to be
3 · Evaluate where you ended up, and fix anything you broke

  

  
The same loop works for a whole project (sometimes the first step shows that the best solution needs no software at all) and for the team's process: a team that doesn't keep experimenting with how it works isn't agile. And it drives design: if agility needs feedback, code must be easy to change. ETC again.
