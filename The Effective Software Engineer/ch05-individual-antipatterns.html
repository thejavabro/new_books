Anti-Patterns That Limit IC Effectiveness

Smart, hard-working engineers still fall into habits that cap their impact: hoarding knowledge, playing the hero, over-building, never shipping. Here are all fifteen anti-patterns from the chapter, how to spot them and what to do instead.

1

Fifteen anti-patterns, one map

The chapter catalogues fifteen habits that quietly cap an engineer's effectiveness. Some can show up at any level (knowledge silos); some get more common with seniority (over-engineering, failing to delegate); visibility becomes critical on the way to staff. Each one gets the same treatment: signs, remedy, a team-level fix and the benefit. Filter by theme, then tap an anti-pattern.

A sidebar notes that AI-assisted repository analysis can surface silos from version-control history, for example flagging a service where one person authored nearly all recent commits, which turns a vague worry into a concrete risk to address.

2

Spot it from what people say

Anti-patterns are easiest to catch from the phrases they produce. Which one is talking?

3

Delegation: go fast alone, go far together

"I'll just do it, it's faster" is true for one week and false for a quarter. The chapter's example: do two tasks and coach three people through one each, and five tasks still get done while three teammates get stronger. In this toy model, coached teammates need less help each week. Drag the week.

Ways to let go: hand a junior part of a feature and resist rewriting it; pair if you can't let go completely; design the interfaces and let others fill them in; accept that 90% right and done by someone else often beats 100% right and only possible through you. If you are buried, telling your manager "I'd like to hand off X so I can focus on Y" is prioritization, not weakness.

4

Analysis paralysis: most doors swing both ways

Analysis paralysis often comes from treating every decision as permanent. The remedy: time-box research (say two days), prototype early, set a forcing deadline, and recognize that most technical choices are two-way doors you can walk back through. Sort these decisions.

5

Scope creep: make the trade-off visible

The engineer who says yes to every "small" request overcommits, misses dates or cuts quality, and teaches stakeholders that additions are free. The remedy is to answer a request with a question about priorities. A stakeholder asks for a two-week feature in a six-week project. Choose how to respond.

6

The common thread is balance

Every remedy in the chapter pulls you back toward the middle rather than to the opposite extreme. The healthy point sits between the pairs.
