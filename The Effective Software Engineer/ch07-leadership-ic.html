Leadership as an Individual Contributor

You don't need a manager title to lead. Senior ICs coordinate, persuade, grow other people and take responsibility when things go wrong, and that is what earns them authority.

1

Leadership is earned by what you do

Senior ICs coordinate people who don't report to them: tech-leading engineers from other teams, proposing standards others must adopt. Without a title, people follow you because of what you consistently do. The chapter names seven habits.

2

Stepping up on a stalled project

You're a senior engineer on Project Z. Nobody is officially tech lead and the project has stalled. Instead of waiting, you act. Watch momentum return.

3

Aligning five teams and fifty engineers, with no reports

At staff level you may lead an initiative across the organization, such as moving from on-premises to the cloud over six months. You can't talk to fifty people individually. The chapter's first move is a working group: one senior representative per team, each with a clear role.

Then you do real project management: make dependencies explicit and track them. A slip in one team ripples to others, which is why you need to surface it early. Try delaying Team A.

4

Give credit, take responsibility

When reporting success, name the teams. When reporting problems, own them as the leader. Teams learn you won't throw them under the bus, and executives see maturity. Pick a moment.

The chapter's AI sidebar: connect an assistant to the tracker and chat so it can summarize each team's progress, flag blocked tickets and draft the weekly status update. That leaves you more time for the leadership work itself.

5

Lift others as you climb: mentor and sponsor

A mentor gives advice, answers questions and provides feedback. A sponsor spends their influence for someone: recommending them for a key project or a promotion. Senior ladders often expect both ("develops other technical leaders"). Sort these actions.

Mentoring or sponsoring?

Why it matters for your own effectiveness: it multiplies team capability, creates allies who back your initiatives, is noticed at principal level, spreads knowledge so silos and hero dependency fade, and it is satisfying. Be the senior engineer you wish you'd had.

6

When you break production

You deployed a change and checkout is failing. The chapter gives an order of operations. Tap the steps in the order you'd take them.

7

More influence, more responsibility

Effective engineers don't only build what they are asked; they think about possible harms. Before shipping, ask the four questions below. If you see a risk, you have a duty to raise it, even when you are junior and the team is under pressure. Raise it constructively, as specific scenarios with an alternative, and escalate through your manager or an ethics process if it is dismissed without real consideration.
