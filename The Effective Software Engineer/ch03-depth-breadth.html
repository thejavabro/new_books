Technical Depth Versus Breadth

Senior engineers need a deep specialty that makes them the person to call, and enough breadth to see how the pieces affect each other. Depth tells you how to fix a component; breadth tells you whether, when and with whom.

1

Depth: be the person people call

From senior level up, at least one area of real depth multiplies your impact: you know the nuances, have seen the failure patterns, can solve the hard problems faster and lead projects in that area. A specialty sometimes picks you (a crisis lands on your desk); sometimes you pick it (high demand plus genuine interest). Either is fine, as long as the expertise is real. The chapter describes it as three steps.

Depth also builds a reputation that lines up with senior and staff expectations: being known as "the one who figures out our hardest scaling problems" is useful evidence when promotions are discussed.

2

Breadth: seeing more than one way to fix it

Breadth is working knowledge across the stack and the business. It stops the tunnel vision deep specialists can fall into. The chapter's example: a page loads slowly. Compare what a narrowly focused database expert reaches for with what someone who also knows the backend and frontend sees.

Broad engineers also tend to find more opportunities and face less career risk when technology shifts. To build breadth while specializing: learn the vocabulary of neighbouring areas (a backend engineer serving mobile clients should know what "view lifecycle" means), pair with an SRE on a deploy problem, sit in on user research.

3

Draw your own skill shape

"T-shaped" means deep in one thing (the vertical bar) and broad across many (the horizontal bar). Set your levels honestly; the drawing tells you what shape you have.

4

Scaling a system needs both: a caching story

Asked to handle ten times the load, depth tells you which fix suits the bottleneck: shard or add read replicas, go async, move to horizontal scaling, add a CDN. Breadth makes sure the fix works for everyone else. Step through the chapter's staff-engineer example.

Breadth also shapes the system so many people can work on it: clear module boundaries instead of a ball of mud only you can navigate, APIs that don't turn one mega-endpoint into everyone's choke point, and observability designed in with SREs from the start (or by you, in companies without SRE or QA teams). AI can speed up the research phase, for example comparing a message queue with a streaming platform, but deciding which trade-offs fit your context still takes your depth.

5

Technical debt: depth finds it, breadth decides when to pay

Ward Cunningham's metaphor: shipping not-quite-right code is borrowing, and every minute spent working around it is interest. Depth lets you spot the debt and plan a proper fix. Breadth tells you which debt is worth paying now. You have room for two items this quarter: pick them.

6

Timing a refactor around the business calendar

A senior engineer finds the payment code brittle after a rushed launch, and knows from a past job exactly how to redesign it. Instead of refactoring in a vacuum, they fit it to the roadmap and the calendar. Step through.

7

How the balance shifts over a career

Depth and breadth are raw capability. At senior level, finding the right solution is half the job; the other half is convincing the organization to build it, which is where chapter 4 picks up.
