Strategic Thinking for Engineers

As you gain autonomy, the question shifts from "how do I build this?" to "what should we build, and why?" Strategic engineers pick high-leverage work, speak the business's language, phase big visions into shippable steps and let data settle arguments.

1

Not all work is equal: the effort–impact matrix

From senior level, the job shifts from doing the work right to doing the right work. Block an hour or two of strategy time a week, or urgent-but-unimportant tasks will crowd it out. A common tool is the effort–impact matrix: quick wins first, big bets planned as projects, low-impact high-effort work dropped. Place each item.

2

Ask why, and you may find a better problem

When given a project, ask what goal it serves. If the answer is unclear, the requirement may be outdated or misprioritized, or a better problem may be hiding behind it. Step through the chapter's example.

3

Challenge an assignment with data, politely

If a project looks like a lot of work for little gain, say so, and bring numbers. Even if the plan doesn't change this time, it shows you think in terms of return on investment.

"This feature is projected to reach under 2% of users, while 30% complain about load times. Could we shift some focus to performance, which helps everyone?"
4

The 80/20 rule: find where the leverage is

For many products, roughly 80% of the value comes from 20% of the features. Days polishing a barely-used admin panel instead of the core flow everyone uses is misallocation. Move your week's effort and see how much user value it touches.

5

Same backlog, different strategy, different priorities

What does the company need most from engineering right now? Pick a strategy and watch the same backlog reorder. Working on what the company cares about multiplies your impact, and gets noticed.

Two more habits: solve business problems that never arrive as engineering tickets (support says nobody understands feature X; you propose an in-app tutorial), and set guardrails: "If we launch this insecurely we risk a breach and fines; two more weeks makes it safe." That protects the deeper business goal of trust.

Speak business language

6

Think big, deliver results

A technical vision is your picture of where your area should be ("independent deploys for every team"). Execution is what you ship now. Months on a perfect system with nothing delivered fails; so does endless short-term work with no direction. The chapter suggests something like 70% product features, 20% foundation work toward the vision, 10% maintenance. Try other mixes.

Amazon is the chapter's case study: "Think big" and "Deliver results" are separate leadership principles. The vision of utility computing was rolled out one service at a time (S3, EC2, and so on), delivering value at every step.

7

Data over gut feeling, but the right data

Define success metrics up front, watch them, and adjust: if a metric doesn't move, the problem may be elsewhere (you optimized the backend, but the frontend was the bottleneck). Run A/B tests instead of debating; benchmark architectures before pivoting. And beware vanity metrics, numbers that go up without meaning anything. Sort these.

Meaningful or vanity?

Quantify engineering work with proxies: "Bugs found in QA per release fell from 15 to 5 after the refactor", or "on-call incidents fell from five a week to one, freeing hours and reducing burnout risk." If you can't tie work to any tangible result, question whether it delivers value. An AI sidebar suggests using AI to cluster support tickets by frustration and map them to backlog items or to company OKRs, giving you evidence for high-impact work.
