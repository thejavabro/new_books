Collaboration and Cross-Functional Influence

Software is a team sport. Your technical judgment only matters once other people act on it, so this chapter is about working with product managers, designers and your manager, and about persuading people you don't manage.

1

Product owns what and why; you own how

Great products come from engineering, product management and design working closely. Tension is normal: engineers want simplicity and clean tech, PMs want more scope, designers want the pixel-perfect version. Effective engineers bridge the gap. Tap each role.

2

Don't say no; say what is possible

When a request looks infeasible, "no, can't be done" ends the conversation. Explaining why and offering an option with the same user benefit turns conflict into product brainstorming with an engineering lens. Choose the best reply.

3

Same proposal, three different pitches

Influence without authority starts with knowing what each stakeholder worries about. You want the team to adopt a new infrastructure tool. Match each pitch to the person it was written for.

4

Running a refactor campaign as an IC

You're convinced the codebase needs a major refactor, and you have no authority to order one. The chapter lays out a sequence that gathers evidence and allies before asking. Watch support grow at each step.

Other levers from the chapter: build trust through competence and by crediting others, use data and prototypes instead of opinions, lead by example (write design docs for your own features and let others see them work), mentor and help, because goodwill is real, and invest in communication skills such as structuring arguments and drawing both options side by side.

5

Influence is a budget: pick your battles

If you object to everything, people tune you out. Save your influence for what matters, and when you lose, support the decision and help it succeed. That makes your next objection count for more. A toy model, not from the book: drag how many things you push back on per month.

6

Managing up: bring solutions, not just problems

Managing up means working with your manager on goals, progress and trust. For juniors it starts with proactive updates and asking for feedback; for seniors it grows into a strategic partnership. Compare two ways of raising the same issue.

The aim is not to become a yes-person. A manager who trusts you will advocate for your ideas and let you run experiments, and you keep your independent judgment, bringing data and context so you decide better together.

7

Reading the other side's documents faster

AI to get up to speedCross-functional work means reading long PRDs and research reports. The chapter suggests asking an AI for a summary from an engineering angle: the core user problems, key features, stated constraints and performance goals. Then verify the details that matter for your implementation in the original, because summaries can miss nuances or misread a constraint. Use the meeting for discussion instead of pre-reading.
