Career Growth and Leveling Up

Promotions follow scope and impact, not years served. You get to the next level by already working at it, making sure the right people can see it, and asking for it at the right time.

1

Promotions follow scope, not years served

In tech, impact and scope drive promotions. A newer engineer who solves high-impact problems can move up quickly while someone with many years plateaus doing the same work. The typical bar is consistently exceeding your current level and sometimes doing the next level's work. Compare two engineers.

Blue: keeps expanding scope (leads projects, mentors, volunteers cross-team)Red: does the same good work every year

Tenure is not worthless: years bring system knowledge, domain expertise and relationships. But if years pass without promotion, ask whether you have been growing scope or repeating. Talk to your manager; some companies have budget or level caps, and in the worst case you may need to go where you will be recognized.

The unwritten rules

Beyond the formal rubric, a few rules shape who gets promoted. Knowing them saves frustration.

4

What changes from mid-level to senior to staff

Every ladder differs, but the progression widens scope, autonomy and leadership. Pick a level to compare. Note the chapter's caveat: staff is a different kind of work focused on organizational impact, not an automatic next step, and many excellent engineers stay senior by choice.

5

IC or manager: two parallel paths

Around senior level many people face the choice. Managers focus on people and delivery (hiring, one-on-ones, coordination, reporting up); ICs focus on technical solutions (system design, code, technical mentoring). Neither is a promotion from the other. Answer six questions to see which pulls harder for you.

Unwritten considerations: managers have more direct say over promotions and assignments; junior and mid IC work usually has more focus time, but senior and staff ICs spend a lot of time collaborating; pay can reach similar heights on both tracks, though staff roles are fewer. You can try it (tech lead manager roles, covering a manager's vacation, managing an intern) and you can switch back. Don't chase management only because it looks like the way up.

6

Keep a brag document (it's just facts)

Julia Evans popularized the brag document: a running record of accomplishments with metrics. Managers with many reports depend on it during calibration. If "bragging" feels wrong, think of it as reporting facts. Turn a task into an impact statement with STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result), and watch the tone.

The chapter's sidebar suggests asking an AI to turn a quarter's tickets, PRs and design docs into STAR bullets that emphasize business impact. Check every claim before it goes in a promotion packet.

7

Know when the decisions are made

Promotions and raises run on calendars. If promotions are decided in Q4, have the readiness conversation by Q3; if raises follow June reviews, don't raise pay in July. Some companies want six months at level, so say when you think the clock should start. Tap the month your company decides promotions.
