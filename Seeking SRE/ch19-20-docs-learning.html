Chapter 19

Do Docs Better: Integrating Documentation into the Engineering Workflow

Ríona MacNamara, Shylaja Nukala and colleagues at Google, with Niall Richard Murphy.
1

A doc is good when it fulfils its purpose

Confidence in engineering docs is low across the industry (developers ranked docs their number-two challenge in a 2016 Stack Overflow survey), and for SREs bad docs hurt reliability directly. Borrowing from software testing, the authors split quality in two. Structural quality: spelling, style, tone, organization, working links. Easy to measure, so tempting to over-weight. Functional quality: does it do its job? Harder to measure and what actually matters; internal docs for a system in flux need less polish than customer docs.

Structural or functional?

2

What SRE documentation must do

SRE docs support monitoring, emergency response, capacity planning, change management and performance. Five types carry most of the load.

3

Write a playbook entry from a template

Templates codify the requirements for each doc type, make docs faster to write, and make them predictable to read at 3 a.m. Name an alert and fill the skeleton.


  






  
4
Docs live next to code

  
In 2014 a couple of Google technical writers did something unusual: asked internal engineers about docs. Engineers were unhappy with the docs, and felt bad about feeling bad. The result was g3doc: Markdown files in source control beside the code, rendered nicely, edited and reviewed with the same tools as code. Engineers then actually maintain them. Practices that followed:

  
Does this change need a doc update?
Rule of thumb: if a developer, SRE or user must change their behavior after the change, the change includes docs. If it wouldn't need tests, it probably doesn't need docs.

5
Learning by doing

  
SREs are generalists and experts at once: kernels, networks, monitoring, debugging, incident management, distributed systems, capacity planning, security and more. Seymour Papert (inventor of Logo) said learning works best when the learner builds something, and that learning through play is engaging, not easy: his students called it fun because it was hard. Games have taught strategy for a long time: chess reputedly began as a way to teach nobles, and Kriegsspiel trained Prussian officers from 1812 with a gamemaster, fog of war and dice.






  
6
Play a round of Wheel of Misfortune

  
A gamesmaster describes a page; a team member plays on-call and responds as they would for real. The gamesmaster answers what monitoring and logs would show. Everyone learns the system, its tools and its escalation paths. Google's Dublin SRE building even has a room designed for it. You're on call.

  






  
7
Incident Manager: the card game

  
Nolan's card game teaches incident management: communication, coordination and separated responsibilities. Two common failures it targets: everyone jumps in uncoordinated (and all chase the same root cause), and a junior person sees the answer but isn't heard. Players split into two shifts (EMEA and US) for a GFS-like storage system, with four card types: basic rules, roles, keepers and actions. Pick your role card.

  

  






  
8
SRE Classroom: design at scale

  
Not a game but a workshop, run since 2012 at Google and conferences. Groups of about five, each with a facilitator who asks questions rather than giving answers, design a system whose business logic is as simple as a SQL join but which must handle page views on the order of what Google serves daily, then estimate hardware. Try a back-of-envelope estimate.

  

    

      
      
      
      
    

    

  






  
9
Teams that keep learning

  
Failing to learn is expensive: we extend outages, cause security breaches, lose data. New hires take longer to become useful, and the few experts become bottlenecks and burn out. The two most widespread learning habits:

  

    
Weekly production meetings
Developers and SREs review how the service is doing. The team learns, individuals learn, and follow-up questions send people off to find answers.

    
Postmortems
Writing them and reading them teaches the curious corners of your systems, beyond preventing a repeat. Some teams run postmortem reading clubs, or replay old incidents as Wheel of Misfortune scenarios.
