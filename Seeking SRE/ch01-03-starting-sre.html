Chapter 1

Context Versus Control in SRE

A conversation with Coburn Watson about how Netflix's SRE team improves availability it does not own.
1

Two ways to steer reliability

In a control model, teams know their availability goal and face a penalty if they miss it, for example losing the right to deploy. In a context model, teams get the information they need to make good reliability decisions themselves, and SRE helps when asked. Netflix leans hard toward context: when someone makes a poor operational choice, the first question is whether they lacked context, not whether they were careless.

Context isn't free, and it doesn't fit everywhere. Tick the conditions that hold for your organization and see where it should probably sit.

Mostly contextMixedMostly control

Scale means headcount, not trafficWatson argues context works at any production scale. What strains it is organizational size and distance: fewer face-to-face conversations, teams on other continents. Companies that start with control usually do so because they are more comfortable leading with process, whatever their size.
2

Information is not context

Mailing a team a dashboard of time series is information. Most engineers ignore it: they write business logic and are not specialists in reading resource graphs. Context means applying domain knowledge so the data tells a story. Netflix's version is a per-microservice availability scorecard: one bar per week, measured from the callers' side, with drill-downs into upstream callers and downstream dependencies. It is pushed only when something changes worth attention.

A weekly scorecard

green = met goal · yellow = missed but better than last week · red = missed and worse

Watch the slow driftCanaries catch a 20% jump in CPU on a deploy. What they miss is five extra milliseconds a week for six months, until a service needs three times the capacity. The scorecard exists to catch small reliability drifts before they add up to the big outage. Like a tire-pressure warning on a dashboard, it doesn't tell you what to do, only that a trend is going the wrong way.
3

From context to (a little) control

Context is delivered at several levels, and control is the last rung, used rarely. Step through what happens as a team keeps having incidents.

4

Every ask has a cost somewhere else

Watson thinks about four engineering dimensions: rate of innovation, security, reliability and efficiency. His team owns reliability and efficiency (it runs capacity planning), and tries to improve them with as little drag on the other two as possible. Compare what each lever does.

Context can backfire too. One team, given cost reports but no cost budget, chose an older, cheaper instance type and caused an outage. The lesson Netflix spread afterward: we would rather run less efficiently than compromise reliability. Over five years, with context plus a better platform, customers and streaming grew about sixfold while availability improved every year.

Chapter 2

Interviewing Site Reliability Engineers

Andrew Fong on the hiring process, drawn from AOL, YouTube, Google and Dropbox.
5

The hiring funnel

Hiring involves at least three parties: the candidate, the recruiter and the hiring manager. Most companies picture it as a funnel. Candidates drop out at each stage, and measuring each stage lets you improve it. Two simple guards against bias belong at the top: a blind résumé review (hide identifying details) and a standardized process so every candidate is judged the same way. Tap a stage.

6

Interview against a hypothesis

Before the onsite, write an explicit hypothesis about the candidate: expected seniority, growth path, and leadership. It sets expectations for the panel, clarifies what you are really hiring for, and lets you design a loop that tests it fairly. Build one.

7

The hiring manager's two hard jobs

Selling

From the first minute, you are selling the organization. To do it well, learn three things: what motivates the candidate, what they value, and which environment brings out their best. Then tell a story that connects your team to those.

Walking away

Every new fact about a candidate is a chance to re-evaluate organizational fit, at any stage. It is hard after investing time and emotion, but a wrong hire is almost always worse than a missed one, so tune the pipeline against false positives. Whatever the outcome, the candidate should leave feeling the process was fair.
