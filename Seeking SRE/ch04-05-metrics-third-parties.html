Chapter 4

Using Incident Metrics to Improve SRE at Scale

Martin Check on how Microsoft Azure chose, reviewed and acted on incident data.
1

The virtuous cycle

Azure had thousands of data sources and no obvious order to look at them in. The team settled on a framework called the virtuous cycle: measure how fast you detect outages, how well you learn from them through root-cause analysis and repairs, how fast those fixes ship, and how code quality and deployment velocity close the loop. Step around it.

2

TTD + TTE + TTF = TTM

Every minute of downtime counts, so the first job was to agree on definitions and start and end times. Time to Detect starts when customers first feel impact, even if monitoring missed it. Time to Engage runs from detection until the right engineer is working on it (triage plus escalation). Time to Fix is the responder mitigating. Together they make Time to Mitigate. Agreeing the taxonomy matters: if teams disagree what "mitigated" means, some disengage too early.

Who noticed first?

Why is TTD so important? For incidents that need a human to act, nothing else can start until someone knows. It measures the quality of your monitoring. Azure found TTD was about ten times higher when a customer reported the outage than when monitoring caught it.

3

Surrogate metrics: specific enough to act on

"Improve your TTE" is not an action. Digging into slow incidents turned up narrower measures that correlate with the big ones. They are necessary but not sufficient for good results, and they give managers something concrete to do.

4

Repair debt, and the debt nobody logged

Every postmortem finding becomes a tracked repair item: short-term (a script, a process change, a hotfix within a week) or long-term (fix the class of bug, not the instance; training across organizations; auto-escalation tooling). Repairs live in the normal work tracker but stay distinguishable, so operational debt competes openly with features. When teams first start tracking, debt spikes as hidden work becomes visible, then falls as fixing it becomes routine.

Some services stopped improving. Why?

Logged repair debt looks stable and well attended. Yet month-over-month reliability wasn't getting better.

Measuring postmortem quality without reading postmortemsParsing postmortem text with machine learning would be costly and non-deterministic. The simpler rule: any incident that missed a detect, engage or mitigate goal must have a repair item tagged with that miss. Incidents without one are "missed repairs", and their total is virtual repair debt. Until logged and fixed, those same misses repeat. Then put it all on real-time dashboards that pivot by service, manager and owner, because monthly reviews are too slow to drive change.

Working with Third Parties Shouldn't Suck

Jonathan Mercereau, formerly of LinkedIn's traffic team, on DNS, CDN, monitoring and other vendors.
5

Build, buy, or adopt?

First establish how important the decision is and who it affects (choosing a JavaScript framework touches many teams; a certificate authority might touch few). Then choose among three options. Adopt, meaning integrate an open source project, deserves its own category: a proof of concept is cheap, but running it at scale needs skilled people, upgrades and bug fixes. Answer a few questions.

BuildSo custom only you can do it. Often open-sourced later once battle-hardened.
BuyCommon need, a paid SaaS, hosted or licensed product exists.
AdoptCommon need, an open source solution exists.

Rule of thumb on timelines: however long you think an integration will take, double it, then add a bit. Legal, security and procurement all have a say, and production readiness means testing, monitoring, logging, config management, deployment and disaster recovery, not just a working demo.

6

When they're down, you're down

Customers don't care whose fault it is. Third parties sit all around the edge of your stack. Some hit users directly; others hurt quietly from the back end. Sort each vendor.

7

Not every vendor is a black box

Vendors sit on a spectrum from closed box (nice portal, little API) to open box (reporting APIs, real-time logs, automation hooks, good docs). Openness determines how much of SRE practice you can apply. Click the signals you have for a vendor to see how good your SLIs can be.

black boxopen box

SLOs for things you don't runDon't just copy the vendor's SLA: most promise around 99.5% uptime, which is easy for them to meet and gives you only service credits. Your "product owners" may be sibling SRE teams who need your SLO to compute their own error budgets. Get Legal and Procurement involved in the Master Service Agreement and SLA before signing.
