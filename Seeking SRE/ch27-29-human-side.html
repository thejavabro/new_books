Chapter 28

SRE Cognitive Work

John Allspaw and Richard Cook (Adaptive Capacity Labs): what really happens in people's heads during incidents, and why it matters more than tooling.
1

The irony of automation

Lisanne Bainbridge's 1983 "Ironies of Automation" observed that automating a process doesn't remove humans; it leaves them the hardest parts: monitoring a system they no longer operate hands-on, and stepping in when automation fails in ways no one anticipated. SRE work is exactly that. The authors contrast two views of work:

Taylor/Ford viewWork is a fixed procedure; humans are interchangeable parts, and errors come from deviating from the procedure. Improve by tighter rules.
Cognitive systems viewSince Three Mile Island (1979), research shows operators constantly adapt to conditions procedures never foresaw. Expertise, not compliance, keeps complex systems running.
2

Four themes found in incidents

Studying real incidents in depth, the authors repeatedly find the same kinds of cognitive work.

3

Sacrifice decisions: give something up to save more

During incidents, responders often must choose to accept a definite loss to avoid a possible bigger one. These are agonizing and judged harshly in hindsight. Pick a case, then choose.

4

Above and below the line of representation

Engineers never touch the running system directly. They see it only through representations: dashboards, logs, CLIs. The real system lives below that line; above it, people build mental models from what the screens show. People and machines together form a joint cognitive system.

5

The cost of coordination

Incident response is joint work, and coordinating costs effort: bringing people up to speed, relaying findings, deciding who does what. Formal roles and incident classification help, but add overhead of their own, so formal structures that look good on paper can slow real response. Watch how communication paths grow with responders.

6

What organizations can do

Incidents won't stop, they are costly (one airline lost over $200 million from a single bad day), and their patterns keep shifting. The authors argue for a research agenda: a shared corpus of well-analyzed cases across companies (the SNAFU Catchers consortium), automation designed as a team player that shows what it is doing, and attention to the calibration problem. For your own organization:

  1. Build internal capacity for incident analysis, as a skill in its own right.
  2. Instead of shallow reviews of every incident, study a few medium-severity ones deeply.
  3. Build tooling that captures the data streams of an incident (chat, commands, graphs viewed) for later analysis.
  4. Treat the findings as learning about how work is actually done, not as a list of fixes.
