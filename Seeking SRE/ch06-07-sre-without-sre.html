Chapter 6

How to Apply SRE Principles Without Dedicated SRE Teams

Björn Rabenstein and Matthias Rampke on SoundCloud's move from a Rails monolith to hundreds of microservices.
1

The arithmetic that sank the plan

Around 2012 SoundCloud hired a few former Google SREs and tried to run things the Google way. The numbers didn't work. An SRE team must cover an on-call rotation, and best practice puts the minimum team at about eight people. Move the slider to see what share of engineering that is.

Embedding SREs in development teams was also doomed by the numbers: teams had no more than ten engineers, so one embedded SRE was either alone on call for everything or a token presence. SoundCloud had always let developers control their own deploys. So it leaned into that culture instead: you build it, you run it. No separate dev and ops teams, not even dev or ops roles within a team. The trick was to give everyone the SRE mindset.

Developers versus the pager

Putting developers on call created the opposite danger: tiny, "experts only" rotations. They look efficient but hide three traps. Change the rotation size and the alert noise.

4

Making the right thing the easy thing

Google keeps SRE practice honest with incentives rather than "golden rules": error budgets instead of mandated release freezes, and the threat to hand back the pager. SoundCloud had no pager to hand back, so it relied on making good practice cheap.

SRE Without SRE: The Spotify Case Study

Nine Spotify engineers on eleven years of growth without a central SRE organization.
5

Eleven years of operations at Spotify

Spotify organizes into small autonomous squads that each own a feature end to end. It never had an SRE team, yet it scaled by applying SRE principles everywhere. The headcounts tell the story: ops grows, but services and engineers grow faster, until a central ops team can no longer keep up. Pick an era.

6

Who does what, then and now

Ops-in-Squads moved operational responsibility into the squads, with ops engineers building tools and platforms rather than doing the work by hand. Compare who owns each task.

Autonomy versus consistencyFreedom produced fragmentation: every squad its own tools. The answer was a golden path, one blessed stack (a standard Java service framework, deploy pipeline and monitoring) supported very well, plus clear incentives to use it. The payoff showed during the move from data centers to Google Cloud Platform: for developers it was almost invisible. The trade-off: use cases the path did not yet cover (legacy Python, data pipelines, autoscaling) drove teams to build workarounds.
