Chapter 30

Against On-Call: A Polemic

Niall Richard Murphy: on-call is treated as an unquestioned part of the job. It shouldn't be.
1

Emergency room or ward?

Murphy borrows from medicine. Emergency (A&E) medicine is triage: stabilize the patient, buy time, hand over. Ward medicine is slow, deliberate treatment of root causes. On-call in software is mostly A&E work done by people hired to do ward work, and the industry rarely asks whether so much emergency medicine is necessary at all.

Strong and weak anti-on-call

Strong anti-on-callRemove the sources of pages entirely: more deterministic software, fewer unknowns, systems that don't need humans in the loop. Ambitious and maybe unreachable, but a direction.
Weak anti-on-callLike driverless trains that simply stop safely when something is wrong: design systems that fail into a safe, stable state that can wait until morning.

Murphy closes with a call to standardize on well-understood, reliable components, and for engineers to collectively push back (even, he suggests, through unions) against on-call as a default condition of employment.

Chapter 31

Elegy for Complex Systems

Mikey Dickerson (former US Digital Service administrator): lessons on organizations and failure from rescuing HealthCare.gov.
6

The Nation of IT and the Nation of Tech

Dickerson describes two cultures. The "Nation of IT" (government and enterprise IT) buys systems through contracts, measures compliance and treats failure as someone's fault. The "Nation of Tech" (internet companies) builds in-house, iterates and treats failure as normal. HealthCare.gov's 2013 launch failure showed what happens when a Tech-sized problem meets IT-style organization; the rescue led to the US Digital Service.

7

Draw the org chart, then the system

Conway's law: systems mirror the communication structure of the organizations that build them. Dickerson's exercise: draw your org chart, draw your system diagram, compare. Pick an organization.

8

Always partially failed, margins always consumed

Large systems are always partly broken; they keep running on safety margins. Margins invite use: "the right hand giveth nines, the left hand taketh away". Each layer you add an extra nine to, someone spends on a riskier feature or tighter cost. The shuttle program flew with known risks and lost 2 of 135 missions. Lesson: don't design to a vendor's published SLA; design for the failure you'll actually get.

9

Novelty priority inversion and coordination

Promotions reward launching new things, so engineers build new systems while old ones decay; the system's evolution is driven by career incentives rather than need. Meanwhile coordination overhead grows with size (Dickerson retells a Pentagon legend of an 8-minute-per-day budget for actual work). Suggested reading: Inside Bureaucracy (Downs), "The Tyranny of Structurelessness" (Freeman) and The Mythical Man-Month (Brooks).
