1

Toil, the hidden villain

Google's definition (via Vivek Rau): toil is work tied to running a production service that is

manualrepetitiveautomatabletacticalno enduring valuescales linearly with the service

Organizations that live SRE eliminate toil by reflex. Traditional enterprises are fertile ground for it: legacy management was either blind to it ("everyone looks busy, great") or indifferent ("I pay you to have headaches"), and organizational complexity both creates it and blocks its removal. Practice spotting it.

Toil or engineering?

2

Silos, queues and tickets

A silo isn't a team; it's a way of working: disconnected from the groups around you. Silos talk through request queues, and queues are expensive. As the people behind a queue get busier, wait time doesn't grow linearly, it explodes. Drag utilization and handoffs.

3

Clearing the obstacles

Big-bang transformations of complex systems fail. Improve continuously, using Lean's Kaizen and the Kata method to run small experiments toward a target. Then work through these moves, roughly in order.

4

Self-service is more than a button

The classic version: a privileged ops team builds static buttons for others to push. That keeps ops as the bottleneck, because they must build and maintain every button. The better version gives requesters safe primitives and permission to compose their own automation, with security and compliance built in. Compare a typical request both ways.

5
Automated testing at Google Web Server

  
In the mid-2000s the team behind Google's home-page web server (GWS) was stuck: long builds, untested code reaching production, large conflicting check-ins from many teams. Fear stopped newcomers from changing things they didn't understand, and stopped veterans from changing things they understood too well. The team drew a hard line: no change accepted without automated tests, a continuous build kept green, test coverage tracked and rising. GWS became one of the company's most productive teams, and an informal Testing Grouplet spread the culture across Google over five years.

6
Launch and handoff readiness reviews

  
Developers self-manage a new service in production first. To launch to real users it must pass a Launch Readiness Review; to be handed to SRE it must pass a Handoff Readiness Review. SREs act as consultants along the way. Tick the requirements a service meets.

  

    

    

  

  
The teams that get through the handoff review fastest are the ones that worked with SREs earliest, from design onward. Helping product teams early counts as good citizenship and is considered in SRE promotions.






  
7
One shared source repository

  
A firm-wide repository turns one team's fix into everyone's fix: update a shared library and every dependent service picks it up through its own pipeline. By 2015 Google's single repository held over a billion files and two billion lines of code, used by around 25,000 engineers across Search, Maps, Docs, Gmail and YouTube. Engineers can learn from code across the whole company, and old library versions don't linger in forgotten corners.

  
The bridgeKim's argument is that SRE and DevOps are not rivals: these patterns came from SRE culture and became DevOps staples. The breathtaking idea underneath is Ben Treynor Sloss's self-balancing system: agree an error budget, and let it decide when to prioritize reliability work and when to ship.
