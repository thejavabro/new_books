Five steps to an enterprise SRE team

The pitch that resonates with executives: SRE is a vehicle to keep customer-facing products healthy while increasing engineering velocity. Getting there in an enterprise with central IT, support, global operations and separate product divisions took a deliberate sequence.

First, without SRE, engineers keep context-switching into outages instead of building what the business prioritized; gather stories and rough hours from support, sales and marketing. Chapter 9

From SysAdmin to SRE in 8,963 Words

Vladimir Legeza (Amazon Japan) on what changes when you start from the customer and work backward.
4

Same metrics, different conclusions

The difference between a system administrator and an SRE is mostly the point of view. A SysAdmin asks whether the system is producing errors or overloaded. An SRE defines the desired state in business terms: if users weren't affected, an error may be fine; if a 2 ms latency rise hurts customers, it's severe. Legeza's example: build a small crawler that takes a URL and reports which links on the page are alive.

Developer"A few dozen lines of code. On it."
SysAdmin"Is there an SLA? What load, what outages must we survive?"Answer: ≤10 requests/s, ≤10 s per URL.
SRE"Who are the customers, and why do they need 10 seconds?"It's a search team validating that indexed links are live. Accuracy matters more than speed. A page with a million links breaks the 10 s promise, so the SLA itself must change.
5

Is this metric an SLI?

An SLI is one carefully chosen measurable aspect of service behavior. An SLA combines SLIs with "good/bad" boundaries, limits and what happens beyond them. An SLO is the same shape but a stricter internal goal: what we want, not what we must have. A metric qualifies as an SLI only if it passes two tests: is it visible to the user, and is it important enough to the user that it must stay in a range?

Good SLI candidate?

The 1,000 requests per second trap"99.9% of requests served in 200 ms, with no more than 1,000 rps" sounds complete, but a user can read the limit as their personal allowance, as total capacity, or as today's hardware limit. It leaks internals and will change whenever capacity does. Fix: "no more than 1,000 rps from a single user account". Now it's measurable on the user's side and independent of your hardware.
6

Per-component SLAs and the moving bottleneck

A message distribution service has a receiver, a transformer (which enriches messages using external sources) and a distributor. A manager asks: can it take three times the peak? Breakpoint tests that count only error-free throughput miss slow, stuck messages, so each component gets its own SLA including a passing-time limit. Each also has an input/output ratio: if the transformer doubles the data, the distributor sees twice the load. Set the forecast and capacities.

Two more rules of thumb from the chapter. Your promised reliability can't exceed the weakest direct dependency (DNS, used constantly, is tightly coupled; LDAP, called only at startup, is not); keep a list of dependencies, their SLAs and outage effects handy for emergencies. And time budgets compose: if the transformer must pass a message in 50 ms and spends 25 ms internally, a dependency promising <20 ms fits.

7

Counting availability in minutes

Counting failed messages directly has flaws: availability only ever falls, a quiet day with 10 messages and one bad host shows a 33% drop, and planned maintenance can't be expressed. The fix: treat "99.9% per year" as time. A year has 525,600 minutes; 0.1% is about 526 minutes of error budget. Each minute's failure fraction converts to lost time, and the window rolls: old days drop off the tail and give their failures back. Simulate a day.


  

  
Partial minutes, not whole messagesIf minute 1 has 10 messages and 5 errors, it counts as 0.5 minutes down; minute 2, with 5 retried messages and no errors, counts as fully up. The quiet-day "33% drop" becomes half a minute. Empty metric buckets count as failure, so emit explicit zeros.
