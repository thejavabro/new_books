Chapter 14

In the Beginning, There Was Chaos

Casey Rosenthal, who built Netflix's Chaos team, on why complex systems need experiments, not just tests.
1

Why old methods stop being enough

Netflix is about a hundred small teams, each owning microservices end to end, with hundreds of services working together. Nobody can hold the whole system in their head. Kent Beck introduced Rosenthal to a model he calls the Economic Pillars of Complexity: four things that confound progress when building a product. Tap each.

2

The invisible safety boundary

Jens Rasmussen described systems drifting within three boundaries: economics, workload and safety. Crossing any of them is failure. Teams can usually see the first two: budgets are tracked, overload is felt. The safety boundary is invisible until you cross it. Pressure from the other two pushes the system toward it. Chaos engineering generates a safety signal: it shows where the edge is before an outage does.

3

An experiment, not breaking things

Ask around and people define chaos engineering as "breaking things in production". That is not a definition: breaking things is easy and worthless. The Netflix team wrote it down (PrinciplesofChaos.org) as an empirical practice: experiments that uncover systemic weaknesses. Netflix's steady-state metric is how many videos are streaming. Chapter 15

The Intersection of Reliability and Privacy

Betsy Beyer and Amber Yust (Google) on why SREs are well placed to help with privacy.
5

Same foundation, same goal

Privacy engineering is a young, quietly discussed field. It is not just legal compliance: it is about building products people trust, often under hard technical, administrative and legal constraints, with real subjectivity about what is sensitive to whom. Reliability and privacy share a starting point (deeply understand your systems) and an end goal (meet user expectations). Both attract people who look at a system and see how it breaks. Flip between the lenses.

6

You can't unring a bell

The disciplines part ways on reversibility and tolerance. Slide the "success rate" and compare how users would feel.

Requests served

User records handled correctly

A down service can be brought back and customers forgive the occasional outage. A compromised database cannot be "fixed". So privacy has less room for the "ship something usable, not perfect" trade-offs reliability makes, and when the two conflict, the balance usually tips toward privacy: reliability bought at the cost of surprising users isn't worth much.
