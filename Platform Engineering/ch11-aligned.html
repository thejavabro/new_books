Alignment: the first sign of success

Part III asks what success looks like, and starts with alignment. Misaligned platform teams create a different swamp from Chapter 1's: overlapping, incompatible products that are hard to combine and work against each other. Misalignment shows up in three places. Pick one to see the authors' example.

2

A red herring: adoption metrics

"Success" often gets measured as adoption. Better than "completion", the ultimate cop-out metric, but risky: with a captive audience, chasing 100% means building what you think people should want and forcing it on them. Per-system adoption targets make your own teams compete for users, and mandatory migrations turn the metric into a stick. Compare two worlds.

Track adoption as an input to strategy, not as the measure of success. Some things (employee identity) should be one system for everyone; many platforms are simply more valuable to some groups, so target the customers where leverage is greatest.

3

Aligning on purpose

Every platform team should share the four pillars from Chapter 2 (product, development, breadth, operations), then add its own area-specific purpose. Three levers build that shared purpose.

4

Aligning product strategy

If product managers report to the engineering managers of single platforms, they become extensions of those silos. The authors put platform PMs in a separate team under a product leader, and the most senior principal engineer beside them, both reporting to a cross-area platform leader. Toggle the structures and watch which problems get seen.

5

Aligning plans

Align only on big projects (about one developer-year or more) and their cross-team dependencies. A detailed plan of everything is too much work and kills agility. Gaming happens either way; culture and parallel reporting lines keep small things honest. Raise the threshold and see what's left to discuss.

Have backbone; disagree and commitWeak leaders greenlight everyone to avoid conflict; the ripples hit other teams and customers later. Amazon's principle, where Ian worked for a decade, puts have backbone first: leaders need a forum to make their best case. Then a senior leader decides with a clear, communicated rationale through a collaborative, transparent process, and everyone commits.
6

Getting an organization to alignment

Camille's platform org: about 100 people in a 1,000-engineer company. The OS team needed immutable images; the build-tools team wanted to rebuild around Bazel, an open source build system. Both migrations would load work on the other team. Deadlock. Step through how it was resolved.
