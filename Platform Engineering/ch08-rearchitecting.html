Rearchitecting Platforms

Successful platforms outgrow the architecture they started with. The choice between evolving in place and building a v2 is one of the riskiest a platform leader makes.

1

Why incremental fixes eventually hit a wall

You did everything right: started small, delivered incrementally, kept investing in maintenance. Still, as load grows, operational issues grow, and keep-the-lights-on (KTLO) work eats the capacity for improvements and features. The best developers leave out of frustration, and critical infrastructure freezes in time. Small fixes have the best short-term payoff, but can't fix deep architectural limits. Slide the load.

2

Rearchitect in place rather than build a v2

Rearchitecture means reimplementing a system's architecture step by step while it stays live and serves load: changing the engine mid-flight. A v2 is a new system that customers must migrate to. The second-system effect (Fred Brooks) explains why v2s go wrong: the design swells to fix every perceived flaw, more people get added, and it ships late or never. The authors add a deeper reason: a v2 asks one team to hold three mindsets at once.

3

Pioneers, settlers and town planners

Simon Wardley describes three kinds of engineer on a growing business. The authors call them mindsets, and map them onto platform maturity. Each needs a different idea of "right", so a team must agree which one it is using. Pick a maturity stage.

4

Addressing security with architecture

Of the four capabilities a rearchitecture targets (features, reliability, efficiency, security), security is the hardest. A guest section by Kelly Shortridge argues for resilience: don't try only to prevent failure; minimize its impact and build the ability to change quickly. Don't make app engineers "security aware"; make the safe way the easy way, until security disappears into the platform. Design-based fixes don't depend on human behaviour and separate users from the hazard. Eliminate a hazard or reduce it?

5

Guardrails that make big changes safe

Ideally users never notice a rearchitecture. Scrappy platforms weren't built for big internal changes, so lower the cost of change first. Step a change through the guardrails.

The three 12-month goals

One piece of initial work, three goals. Aim for the first; fall back if a partner's priorities change.

Goal 1Something audacious that moves the needle for a chosen business partner.
Goal 2Something smaller, maybe for another partner, that the old architecture couldn't do.
Goal 3New components in production serving some real load.

Antipattern: new hires leading the rearchitectureSomeone from a more mature company spots real problems, but lacks your platform's details, your culture and users' trust. For about 12 months let them review and contribute, not lead, and invest in them through 1:1s.
