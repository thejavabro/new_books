Your Platforms Are Loved

The highest bar: people choose your platform because it makes their work feel easy. Love usually comes from being boring, useful and dependable, not from being clever.

1

Why aim for love?

One platform product head sums up the goal as "simpler, faster, cheaper, and your users love it." Why love, for internal tools, and is it affordable outside giant tech firms? Think of the kitchen or garage tools you love: rarely the priciest, rarely trying to do everything; made with care for a purpose that matches yours. For internal platforms, love is a stand-in for productivity, which nobody can measure well. Chasing simple numbers like adoption or efficiency tends to produce systems that are easy for the platform team to control rather than ones users love.

Love needs a plan for adoption

A guest section by Smruti Patel. A monolith and surveys saying "we can't tell what's running in production" led to a service delivery platform aiming to halve feature lead time and make change safer. A product approach, personas, jobs to be done; better 10 delighted users than 1,000 half-satisfied; paved paths, guardrails, escape hatches and layers for the curious. A year in, under 5% of traffic had moved and they ran two platforms. Compare the two strategies.

5

A multitool, not a mystery mushroom

Love is hard. Most good platforms fade into the background: no declarations of love, but no constant pain either. Smruti's image: a platform should be a multitool, boring but useful, not a mystery mushroom that might be fun or might be deadly. One of the most successful platforms the authors saw was an ugly early job scheduler. People kept proposing shiny replacements (Airflow, Luigi…) that never spread beyond niches, while it chugged on with a skeleton crew. Want to replace a neglected success? Learn which features are used, decouple or reimplement them, dual-write to prove parity, and migrate carefully. In platform engineering, love needs trust first.

6

Concluding remarks: not a fad

Is platform engineering a fad? The risk is real: vendors push implementation details (the authors needle that search results fixate on developer portals) and consultants write checklists.
