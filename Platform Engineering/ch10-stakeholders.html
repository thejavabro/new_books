Managing Stakeholder Relationships

Platforms live or die by the goodwill of the leaders around them. Mapping stakeholders, being honest about trade-offs and handling shadow platforms are skills, not politics.

Why it mattersPower-interest gridCommunicationYes and noShadow platformsBudget cutsQuick check
1

Stakeholder management is not just politics

Product management builds the right things for customers; stakeholder management convinces their leaders you made the right choices. Because platforms deliver slowly and tie only indirectly to revenue, stakeholders have outsized influence: without their support, a charismatic peer with a good idea can drain your investment. The authors once saw this as politics and empire building. They now blame organization size: past Dunbar's 50 to 150 people, groups become villages with their own priorities, and each village's "mayor" expects its biggest problem to become yours.

2

Map your stakeholders: the power-interest grid

Meet Juan, an imaginary VP of platform engineering serving three product engineering groups plus finance and HR, reporting to the CTO. His platform works, but the big product teams see it as a necessary nuisance. Click each stakeholder to see where they sit and how to engage.

Product lens: the users come first. Manager lens: the team comes first. Stakeholder lens: the senior people who may judge you harshly, or see your platform as an obstacle or a competitor, come first. Ask: who has the ear of senior leadership? If I did everything perfectly but they still disliked me, how much would it matter? Would overdelivering for them matter?

3

Communicate with the right transparency

Too little information breeds mistrust. Too much is just as bad. Slide the level of detail in your regular updates.

The stakeholder is always rightLike customers in sales. Leaders amplify their teams' gossip, treat you as an internal vendor, think they could run your team better, and forget last year's improvements. Accept that you'll rarely fully satisfy them and keep moving.
4

Finding acceptable compromises

Nobody can prove which platform work is worth most, and senior leaders usually decline to rule, so you compromise. Make your case in business impact, not excuses, and show it with measures. Avoid both extremes: always yes turns you into a Frankenstein feature shop; always no, from a leader sure of their own vision, leaves frustrated stakeholders waiting for you to stumble. Pick a response for each request.

A guest story from Jordan WestA team formed after a reorg promised just three things in a year and overdelivered, then got complaints about saying no. So it said "yes, with compromises" to more, cutting scope: graph storage only for small and medium uses off the critical path; "versioned datasets" moving only static data at first, each with a plan to revisit. Perception improved a lot.
5

Compromising on shadow platforms

A shadow platform duplicates a platform's job with different technology and a slightly different profile. It infuriates platform engineers, but first find out why it's happening. Match each driver to a sensible response.

6

Money troubles: surviving a budget squeeze

In a downturn the powerful-but-uninterested suddenly get interested. Roadmaps beyond KTLO look like plans from good times, and platform metrics are second-order: few care about developer productivity when the company is fighting to survive. Tie work to protected business outcomes. Three steps: find who benefits from each non-KTLO project; group work into team-sized chunks (about 3 to 12 people, never person by person); come with your own cuts and firm views on what to keep. Try it.
