The Pillars of Platform Engineering

Four pillars hold up a real platform: a curated product approach, software-based abstractions, serving a broad base of developers, and operating the platform as a foundation of the business.

A bridge is only as strong as its weakest pillar

Unpack the definition from Chapter 1 and four pillars fall out: product (a curated product approach), development (software-based abstractions), breadth (serving a broad base of application developers) and operations (running the platform as a foundation for the business). Miss one and you only move complexity around. Knock out a pillar and see what you get instead.

2

Pillar 1: a curated product approach

Both words matter. Product without curation makes you responsive but strategy-free: a service centre. Curation without product gives rigid offerings that miss real needs. Done together, two kinds of platform come out of it.

3

Pillar 2: if you aren't building software, it isn't platform engineering

A wiki of approved cloud services can be the right investment, but it is not platform engineering. Without in-house software you are vending infrastructure and passing all its complexity to users. What do platform engineers actually build? Four kinds of abstraction.

Metadata registries: knowing who uses what

To fix problems in primitives on users' behalf, you must know who uses each primitive and how. Tag systems, API/schema registries and internal developer portals (IDPs) all try to hold that metadata. The ones that work capture it automatically from the platform; registries that rely on people filling them in stall, because engineers hate being curators. Match each question to what it's about.

5

Pillar 3: serving a broad base

Lots of services in a microservice estate are called by several teams; that alone doesn't make them platforms. As users and use cases grow, you need capabilities that make the system cheaper, safer and easier: self-service (no manual onboarding), user observability (users can tell whether the fault is theirs or the platform's), guardrails (defaults and limits that make costly misconfiguration unlikely) and multitenancy. Multitenancy here is mostly about saving engineering time, not hardware.

6

Pillar 4: operating as a foundation

A flaky platform forces customers to become experts in it, and they learn the worst way: through its outages. Three things make a foundation: operating the full platform (including the OSS and vendor parts), supporting users, and operational discipline, meaning routine practices that chase anomalies before they hurt. Running mostly other people's code means unknown unknowns are always lurking. Who holds the pager in each pattern?

Support is part of the jobEven with self-service and guardrails, a broad user base sends a constant stream of edge-case questions. Few internal teams can hire dedicated support staff, so support becomes everyone's work, and a source of customer empathy.
