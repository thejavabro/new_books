Platform as a Product

Hiring a product manager does not make a platform a product. A product culture means knowing your customers, curating what you build and leave out, and measuring whether people actually choose your platform.

1

Product culture starts with the customer

Taking a "product approach" often gets read literally: add some product managers. The authors want much more, and they write the chapter so it works even with no PMs at all. Step one is understanding the people who use your platforms, and treating them as customers, not stakeholders. Internal customers are tricky in five ways.

2

Don't just build what they ask for

The relationship model (build exactly what the customer requests, fast, to keep them happy) fails for platforms. Engineering customers don't know how long things take, misjudge what their future selves will need, and often haven't thought hard about the problem. If they then don't adopt what they asked for, you get the blame. Look instead for revealed preferences: what people actually do. And ask questions that can't all be answered "yes, the best one". Which question would you ask?

Product management is not stakeholder managementStakeholder management is partly horse trading, power structures and covering yourself; you can do it brilliantly and still ship bad systems. Product management is betting on what the company really needs and measuring the impact. You need both (stakeholders are Chapter 10). And when PMs arrive, engineers must keep talking to customers too.
3

Escaping the feature shop trap

A cloud enablement team unblocks each new cloud service for each team. Fine at low demand. Then a company-wide cloud push arrives, every group wants its service first, someone important jumps the queue, and soon all product time goes into picking and justifying tickets: "enablement request Tetris". Two mistakes feed each other: scaling adoption before the architecture supports self-service, and assuming precise requests should be met precisely. Try steering out of it.

You don't need a PM title for this. A "lazy engineer" asks: do I really want to build variants of the same thing forever? How can customers plug in their own code for the app-specific parts while the platform owns the common ones?

4

Finding products worth building

Platform teams are rarely pioneers. They are more like settlers and town planners, taking ideas proven by small groups and making them broadly useful. Good ideas often come from other teams. Four ways in:

Smooth the edges, or rethink the problem?

Smoothing makes a task easier. Rethinking removes the need for users to think about it at all, which usually means you operate it for them. Start by trying to rethink. Classify each case.

5

Validating a new investment

Internal customers don't excuse you from market research. Product-market fit depends on context: conference talks skip the pain, and big companies' solutions lean on unwritten context. Google's build tool works so well inside Google because of everything around it; a monorepo shines with lots of compile-time-linked C++ and can hurt a microservice shop. Score a proposed product against the six checks.

Then tell people it existsGood products at big companies fail when nobody knows about them. An internal landing page, announcement lists and chat rooms, roadshows, and early-access communities who evangelize; at scale, maybe developer advocates.
6

Product metrics and impact theories

At the top level, track the cost of using the platform (migration hours you impose), its benefit (time or money saved), demand (share of potential users adopting voluntarily) and opinion (CSAT). A guest section by product manager Leif Walsh goes deeper. Impact metrics need an impact theory: a causal chain from your work to a business KPI. Click a node; then test the theory.

7

From vision to features: the product roadmap

A familiar starting point: a compute group with people on OS images, configuration management, a legacy VM cloud, Kubernetes and Terraform. Each bit works, pairs cooperate, but nobody owns how it all fits, so developers get a disjointed experience. The roadmap ladder ties it together. Step down the ladder with the book's compute example.

Share only user-visible features on the customer-facing roadmap. Show the technical milestones when asked, but leading with them invites "you build what engineering thinks is cool".
