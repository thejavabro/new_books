How and When to Get Started

Start a platform team too early and you burn money on guesses; too late and the swamp has already set. This chapter is about timing, the first team, and how to turn an old infrastructure group into a platform group.

Straw, sticks or bricks?

Like the three little pigs, sometimes a quick house of straw is the smart choice: it frees time for work that matters more to the business. The trick is knowing when to start laying bricks. The chapter covers three starting points: a small startup, the moment cooperation stops working, and an older infrastructure organization that must change culture. Slide your engineering headcount.

Platforms at a startup: cooperation first

A guest section by James Turnbull offers a quick maturity model for roughly the first 50 engineers. Expect to get things wrong a lot: under-scale here, over-optimize there. The goal is to make new mistakes. In both stages the guiding question is the same: is this core to our business? If not, outsource it. Tick what your team already has.

3

When cooperation stops working

The trigger is often small: a slow build-up of debt, or a sudden acquisition or vendor change that exposes how unmanageable things have become. Nobody owns the problem, so a platform is born. Dunbar's number is a handy guide: past about 50 to 250 people in a cooperating group, nobody knows everyone and you need named owners. This happens early for infrastructure (everyone uses it) and later for data, frontend or external APIs.

Should this be centralized?

a rough total-cost-of-ownership test

The test is leverage, not efficiency. "Two engineers on a shared platform instead of five engineers part-time on similar code" isn't enough on its own, because every central offering is another coordination point for application teams.

Four traps for a brand-new platform team

New platform teams struggle at first even at companies that already have platforms: the old cooperation worked for a long time, and now people argue whether the platform or the customer must fix things. Tap each trap.

How many product and project managers?

the authors' rule of thumb for a mature org

Needing more, or sooner, usually means your abstractions push too much change work onto users. And hire for "process fit": people who can operate without big-company guardrails.

5

Bonus problems for shared-services platforms

Billing, identity, mobile and web frameworks, revenue systems and shared services such as search or notifications sit higher in the stack and touch external customers. Two extra questions come with them. First, product management: hire a PM early here, but not just any business PM, because many will push reliability and developer productivity aside until customers feel the pain. Watch for the bike shed and the nuclear plant.

Where does the attention go?

Second, discoverability. Semi-optional offerings get missed, and then product X ships its own search and the customer notices. Make a plan to be found, starting with the name. Which of these would someone actually find on the intranet?

Pick the two discoverable names.

Stuck in the middleIntegration teams sit between apps and the core infrastructure org, often without superuser access, catching errors from both sides. Keep them aligned with core platforms (an API gateway is a classic shared boundary) and build camaraderie: joint projects, shared events and conferences.
6

Transforming a traditional infrastructure organization

Infrastructure groups are good at cost, vendors, scale and deep specialist debugging. They are usually less good at treating users as customers they must keep, often because those users are a captive audience. The whole culture has to change, without losing the rare skills. Turn on the levers and watch the culture shift.
