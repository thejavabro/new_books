Migrations and Sunsetting of Platforms

Every platform eventually asks its users to move. Migrations burn goodwill faster than anything else, so the platform should carry as much of their cost as it can.

1

Migrations: the platform's chance to prove its value

Underlying systems never stop changing: security patches, vendor changes, new hardware. End-of-life cycles that once lasted a decade now run one to two years for some managed cloud products. Without a plan, a platform becomes a source of migration pain bigger than its value. Great teams see migrations as the opportunity to show what they are for. Here a migration is any mandatory change that needs some user work. How much work depends on where it sits on the spectrum.

Four antipatterns keep recurring at big companies. Deadlines and clipboards are sometimes needed, but only as a last resort.

2

Engineer migrations to be easier

Before program management and rules, reach for engineering. Start early: migration support should be one of a new platform team's first jobs, using whatever scrappy work makes the next migration cheaper. Limit glue and the number of versions you offer, so there are fewer permutations to change and test. Best of all is a migration users never see: a stable surface on top, with old and new running side by side underneath and traffic moving across as clients update. That needs agreements with users up front. Turn them on and shift traffic.

Do monorepos help?For pure in-house library changes, yes. For platforms, less: services mean old clients stay in production until customers redeploy on their own schedule, and thick clients pull in OSS libraries that customers use for other things too. Versioning clients helps, but undoes the monorepo's point.
3

Automate instead of hiring clipboards

Know who uses what: which apps use which parts, and who owns them. Track ownership centrally, and partner with HR on a system of record so that jobs, pipelines and cron jobs get reassigned when people leave. Then automate. Camille's base OS team, still behind on its last Linux upgrade, asked for project managers. Her answer: prove you've automated everything first. They mapped the upgrade as a dependency tree that released each team's ticket only when what it depended on was done. Try it.

On-ramps, off-ramps and docsWhen users must move themselves, show them how to move part of a system first and test it. Migrations are the one place where professional, usage-level documentation pays off, because it is written once. Find gaps by dogfooding with other platform teams, then embed with keen early-adopter customers.
4

Coordinating smoother migrations

Scope backward from hard deadlines within the next 12 months. Dates further out, however "hard", tend to slip, and the industry often ships a shared fix (as AWS did for one VPC migration in 2015). 5

Pushing through the final 20%

After automation and communication, maybe 80% is done. The rest is the oldest, riskiest, most customized, and the last 5% can look impossible. Plan for it.

6

Sunsetting: a migration to nothing

Removing a system with no near-equivalent is sunsetting. For widely used systems, first try to build a path to another offering so it's a migration instead. True sunsetting is for the tail. Work through the decision.

Sometimes the builders resist, not the usersThe authors once sunset a build-and-test tool a team had pushed for years. Few teams used it, and the original problems had largely been fixed. Convincing its believers was as hard as removing it, and it only finished after some of them left. Teams staffed on passion for a technology tend to leave if it fails.
