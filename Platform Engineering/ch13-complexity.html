Your Platforms Manage Complexity

A platform exists to manage complexity, yet platforms themselves grow complex. Success is when the total complexity the company carries goes down.

1

Platforms manage complexity; they don't remove it

The book's whole "why" is complexity slowing application teams down. Platforms win leverage by managing complexity, never eliminating it. Four areas need watching: accidental complexity (pushing work onto humans), shadow platforms, uncontrolled growth, and product discovery. And, echoing Don Norman, design for how people actually behave.

2

The accidental complexity of human coordination

One measure of success: how much glue app teams still write. But there's also human glue (Tanya Reilly's "glue work"): manual workarounds, docs and coordination filling gaps. A platform with no diagnostic tooling is a car with the hood welded shut: drivers can't fix the engine, but they need to see where the smoke comes from. Without it, platform engineers become human dashboards. Rule: keep humans for the truly complex; use software for the merely complicated.

Managing the complexity of shadow platforms

Shadow platforms raise total complexity while lowering it locally, so some platform work will always happen outside your team. Stopping all of it is a fool's errand at scale and blocks innovation. Use trust to stay informed: embed an engineer, get updates, agree what a future takeover would need. If you take one over, reduce the pioneer complexity and keep the new complexity inside your team. Managing complexity by controlling growth

Growth is addictive. Unchecked, it throws people at problems instead of automation (leaving dull, manual on-call and migrations), removes pressure to choose wisely, invites empire building, and supplies excuses. Platforms are often seen as cost centres, so efficiency through simplification is part of the job. Once leverage is established, a platform shouldn't grow as fast as engineering overall. The rule of thumb: new work in established areas is funded by the existing team. Find your floor.

5

Managing complexity through product discovery

Handing customers whichever OSS system they ask for leaves you with operations that grow linearly with users. Distributed data systems (relational databases, Cassandra, MongoDB, Kafka) expose huge feature surfaces, and vendors keep adding more. Everyone agrees on fewer choices; nobody agrees which ones. Between "let a thousand flowers bloom" and a rigid platform lies product discovery: learn what teams truly need versus merely prefer. Ian's data reliability team of about ten took four attempts.
