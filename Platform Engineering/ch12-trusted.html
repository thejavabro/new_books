Your Platforms Are Trusted

Users trust a platform that is stable, whose leaders are honest, and whose promises come true. Trust is earned slowly and lost fast.

1

Why trust comes before results

Value delivered continuously eventually earns trust. But your platforms are in production now, improvements take time, and delivering them needs customers' patience, partnership and willingness to test and adopt. Without trust, one bad event can wreck your roadmap. Platforms lose trust in three ways: operations that don't meet the scale needed, big investments started without buy-in, and becoming a bottleneck to the business. Trust builds slowly and collapses fast. Try it.

Red herring: trusting the leader, not the platformA benevolent dictator who settles every product, engineering and stakeholder question in 1:1s is efficient with a small team, and brittle. When the users outgrow one person, or that person burns out and leaves, there's no decision-maker and no trust in anyone else. Fine in scrappy and scalable stages; then start delegating, even though it is slower at first.
2

Trust in how you operate

On-call, SLOs, change management and operational reviews are essential, yet senior application engineers may still not trust you, showing up as standoffs, vague "proof of concept" demands, or pressure for a simpler shadow platform after a hiccup. Worse is the double problem: "they're bad at operating, and don't know it." As Amazon puts it, there is no compression algorithm for experience. Two levers remain: accelerate the curve by empowering operationally experienced leaders, and optimize it by ordering use cases by risk tolerance.

Trust in your big investments

Big investments need faith before there's proof, and they pull people from today's platforms, inviting talk of "resume-driven development". "Trust me, it's my team" invites trouble: users denied fixes "because of the rearchitecture" complain upward, and your roadmap gets flipped. Get trust up front: a documented decision process for management, a yearly proposal senior engineers in customer teams can critique (if they can't voice disagreement first, they won't commit later), executive sponsors for new products, and keep improving the old system until its load really falls.

Trust that you won't slow the business

A platform that blocks business delivery has questionable leverage, however good its long-term story. Outsiders underestimate the work, then lose patience. Planning helps, but businesses are uncertain by nature: if apps iterate every two weeks, "wait for next quarter's OKRs" kills momentum. Ian built a culture of velocity: resisting a new ask because it wasn't planned is not acceptable, and stakeholders learn that late asks cost more. Next, in a guest section, Diego Quiroga freed capacity by automating recurring requests. Try his approach.

Is your scope a built-in bottleneck?

Huge surface area + diverse apps + users you can't trust to unblock themselves (classic: central cloud enablement with security vetting). Check the mitigations you have.

5

The case of the overcoupled platform

After an era of siloed benevolent dictators, the org pushed "batteries included": end-to-end workflows so users never built or operated anything. Lovely in theory, like an Apple product. In practice, every use case had to be designed end to end first, shortcuts coupled components together, rearchitecture became impossible, and the answer became v2s, which slipped. Stakeholders said the org "builds new platforms for the sake of building new platforms". Camille's fix was a new objective: building blocks, not batteries included.
