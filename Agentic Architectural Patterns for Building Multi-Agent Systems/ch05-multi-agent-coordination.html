Multi-Agent Coordination Patterns

Once there is more than one agent, someone has to decide who does what, in what order, with which tools, and what happens when agents disagree. Fourteen patterns answer those questions, from a simple router to self-organising swarms.

The coordination pattern map

Coordination patterns are not tied to one maturity level; they get more dynamic and decentralised as you climb. Foundational multi-agent systems (Level 4) lean on central, predictable control. Advanced systems (Levels 5–6) add markets, debate and self-organisation. Slide along the spectrum.

2

Agent Router: turn a request into a route

The router does not guess an agent's name from keywords. Step 1: an LLM with a strict schema extracts an intent object (action + resource). Step 2: a capability graph says which agent can do that action on that resource. No edge, no route. Pick a request.


  






  
3
Task delegation: Supervisor or Swarm?

  
A Supervisor plans and calls each worker in turn. A Swarm posts tasks on a board and lets any ready agent pull the next one. Run both, then knock an agent out.

  

    

    

    

  






  
4
Contract-Net: let agents bid for the work

  
A training job must go to a cloud-provider agent. The solicitor broadcasts the task, agents bid with cost, ETA and confidence, and the award goes to the highest utility. Change what you care about and watch the winner change.

  

    

      

      

      

    

    

    

    
Risk to manage: agents may overstate confidence to win. Track delivered quality and feed it back into future bids (see Trust Decay in chapter 7).

  






  
5
Consensus: debate until the numbers agree

  
Four forecasting agents start with different revenue estimates. Each round they share beliefs and move toward the group according to a rule. Run rounds until they converge within tolerance, then act.

Negotiation and conflict resolution

  
Two data-processing agents want the same GPU cluster tonight. In negotiation they trade offers and counter-offers until both accept. When agents cannot settle it themselves, conflict resolution picks a mediator.

Formation Control: no leader, just neighbours

  
Agricultural drones keep a V formation by each watching only its neighbours. Drag the obstacle or knock a drone out: the formation bends and heals without a central controller.

  

    

    

    
Each drone steers toward its slot relative to its left neighbour and away from anything too close. Simple local rules, collective behaviour.
