Syllogisms

Aristotle's logic of "All S are P" and "Some S are not P" was the gold standard for two thousand years. Venn diagrams turn checking any of its 256 forms into a drawing exercise.

1

Aristotle's four sentence types

Aristotle built the first formal logic around categorical sentences relating two terms, a subject S and a predicate P. In first-order terms they use only unary predicates, which makes the syllogistic a small, decidable fragment of first-order logic. The traditional letters come from the Latin AffIrmo and nEgO.

AAll S are P∀x(S(x) → P(x))universal affirmative
ENo S are P∀x(S(x) → ¬P(x))universal negative
ISome S are P∃x(S(x) ∧ P(x))particular affirmative
OSome S are not P∃x(S(x) ∧ ¬P(x))particular negative
2

The square of opposition

The four types are tied together by logical relations, traditionally drawn as a square. Tap a relation. The bottom two relations (contraries, subcontraries) and the vertical ones (subalternation) assume existential import: that S is non-empty. Modern first-order logic does not assume it, so only the diagonals survive unconditionally.

3

Syllogisms: moods and figures

A categorical syllogism has two premises and a conclusion. The conclusion's predicate P is the major term and its subject S the minor term; the middle term M appears in both premises but not in the conclusion. The mood lists the types of major premise, minor premise and conclusion (e.g. AAA); the figure (1–4) says where M sits. With 4 × 4 × 4 moods and 4 figures there are 256 forms.

Terms:

Shading: region forced empty by a universal premise. ●: region forced non-empty by a particular premise. ◌ on a boundary: something exists in one of the touching regions, we don't know which.

5

How the Venn test works

Behind the drawing is a finite check: each of the 8 regions is either empty or not, so there are only 256 possible "situations". A form is valid when every situation satisfying both premises also satisfies the conclusion. That is exactly what this page computes, and why the syllogistic, unlike full first-order logic, is decidable by brute force.
