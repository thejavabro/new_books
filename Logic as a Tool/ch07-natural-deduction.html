Natural Deduction

Natural Deduction mirrors the way mathematicians actually argue: suppose something, reason forward, then discharge the supposition. Every connective gets a rule to build it and a rule to use it.

The ideaRulesWorked derivationsTacticsSoundnessQuick check
1

Reasoning the way mathematicians do

Axiomatic proofs are hard to find because they hide the reasoning inside axiom instances. Natural Deduction, introduced by Gentzen and Jaśkowski in 1934, has no axioms at all. Instead each connective has introduction rules (how to prove a formula of that shape) and elimination rules (how to use one). Some rules let you make a temporary assumption and later discharge it, exactly like "suppose A … therefore A → B" in a math proof.

Assumptions as boxesThe book draws derivations as trees with discharged assumptions marked. Here we use the equivalent line-by-line style: an indented, bracketed block is the scope of an assumption, and nothing inside it can be used after the box closes.
Two flavours of contradiction⊥ (falsum) is a formula that is always false. From A and ¬A we get ⊥; from ⊥ anything follows ("ex falso quodlibet"); and if assuming ¬A leads to ⊥, then A (reductio ad absurdum).

4

Which rule do I reach for? A tactics drill

The rules double as a strategy guide. Look at the goal: its main connective suggests an introduction rule. Look at the assumptions: their connectives suggest elimination rules. When stuck, try reductio. Choose the most promising first move.

5

Soundness, completeness and the intuitionistic twist

SoundEvery rule preserves truth: if the open assumptions are true, so is the conclusion. Checking this rule by rule is an exercise in the book.
CompleteEvery valid consequence has a derivation (§2.7 gives a generic proof).
Derived rulesOnce proved, patterns like Modus Tollens or De Morgan can be used as shortcuts.
Without reductioDrop RAA and you get intuitionistic logic, where ¬¬A → A and A ∨ ¬A are no longer derivable: a proof of existence must construct its object.
