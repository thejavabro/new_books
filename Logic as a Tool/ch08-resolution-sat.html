Normal Forms, Resolution and SAT

Flatten any formula into clauses, then apply one rule over and over until you reach the empty clause or run out of moves. This is how machines reason, and why SAT matters.

1

Conjunctive and disjunctive normal forms

A literal is a variable or its negation. A DNF is a disjunction of conjunctions of literals; a CNF is a conjunction of disjunctions of literals (the disjunctions are called clauses). Every formula has both. The quickest construction reads them off the truth table: each true row gives one conjunction for the DNF; each false row gives one clause, ruling that row out, for the CNF.

DNF: one term per true row
CNF: one clause per false row

The second method transforms the formula with equivalences: eliminate ↔ and →, push negations in (NNF, §1.3), then distribute ∨ over ∧ (for CNF) or ∧ over ∨ (for DNF). Distribution can blow the formula up exponentially.

2

One rule to rule them all: clausal resolution

Treat clauses as sets of literals. The resolution rule: from a clause containing p and one containing ¬p, derive their union minus that pair. Resolving {p} with {¬p} gives the empty clause □, a contradiction. To check A₁, …, Aₙ ⊨ B: convert the premises and ¬B to clauses and resolve until □ appears (valid) or nothing new can be derived (invalid).

Optimisations:
Clause set: premises + ¬conclusion
3

SAT: easy to check, (apparently) hard to solve

SAT asks whether a formula is satisfiable. It is in NP: a proposed assignment can be checked in time linear in the formula's length. It is also NP-complete (Cook, 1971): every NP problem reduces to it, so a fast SAT algorithm would make all of NP fast. Every known method, from truth tables to tableaux, resolution and modern solvers, takes exponential time in the worst case. Whether P = NP is a Clay Millennium Prize problem.

Be the solver

4

Why every system here is complete

The book's supplementary §2.7 gives one proof that works for all four systems. Call Γ consistent if it cannot derive ⊥. Then Γ ⊢ A iff Γ ∪ {¬A} is inconsistent, so completeness reduces to: every consistent set is satisfiable. To prove that, extend Γ to a maximal consistent set (Lindenbaum's lemma) and read off an assignment: make p true iff p is in the set. The truth lemma then shows every member is true.

Start: Γ = { p ∨ q, ¬p }. Enumerate formulas; add each one if that keeps the set consistent, otherwise add its negation.
