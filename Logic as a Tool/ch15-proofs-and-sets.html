Mathematical Proofs, Sets, Functions and Relations

Mathematical proofs are deductions in disguise. Knowing the logical shape of a goal tells you how to start, and set theory gives the vocabulary the rest of mathematics is written in.

Proof strategies: the global shape of a proof

A mathematical statement typically says "if these premises hold, then this conclusion holds". Whatever the subject, the logical skeleton of the proof comes from a small menu of strategies. A direct proof assumes the premises and reasons forward (Natural Deduction without reductio). An indirect proof assumes the premises and the negated conclusion and derives a contradiction (the tableau and resolution idea). Pick a statement and a strategy to see the skeleton.

2

Tactics: what the goal and the premises tell you

Tactics are local moves, read off the main connective of what you have to prove and of what you may use. They are Natural Deduction rules in disguise.

3

Sets: the language of mathematics

Naively, any property defines a set. Russell showed that fails: let R = {x : x ∉ x}. Is R ∈ R? Toggle and see. The fix is the axiomatic set theory of Zermelo and Fraenkel (ZF), where new sets can only be carved out of existing ones.

Functions: injective, surjective, bijective

A function f : A → B is a relation giving each element of A exactly one image. It is injective if different inputs have different images, surjective if every element of B is hit, bijective if both (then it has an inverse). Tap an element of A to move its arrow to the next element of B.

5

Binary relations, equivalences and orders

A binary relation on a set is any set of pairs. Toggle cells of the matrix (row x, column y means "x R y") and watch its properties. Equivalence relations (reflexive, symmetric, transitive) split the set into classes: a partition. Partial orders (reflexive, antisymmetric, transitive) arrange it in a Hasse diagram with minimal and maximal elements.
