Think like a hiring manager

The book leaves models until now on purpose. Once you know how to organize and coach a team of agents, you can ask the hiring question: who should be on it? Every model family and size has a different skill profile. Staff with that in mind and you get better results for less money. The cast: decoder-only models are generators and planners, encoder-only models are analysts, mixture-of-experts models are specialists, and reasoning models are the deliberate thinkers.

Decoder-only models and the KV cache

Decoder-only models generate one token at a time, attending only backward through a causal mask. Naively, every new token would recompute attention over the whole past. But the keys and values of earlier tokens never change, so they are cached and reused. That is a big throughput win paid for in memory, and the cache keeps growing with the sequence.

Causal attention, step by step

Rule of thumb: KV bytes ≈ tokens × layers × KV heads × head dim × 2 × bytes per value (the 2 is keys plus values). Modern models use grouped-query attention (GQA): many query heads share a few KV heads. Qwen 2.5 7B has 28 query heads sharing 4 KV heads, a 7× smaller cache.

Assumes 28 layers, head dimension 128, 28 query heads (MHA) versus 4 KV heads (GQA).

3

Keeping the cache honest: haystacks and invalidation

Long contexts have a second problem. Attention weights come from a softmax over all token scores. As the context grows the denominator grows, the distribution flattens, and the one token that matters stands out less: the haystack problem. Trimming old tokens or sliding a window helps memory and focus at the same time.

Context engineering (inserting, pruning or reordering what the model sees) interacts with the cache: cached keys and values are tied to token order and position. Change anything early and every cached block after it is stale; reusing it produces incoherent output. Try it. Keep stable things, like the system prompt, at the front so they stay cached.

4

Encoder-only models: the analysts

Encoder-only models such as BERT do not generate. They read the whole input in both directions, trained by reconstructing masked words, and produce representations for classification, retrieval and embeddings. No token-by-token loop and no KV cache, so they are fast and cheap. In RAG pipelines they are the retrievers and rankers. Newer designs (NomicBERT, ModernBERT, NeoBERT) add rotary positions and FlashAttention and stretch the context from 512 to thousands of tokens.

Rotary positional embeddings (RoPE) rotate query and key vectors by an angle that grows with position, instead of adding a position vector. The dot product of two rotated vectors depends only on their difference in angle, that is, their relative distance. Move both tokens along and the relation stays the same. Frameworks can rescale RoPE frequencies to stretch, say, a 32K model to 128K, though you should re-evaluate quality afterwards.

5

Mixture of experts: specialists on call

In a dense transformer every token goes through the same feed-forward network. A mixture-of-experts model replaces it with many expert networks plus a light router that activates only a few per token. The model holds huge capacity but spends compute only where needed: conditional computation. Routing variants include top-k, noisy gating, and expert-choice (experts bid for tokens).

6

Reasoning models: thinking modes and budgets

Reasoning models are usually decoders underneath; what changes is behavior. They are trained to produce intermediate thoughts, and some search their own reasoning space at inference. They are slower and often save the team from mistakes. Two controls matter: a thinking mode switch (on for planning, decomposition, tool choice, verification; off for short, low-risk turns) and a thinking budget that caps reasoning tokens, after which the model is nudged to answer. Hidden or not, those tokens are generated and billed.

Curves are illustrative. The book's rule of thumb: about a thousand tokens or more for nontrivial reasoning; more for math, code and multi-tool plans.

7

Putting it together: a staffed research team

The book's example staffs a research-and-writing system by skill, not by default. Fast decoders with thinking switched off do search and drafting. One deliberate reasoning model acts as analyst, with an encoder (ModernBERT) scoring each snippet's relevance as a cross-check. A stronger closed model supervises and decides when to finish. Expensive thinking is spent once, where it pays.

8

Open or closed models?

The choice shapes cost, compliance, speed and vendor dependence, not just quality. Closed APIs are instant and infrastructure-free but you never own the weights, even when you fine-tune them. Open models can be inspected, adapted and carried with you; managed open providers give a quick start before you self-host. The performance gap has narrowed a lot. The book suggests a hybrid path: start managed while workflows form, bring open models in-house as control, customization and data isolation matter more.

9

Making open models fit: LoRA, adapters, quantization

Full fine-tuning updates every weight and needs roughly four times the parameter memory during optimization. Parameter-efficient fine-tuning updates a sliver. LoRA learns a low-rank correction ΔW = B·A beside a frozen weight; adapters insert small trainable layers. Keeping the base frozen also guards against catastrophic forgetting. Servers like LoRAX load adapters on demand and batch requests for many adapters on one GPU. Quantization stores weights in 8 or 4 bits to shrink memory and speed inference.
