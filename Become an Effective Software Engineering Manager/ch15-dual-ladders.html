Dual Ladders

Engineers should not have to become managers to progress. Two parallel career tracks, with a clear framework, make growth visible and fair.

1

The career progression crisis

Over coffee, your friend Charlie admits the "promotion" was into management, because it was the only way up without waiting ten years. Their manager left, there was no budget to hire, and now they feel they get nothing done and miss building things. A management-skills crisis implies a career-progression crisis. Without defined tracks, you see:

The fix is two separate tracks, because they are separate jobs, and a framework that describes progression on each.

2

Two ladders side by side

Typical levels in a tech company (every company differs, and titles carry more weight in bigger organisations). The IC's currency is technical expertise and influence; they don't line-manage. The manager builds people instead of software. Tap a rung.

Compensation: ideally no glass ceilings and similar pay for similar contribution. Leadership bias shows when a company systematically pays one track more: "pure" tech firms may favour principal engineers; corporate ones, managers.

3

Why managers grow by adding managers

The surest way for a manager's output to grow is a bigger team, but a rule of thumb caps direct reports at about seven. No hard science, but an hour-long 1:1 each is already a day a week. So growth means splitting teams and promoting new managers.

4

Build a progression framework

A framework is a grid: roles across the top, competencies down the side. It's a compass, not a GPS: it helps people say where they are, discuss where they want to go and set expectations. It is not for ranking staff, blocking progression, or forcing anyone up a path they don't want. It works brilliantly as a team workshop.

Example grid (paraphrased)

Keep competencies as similar as possible across tracks so people can see the difference in focus. Track-specific rows are fine too (people below you; rough % time coding).

Other competencies to consider: conflict resolution, communication (technical and non-technical), efficiency and reusable work, and how each level lives your company's values.

5

Your turn: score yourself

Rate yourself on each competency. Nobody fits a single level everywhere, and that's the point: the shape shows strengths and bite-size areas to work on in your 1:1s.

6

Debugging career progression

Isn't a manager on both tracks? I code too.

Your track defines your primary function. Coding is great if it doesn't put management at risk; if it does, rethink your time. Be a good manager first.

What if someone hates management after trying it?

Build a safety net: a 30-60-90-day plan with their new manager, plus a get-out clause to return to their old role, no hard feelings. Otherwise you risk managing a high performer out of the company.

30-60-90 plan with a get-out clause

The Peter Principle

Laurence J. Peter: in a hierarchy, people are promoted for success until they reach a level where they're no longer competent. Compare a single ladder with dual tracks plus a safety net.

Promote a great engineer

Isn't progression subjective?

Yes. No checkboxes: they're impossible to measure honestly and easy to game. A promotion should ratify a level someone has almost reached, never be a surprise, and be backed by peer feedback over time. Think opening night after years of rehearsal, not being shoved into the spotlight.

Big differences between skill sets?

Make the main framework more generic, or let groups (say, sysadmins or mobile) add overrides and extra competencies.

"This doesn't work for my company!"

Why? Overcomplicated, management not seen as a separate role, unclear paths? Public frameworks (progression.fyi) give you ammunition. Other models exist too: a trident of management, tech leadership and IC, or swinging between tracks (the engineer/manager pendulum).
