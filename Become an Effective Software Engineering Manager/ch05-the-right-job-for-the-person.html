The Right Job for the Person

People are motivated in different ways and learn best at the edge of what they can already do. Matching the work to the person is one of the highest-leverage things a manager does.

Not the right person for the job: the right job for the person

Old-fashioned hiring training promises you'll always pick "the right person for the job". But technology changes constantly, and no single person can do everything. Look closely at any team and the differences in what drives people are striking. Take four engineers on a fictional team:

RosaseniorAt her best in an outage. Ambiguity and mess fuel her creativity.
IdrisseniorBuilt the search engine almost single-handedly. Happiest spending weeks on one deep problem and getting it exactly right.
MeijuniorWants to learn everything, from the UI to the infrastructure to production ops.
TomjuniorWants to become a deep React expert; frustrated by work that doesn't move him there.

All four want to grow, but in different ways. Your job is to shape the work so each one gets the right kind of challenge. The chapter uses three ideas: what motivates people (Maslow), how people learn (Vygotsky), and stability versus chaos (The Cathedral and the Bazaar).

2

The hierarchy of needs, at work

Abraham Maslow (1943) described five levels of human need, each one becoming reachable once the level below is met. (The pyramid drawing came later, reportedly from a management book.) Money mostly serves the bottom levels. Past "enough", more money doesn't make people happier, but more mastery and creativity do. That's why people leave well-paid, boring jobs, and why some take a pay cut to join a startup. Tap a level, and switch views.

Five levels

Where you come inThe company supplies pay, benefits and security. The top three levels (belonging, esteem, self-actualisation) are where a manager makes a lasting difference. Ping-pong tables don't get anyone up the pyramid. Interesting work, hard problems, strong relationships and candid feedback do.
3

The zone of proximal development

In the 1930s Lev Vygotsky argued that, for most subjects, children learn fastest on tasks they can't yet do alone but can do with help from someone more knowledgeable. Master those, and the zone moves outward to harder tasks. It sounds like common sense, but it implies two things: give people tasks inside their zone, and make sure there's someone to help. That's delegation and mentoring.

At task levelPair a less experienced engineer on a hard task with someone senior: one gains coding skill, the other mentoring skill. Hand your own decisions to someone who wants to manage one day, and coach them through.
At career levelBig goals ("be a CTO", "rebuild search at a giant") are far outside the zone today. Break them into a tree of steps that each sit just at the edge.
4

Career skill trees, borrowed from RPGs

Role-playing games let characters spend points on skill trees: master one skill to unlock the next, with several branches. Begin with the end in mind and you know the path. Use the same idea with a report's goal. Example: someone wants to speak at an international conference but isn't a confident speaker. Split the goal into speaking skill and a project worth talking about, then draw the first branch. Tap the skills in an order that works.

Locked skills need their prerequisites first.
5

Cathedral constructors and bazaar browsers

Eric Raymond's The Cathedral and the Bazaar contrasts two open-source styles: the cathedral, built carefully by a small closed group with long release cycles (GCC), and the bazaar, open to everyone, chaotic and fast, where "given enough eyeballs, all bugs are shallow" (Linux). The author reads them as two personalities. Some people are motivated by control and stability, others by chaos and change. Neither is right, and people can shift over their lives.

Where do you sit?

pick a side on each line

Cathedral constructors want
  • Subject-matter expertise: ownership of a core part of the stack.
  • To be a cornerstone: the person others consult; the scaffolding new work stands on.
  • Depth, not breadth: perhaps contributing back to open-source projects they know deeply.
Bazaar browsers want
  • Newness: put each new project in front of them first.
  • Build and throw away: prototypes and experiments.
  • No stagnation: staying put feels like stalling, not mastery.
  • Even a move: lend them to another team for a while.

Ask about it in 1:1s; your assumptions may be wrong. The person you kept on familiar work may be underperforming for lack of variety, and the "exciting" new challenge you gave someone else may just be uncomfortable. People at the extremes of either end can be harder to fit into teams and need careful placement.
