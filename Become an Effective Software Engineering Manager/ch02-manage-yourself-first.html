Manage Yourself First

Before you can look after a team, you need a way to stop dropping balls. This chapter builds a personal operating system: one inbox, a to-do list, a calendar you control, and a different idea of what your output is.

1

Why you need a system

Week two: production goes down, you miss lunch and induction sessions, and water floods your notebook. Every manager has days like this, and plenty more that die from a thousand small interruptions. Engineers have frameworks that protect their focus (Scrum sprints queue new work for later). Managers rarely do. This chapter gives you one: a way to capture, organise, prioritise, execute and measure your work.

The core principleKeep as little in your head as possible. Brains are poor at remembering a stream of small tasks under constant input. Write everything down in a small number of trusted places, and people will start to think you have a superhuman memory.

3

The calendar: only for time

Live your day by your calendar, and let it show at a glance where you are and where you'll be. Only three kinds of entry belong there: meetings, things that make you busy (lunch, out of office, a doctor's appointment), and blocks that stop people booking over you, such as a "no meetings please" focus window. Tasks and reminders go elsewhere. Make meetings public by default, turn on a 10-minute alert, and review and re-batch the day first thing each morning.

The to-do list: if it's not there, you're not doing it

Your to-do list is the only home for tasks. Emails, sticky notes on your keyboard, promises in the kitchen: they all go onto the list. Open it first thing in the morning and close it last thing at night. Software beats paper because of a few features:

5

Email: a small inbox and no hidden tasks

Make email your one primary source of incoming messages: set chat, tickets and other tools to notify you by email, so staying on top of email means staying on top of everything. Then follow simple rules: archive anything you're done with; if it needs action beyond a reply, put the action on your to-do list and archive the email; never delete (it's a record you'll want to search years later); unsubscribe from lists you don't read; and set a filter so a keyword from your reports jumps to the top. Read email in batches rather than watching it all day. Inbox zero is a guide, not a doctrine.

Four kinds of managerial work

As a programmer you could point at code and say "I did that". As a manager you work through others, juggle dozens of small tasks, switch context constantly and sit in meetings that produce no artifact. Andy Grove, long-time CEO of Intel, offered a way to see the value in that: almost everything you do falls into one of four activities.

8

What a manager's output actually is

Grove's rule of thumb for measuring a manager:

Your output = output of your team + output of neighbouring teams you influence

So you are accountable for what your team achieves; the team's output matters more than yours alone; and the nudging and role modelling you do outside your team count too. Nobody can measure this precisely, but it's a superb tool for deciding how to spend an hour. Try it: split a manager's week between activities and watch the (illustrative) output.
