1

Picking who to hire: look at the whole team

You've been given two headcount. Three traps await: always hire more engineers, always hire the most senior person possible, and hire people like yourself. Remember your output is the team's output, and modern teams are interdependent: engineers, design, QA, an agile practitioner, product. Ask where work slows down each week, which area has the least seniority, which outside dependency you'd like to bring in, and who the team would add.

Where's the bottleneck?

Work flows left to right; the team ships only as fast as its slowest stage. Add one hire.

2

Senior, fast, in budget: pick two

Project management's iron triangle says good, fast, cheap: choose two. Hiring has its own version. Select two corners.

You can't wait forever, so you'll interview as much for potential to grow as for current skill. And that's fine, because seniority has diminishing, even negative, returns.

3

Not culture fit: culture add

Culture isn't something people "fit" into (unless it's monolithic and dictatorial); people add to it. Tap each trait to sort it: something to look for, or something that shouldn't count.

Hiring friends?Fine, if they're good. But you're biased: keep yourself out of their interviews, and think about awkward situations, such as having to performance-manage a friend. If they join your team, treat them exactly like everyone else.
4

Write a job description people want to read

You're competing with hundreds of companies and have seconds of a reader's attention. A good description takes them on a journey: the company (why it exists, in plain words a non-engineer would get), the role (why it matters, with example projects, not a list of duties), who you're looking for (traits and potential, not a laundry list), salary and benefits (publish the range; healthcare and pension beat foosball), and how to apply (exactly what you want to receive).

Style excludes people without you noticingA widely cited Hewlett-Packard finding: men tend to apply when they meet about 60% of listed requirements, women when they meet all of them. Long requirement lists filter out good candidates. Research also shows certain words read as masculine- or feminine-coded. Tools exist to check; try a simplified version below.

5

The interview funnel

Design stages so you spend the least time on the people you're least likely to hire and the most on those you're most likely to. And everyone, at every stage, gets a good experience: some applicants have just been laid off and are supporting a family; others were headhunted and need selling to. Tap a stage.

6

Technical exercises without the torture

An interview isn't how engineers normally work, so the first interview is about whether you could work together, not a grilling. If you do a technical exercise, imagine you're teammates and you're mentoring them. Or step away from code: draw two stick figures with phones and design a chat app together, following them toward what they know best.

7

Making the offer

Decide in isolation: each interviewer forms a view before conferring, to avoid bias. All yes: go. On the fence: no. Your team pays for a weak hire. Then make a fair offer with a reason behind it, balancing three things. Never lowball; it's insulting. If they decline after all that effort, let it go.
