The Most Wonderful Time of the Year

Performance reviews have a bad reputation. Done with steady preparation through the year, they become a calm summary of conversations you have already had.

1

Seven myths about performance reviews

Remember a review where your manager asked "So, how do you think you've done?", picked holes in everything, gave no verdict, and then mentioned your 3% raise on the way out? Reviews don't have to be like that. Done well they are reflective, full of praise and useful critique, a springboard for the next six months, and the best moment to push your stars further and course-correct anyone heading the wrong way. Start by clearing away the myths. Tap each one.

2

Twice a year, plus every week

The author recommends a review every six months (say June and December), even if your company only asks for one. A year is too long to wait and quarterly feels too frequent. Six months gives enough work to reflect on and two chances a year to course-correct. The mid-year review also gets to be purely about performance, with no pay hanging over it. Meanwhile, performance comes up in 1:1s all year, so nothing in a review is new.

Start three weeks early, with a tracker

Reviews take a lot of preparation, though you get faster with practice. Writing takes time, peer feedback always takes longer than expected, and outages and holidays will intervene. Start about three weeks out and keep a simple tracker spreadsheet. Book the meetings first: just rename your existing 1:1 slots for review week. 4

Peer feedback from many angles

Gathering peer feedback is like herding cats, but it's where the most interesting information comes from. For each person pick three to five reviewers from as many viewpoints as possible, not just their two closest teammates. Ask simple questions by email: what have they excelled at in the last six months; what should they do differently, and why; anything else; and should it be anonymous? Give two weeks, nudge once after a week, and if a reply is a couple of one-liners, ask them to expand.

5

The written review: shared, and read before the meeting

The written review is the sun the meeting orbits. Use your company's form if it has one, or a shared document in three parts: their reflections, your observations, and goals you agree for the next six months. Both of you write and read the first two parts before the meeting. Budget about an hour per person for your part; give your staff real time for theirs (even a ticket in the backlog).

The form

Why read it beforehand? People getting bad news tend to pass through five stages. Reading the review in advance lets them work through the first four privately, so the meeting can start at the useful one.

The five stages of bad news

6

On the day: three equal thirds

Reread the form and feedback beforehand. Then don't walk through the document line by line; you've both read it. Split the hour into three, keep the thought bubble over their head, and don't dwell on critique they've already absorbed.

7

Talk about money separately

If the end-of-year review is also where a raise is revealed, nobody hears the performance conversation. Before the reveal they're waiting for it; after it they're planning a holiday or seething. Tell people up front that pay will be discussed at a different time, and ask how they'd like to hear it (email or face to face). When you do, bring context: how is it calculated, how much is personal versus company performance, is everyone getting the same? Expect some disappointment, leave the door open, and turn it into a conversation about the career path to the income they want.
