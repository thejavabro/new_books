A New Adventure

Nobody hands new managers an API reference for humans. The book opens with why management is a learnable craft, then gives your first week a structure: meet everyone, book the recurring meetings, and build a snapshot of how things really are.

1

Management is a craft you can learn

Most engineers practised programming for years before anyone paid them for it. Most managers get no practice at all: a team needs a lead, someone says yes, and on Monday they are in charge. They learn by watching whoever managed them, which is a lottery. The author's argument is that this is a skills gap, not a talent gap. Computers don't write software, people do, and people need good managers to do their best work.

The book promises no heroics, war stories or slogans, just the routines and tools of the job, in the same spirit as a book that teaches you a new programming language.

Management vs leadershipManagement is the method: the meetings, systems and tools you use to do the job. Leadership is what happens when you use those tools well and add humility, care, candour and consistency. You don't pick one. Doing management well, with empathy, is how you become a leader.
An art, not a scienceLike writing software, it's a craft. You get a little better every week. Your own personality, values and judgment are part of the toolkit.

2

Day one: you are the new deployment

The book frames you as a newly hired Engineering Manager for an Infrastructure team. Whether you joined a new company or were promoted internally, the first week raises the same questions: what am I meant to do with my time? What is my output? How is my performance measured? How has my relationship with old peers changed?

Impostor syndrome is normalHigh achievers often feel they are faking it and will be found out. Treat that caution as a sign that you care about doing right by others. You were picked for a reason, and confidence comes with repetitions.

Your first week, step by step

3

Meet each person, privately

Your team are the people through whom you do your work, so the relationship comes first. Meet each person one-to-one, in person or on video (not chat), for about 30 minutes at a time that suits them. Keep it informal and aim to uncover two things: what the team is responsible for and working on, and what is going well versus what is hard.

The trick for people who find small talk tiring: ask open questions and let them do most of the talking. Keep the "thought bubble" over their head. Try it below. Each response you pick moves the talk-time meter.

4

Book weekly one-to-ones, around their flow

Meetings are where much of a manager's work gets done, so get the recurring ones in now while your calendar is empty. Rules of thumb: weekly, same time and place, an hour to begin with, in a private room. Ask each person what time of day suits them.

Why ask? Paul Graham's maker's schedule vs manager's schedule: managers live in hour slots, while makers need half-day blocks to reach flow, the absorbed state (named by Mihály Csíkszentmihályi) where hard debugging gets done. One badly placed meeting can wreck an afternoon. Tap a slot to place a 1:1 in an engineer's day.

Then do the same with your own manager, and don't wait for them to invite you. Ask more guided questions: their view of the team, of individuals, of the current project and its history, how best to work with them, and advice on the role. Finish by setting up a weekly one-to-one with them too.

5

Build a snapshot from three views

A snapshot is the state of a system at a point in time. You have no backup to restore from, so you build one. There are three views of any team: yours, your manager's, and the team's. Overlay them like a Venn diagram and each overlap tells you something. Tap a region.

Where does each observation fall?

The overlaps apply to good news too. A team doing well that doesn't know it also needs telling.

6

Turn the snapshot into an action list

Before the weekend, sort what you found into two buckets. If you uncovered months of material, rank items by impact so the list stays manageable. You won't fix everything at once; the point is to know where to start next week.

Bucket 1

Talk about with the team

Poor communication downward (why are production bugs high? QA, deadlines, something else?) and false beliefs you can challenge with an outside view.

Bucket 2

Talk about with your manager

Poor communication upward (why can't they see the team is understaffed? how is performance being measured?) and false beliefs, for a second opinion.

Keep

Alignment

Shared views are your firm ground. Build on the positive ones; the shared problems are often the easiest to get agreement on fixing.
