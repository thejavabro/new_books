One-to-Ones

The weekly one-to-one is the bedrock of your relationship with each person on your team. It is their meeting, not yours, and a little structure makes it far more useful.

1

The highest-leverage hour of your week

Give Archimedes a long enough lever and he'll move the world. Managers look for the same thing: small inputs with outsized outcomes. The weekly one-to-one is one of the biggest levers you have. Over years it will cover everything: good times and bad, promotions, struggles, careers and lives. Done well, it builds the relationship that everything else depends on.

Hence the rule: every week, without fail. Regularity makes you predictable and shows you're there for people week in, week out.

2

Set the stage: time, place, shared agenda

Same time, same place each week (video if anyone is remote), moved only when you truly must. Both of you will start batching topics for it. Hold it somewhere private: a public spot risks being overheard and quietly signals you'd rather not hear private matters. Then create a shared private document per person as a rolling agenda and record. Both of you jot items in it through the week. And book the same with your own manager.

Where should it happen?

3

Contracting: your first one-to-one

First meetings are awkward. In a new company you don't know how formal to be. Promoted from within, it can be worse: a close peer is now your report, and perhaps someone else applied for your job. Take a cue from business contracts, where both sides set out what they need. Contracting is a short set of questions that both of you answer about each other, in a structured, safe way that sets the tone for candid conversation. Sending the questions ahead tends to produce better answers. Step through them.

From the author's own sessionsA former peer kept most of their decision-making autonomy so the change didn't feel like a demotion. Someone who expected him to be domineering got explicit permission to call it out. Another wanted a higher profile, so that went into their projects. Revisit the contract as needs change.
4

It's their meeting, not yours

Paradoxically, your best chance each week to help someone only works if you don't run it. Keep the thought bubble over their head: ask leading questions, nudge, and above all listen. Aim for them doing about 70% of the talking. When you feel the urge to solve their problem, ask another question instead. Leading questions work whether or not you know the answer.

Before each meeting, spend a few minutes on the agenda. Build one for a 60-minute 1:1 below.

Updates are the boring part1:1s are not status meetings; tickets already hold that. Keep updates minimal and make them interesting: "How could we deploy that faster?" "What are the alternatives?" "Is there an open-source tool for this?"
Silence is goldenLet a sentence trail off without jumping in. "…and that's the problem, I guess. I just don't know why they aren't helping us more." The real issue often surfaces in the pause.

Watch the pause work

5

Pull the andon cord

Toyota production lines had cords any worker could pull on spotting a defect; the whole line stopped until it was fixed (andon means sign or signal; the book cites Mike Rother's Toyota Kata). Agree on a metaphorical cord for your 1:1s: if either of you is uncomfortable with a topic, because it's too personal, off track, or needs someone better placed, stop, ask why, sort it out, then carry on.

Topics flowing normally.
6

Notes in the open, actions in bold

Take notes in the shared document as you go, often on the meeting-room screen; they aren't secret. Bullet points, with actions in bold, reviewed at the end. Over months the documents become a history you can scan. Above all, put your actions on your to-do list. Many people feel their manager never acts on what they raise; be the exception. The author's recurring 1:1 task has two subtasks: prepare notes before, move actions to the to-do list after.

After the meeting

7

Remember: you are not a therapist

Good listeners attract confidences, and 1:1s can drift into therapy. You're not trained for it, and the person deserves better support. Signs to watch for: sessions that are only venting with no way forward, deep personal issues unrelated to work, or signs someone may be unwell.
