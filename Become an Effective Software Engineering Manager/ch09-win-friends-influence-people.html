How to Win Friends and Influence People

Once your team is running, your impact grows by working across the organisation: building a network, mentoring and coaching, and contributing to the wider department.

1

Why look beyond your team

Picture a fellow manager who can't cross the kitchen without someone thanking him: a sales lead, an engineer whose bug he helped with, the CEO wanting him to meet a big customer. How is he so connected? This chapter is about the second half of the output equation: the output of the people you influence. Influence isn't sinister. Two of Grove's four activities are influence: nudging another team's decision with your experience, and role modelling behaviour you want to spread. More connections mean more chances to help, and helping builds connections. Watch the loop.

It's harder for introverts (the author counts himself as one), and your manager can't make every connection for you. It takes deliberate effort.

2

The snow melts at the periphery

Andy Grove's Only the Paranoid Survive notes that change is seen first at the edges: salespeople facing competitors and customer-success staff talking to clients see shifts in the market long before Engineering does. Connect with them and you can spot quick wins, build rapport with future stakeholders and join strategic discussions.

A market signal travels inward

3

Build your network, one coffee at a time

Ask peers and your manager who they work well with, who the decision-makers and interesting people are elsewhere. Think of someone you admire in your department, the most visible salesperson, someone in marketing, a power user of your product. Then email out of the blue; nobody minds a polite introduction. Keep in touch either formally (a quarterly email with a leading question) or informally (monthly coffee, in person or on video, no agenda). Every conversation mentions someone else worth meeting.

4

Giving back: mentoring

Mentoring is a teacher-student relationship: you share specific skills and experience to help someone progress, with structure (regular meetings, goals) over months or years. You already have plenty to teach: engineering skills to juniors, what you've learned from this book to new managers, speaking or writing or open source. Mentors sharpen their management skills, widen their impact, learn about other teams and may meet future hires. Mentees gain skills, a wider network and an impartial adviser who doesn't judge their pay.

Mentees should initiate, so create the conditions: a shared mentorship matrix listing each person's name, title, team, contact, skills sought and skills offered. Try matching:

5

Coaching: help anyone, even experts

You can't mentor someone in a skill you don't have, so how do you help a specialist with far more experience than you? Coaching: a way of conversing that helps people solve their own problems. No whistle, no special planning; you can do it in a 1:1 or by the kettle. Conversations swing between two modes: directive (push: you solve it) and following interest (pull: you listen, reflect, summarise, and they solve it). Juniors may need more direction; with experience you'll mostly follow.

Coach across the companyOnce you're good at it, offer regular sessions to people elsewhere. As a neutral party you can be close without judging their performance, and help other areas without meddling.
Get coached yourselfAs you become more senior, some problems are too sensitive for peers or reports. Ask the company about an external coach; executives may know someone affordable.
