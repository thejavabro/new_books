Startups

Startups move through phases quickly, and good management is often missing exactly when it matters most. That creates opportunities for managers who want to grow fast.

2

Software is eating the world

Marc Andreessen's 2011 phrase stuck: ride-hailing, food delivery and dating apps changed habits overnight. A good idea plus venture capital can create a unicorn (valued over $1 billion) in a fraction of the time an old-economy giant took. Early employees with equity can get rich, and startups offer big problems, fast careers, decisive founders, perks, and cultures that reward impact over politics. But do they need a manager?

4

Becoming manager number one

After the first investment, a founding team usually has a CEO (sales, hiring, plans) and a CTO who is really the lead engineer building the product. Nobody is managing process, hiring, communication and delivery. That person, often titled VP Engineering, builds the engineering organisation while the CTO builds the product. It's an engineering manager job with accountability, risk and autonomy turned up to 11.

Engineering team matrix

A good VP Engineering turns a band of pirates into a naval vessel, with room for the fleet to grow. If the startup succeeds you may become a manager of managers far sooner than you would in a large organisation. Watch for roles where the team is still in the low tens, and use your network to find ones not yet advertised.

5

Catching and surfing a wave

The management track has two hard problems: landing the first management job (experience wins interviews), and always having room to grow (big orgs can run out of rungs). Fast-growing startups help with both: when an engineering team doubles or triples, flat structures need managers, and you're already there.

Grow the team

Startups also let you sample roles: full stack instead of back end, product design, the roadmap, even angry customers.

6

Risks, pay and equity

Most startups fail: no product-market fit, no more money, or so many pivots that the job isn't what you signed up for. Base pay is usually lower; equity (stock options vesting over years) fills the gap on paper. Even for successes, turning shares into cash can take a decade. Go for the experience; treat everything else as a bonus. Get advice on any equity terms (amount, share class, vesting).

The upside that isn't money: you see the whole business at once. A year at a startup can feel like five elsewhere, and startup experience (enterprising, self-motivated, quick to learn) makes your next job better even if the company folds.
