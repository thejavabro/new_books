1

Humans are harder than software

A data science team presents six months of work on a hard problem to senior leadership. The sales director accuses them of not working hard enough ("sales are in 7 till 7"). The VP insists competitors do it with off-the-shelf machine learning in two weeks, and suggests outsourcing. The nervous team forgets to mention its useful prototypes. Everyone leaves angry. This chapter explains the forces at play: scrutiny, wobble, the whip and the carrot, and Mount Stupid.

2

Scrutiny and judgment, in both directions

Like public figures, people become more visible, and more scrutinised, as they go up the org chart. You start to represent ideas and values, not just yourself: celebrated in good times, blamed in bad ones even when it isn't your fault. You'll have bad days too: anger, a broken principle, a botched launch. How your reports react sits on a scale. Pick a reaction.

You are often the catalyst, not the causeFurther down the scale needs more work: extend the 1:1, go for a coffee, dig for the real cause (pay, growth, daily frustrations). And when you feel daggers toward your manager, start from empathy: they face even more uncontrolled emotion, plus their own pressures. Ask how you can help.
3

Wobble: the organisation is a jelly

Picture your organisation as a cone-shaped jelly: executives at the narrow top, individual contributors across the wide base. A row in a team is a prod near the base: it shakes and settles. A panic among VPs or the C-suite, over a pivot, a reorg or the company's health, is a prod at the top, and the whole thing wobbles. Leaders are role models; when they lose their heads, everyone below panics without even knowing why. Prod the jelly.

The whip and the carrot

Engineering is opaque to outsiders: you can watch a stonemason carve, not a developer think. So the CEO tells you your team isn't working hard enough: they don't stay late and they play table tennis. Before boiling over, find what's behind it. Usually it's a mismatch between what the observer expects to see and what's happening:

Outsiders expect you to use the whip: work harder, faster. It doesn't work on engineers. Demand far outstrips supply, so fear of losing the job doesn't bite; they're self-motivated, so pressure gets in the way of the good work they want to do; and fake deadlines are seen straight through. Mount Stupid and impostor syndrome

Two mirror-image effects. Dunning–Kruger (1999): in many domains, people with low ability overrate themselves, and don't realise their decisions are bad; as skill grows, so does awareness of limits. The over-confident peak is nicknamed Mount Stupid (after a well-known web comic). Impostor syndrome (described in 1978): high achievers can't internalise their success and fear being exposed.
