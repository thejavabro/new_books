Management has a tooling problem

A programmer starting a new job brings a toolkit: languages, an IDE, a mental model of how programs run. A new manager often sits in front of the metaphorical text editor with no idea what to type. This chapter supplies three basic tools, each mapped onto something engineers know:

2

Three mediums, many channels

You communicate in three ways: spoken (meetings to coffee-machine chats), written (email, documents, pull request comments, chat) and nonverbal (posture, expression, tone). Each channel has traits. Face-to-face gives human connection but is synchronous and hard to steer. Email is formal, archival and asynchronous. Shared documents formalise ideas like a design. Chat feels synchronous: great for quick questions, poor for formal discussion. Ticket trackers solve one workflow well.

Pick the medium for the recipient, not for your convenience, and make every message self-contained or give it a call to action.

Feedback: care personally, challenge directly

The book borrows Kim Scott's Radical Candor model. Good feedback needs two things at once: you care personally about the person, and you challenge directly by saying exactly what you think. Move the sliders and see what your feedback on a sloppy pull request sounds like.

5

Delegation: hand over responsibility, keep accountability

Delegation is transferring a task to someone on your team. Two failure modes are common. The hoarder does everything, drowning in 600 things while the team sits idle (often a strong engineer who never made the switch). The fire-and-forget manager hands a task over and forgets it exists, then is surprised when it's late, wrong or never done.

The fix is one distinction. Accountability: you answer for the task being done to the right standard. Responsibility: you're the one doing it. Delegating moves responsibility but keeps accountability with you. Hoarders keep both; fire-and-forget gives away both, which is really abdication.

6

The scale of delegation

Delegation isn't on or off. Between doing it yourself and handing it over completely sits a range of teaching, coaching and check-ins. Full delegation only works when the person can do the task to your standard with no guidance. Each task is placed separately: nobody, however senior, is expert at everything. And control runs the other way: the more you delegate, the less control you keep. The rule: given the control the task needs, delegate as much as you can.

pick a point, then compare
DoDelegate: a team out-produces one person. Delegate enough to stretch people. Keep more control on critical, highly visible work. Judge outcomes, not process.
Don'tAbdicate. Expect people to work the way you would (that's micromanaging). Snatch the task back in frustration: slide left on the scale instead, and keep it a learning opportunity.
7

Working with your own manager

Pull, don't push. Don't dangle on strings waiting to be told what to do. Book the meeting, start the conversation, ask for help. Your manager is busy too, and probably wants a partner.

Draft your weekly summary: the four Ps

30 minutes, every week

A short written update, sent before your 1:1 or on Friday afternoon. It works like rubber-duck debugging for management: writing it down often shrinks the problems. Tell your manager they needn't reply, but comments are welcome.
