The Information Stock Exchange

Managers sit on a flow of information, some of it sensitive. Deciding what to pass on, when and to whom, is a skill, and so is navigating the politics around it.

1

Information is your currency

An engineer leans over: there's a rumour the CTO leaked customer data to another company. "Trust me. You should tell the team." Minutes later the CTO herself stops by, perfectly normal. Did it even happen? The more you know, the better your decisions, but information also comes as gossip, rumour and malice. You'll see more of it the longer you manage.

2

Be a gatekeeper, not a spy

Espionage means obtaining secret information without permission, often for someone else's benefit, often by deceit. Never do any of those. (The author was once accidentally sent the whole department's salary spreadsheet; he told Finance and deleted it unopened.) And beware people trying to prise information out of you, piece by piece, at the bar.

The better model is the gatekeeper, like a news editor filtering what reaches the public. You decide what is heard by whom, when, and how it's framed. Two ancient rules guide every call: first, do no harm (the Hippocratic oath) and treat others as you'd want to be treated (the golden rule).

3

The trend is toward transparency

Medicine wrestles with this constantly: how to tell someone a diagnosis transparently and kindly. Its default has shifted: in a 1961 survey only 10% of physicians thought it right to tell patients the details of a fatal cancer diagnosis; by 1979 it was 97%. Some companies go far: Buffer publishes every salary. The easy option is silence; the right default is share unless there's a critical reason not to, with care in how.

What managers hold: compensation (including unfairness to fix, like an over-negotiated salary next to an underpaid peer), performance issues (a PIP stays private), wider company changes (an unfinished reorg), and redundancies.

4

Three boxes, shared consistently and just enough

Completely confidentialNot sharedOnly those with authority know. Example: a redundancy list.
Closed boxExistence shared, contents notLike a present under the tree. Everyone knows pay reviews are happening; nobody sees anyone's numbers until it's done.
Open boxShared widelyNot sensitive, e.g. who is on which team.

Two rules: be consistent across people, and share just enough. We're wired to share (toddlers point at things so we look), so fight the urge. Share only if it's relevant to their job, they have a reason to know, it helps you both, it's only what they need, and they can be trusted. The trickiest box is the closed one: many people treat it as confidential, and in the absence of information, people assume the worst ("no pay rises this year!") when the truth may be good (more time spent benchmarking pay).

5

Workplace politics: use it for good

Politics is simply how groups make decisions, and building software is all decisions. It arises from the continual negotiation, persuasion and debate among people with different interests. Backstabbing is only a subset. Opting out hurts your career; the art is to navigate it for the greater good. Tension comes from three structures:

The org chart

Debates with your boss or reports; people reaching up or down to push ideas.

Close-knit groups

Friends and allies with no formal power who lobby together.

Influential individuals

Tenure, celebrity or leverage give them outsized say; hard to build consensus without them.

Positive
  • Map the landscape: who decides, who influences, which groups align or rival; then introduce yourself.
  • Build consensus: informal chats with senior, influential people first, then a proposal or proof of concept they can sponsor. Always a proposal.
  • Be yourself: open, respectfully critical, willing to be wrong. No favourites.
  • Disagree and commit: once decided, everyone backs it.
  • Use your network to trial ideas safely.
Negative
  • Misusing power: orders instead of hearts and minds.
  • Level-jumping: going over or under someone's head leaves them stranded.
  • Being unprofessional: erodes the trust senior roles need.
  • Going rogue: secret changes are like tech debt, worse the longer they run.
  • Personal gain: e.g. forcing a hire for the referral bonus. Seriously bad juju.
