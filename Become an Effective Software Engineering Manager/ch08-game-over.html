Game Over

Everyone leaves eventually. How you handle departures, voluntary or not, says a lot about you and shapes how the rest of the team feels.

1

People leaving is normal

Someone asks for a room, doesn't sit down, and hands in their notice. You mumble "sorry to hear that" and only afterwards think of everything you should have asked: where, why, money, project? It will happen to you. Get comfortable with it: you'll never win against natural turnover, and expecting to keep everyone forever only makes each departure hurt more.

LinkedIn data reported in 2018 put annual turnover in the US tech sector at 13.2%, the highest of any sector, just above retail. Simulate a department:

100 people, year by year

Turnover can even help. People hired for one stage of a growing company may not suit the next, and departures create room to hire for today's challenges. Departures come in two kinds: voluntary (they choose to go, sometimes "regrettable") and involuntary (you make them go, usually for performance). Your bar is only as high as your worst performer.

2

Good reasons, and the ones that catch you out

Careers today are mobile: people move cities and companies to progress, and couples juggle two careers (academia's "two-body problem"). Many departures are for good reasons: no room to grow here, an exciting new role, a fresh start, a move for a partner. You did nothing wrong; be the facilitator. Agree an end date and a handover plan, offer a reference, and part on good terms. Then there are the preventable surprises: someone quietly furious about pay, or unable to stand a teammate, who never felt able to say so. Sort the scenarios.

Good leaver or preventable surprise?

The antidote is the relationshipSurprises share a cause: no open, candid relationship. Talk about career progression regularly (six months, two years, beyond), make it safe to raise pay and coworker problems, and take a real interest in people's lives and hopes. The clues surface early.
3

Fight for them, or let them go?

When notice arrives you're already on the back foot: they've spent time and emotion on applications and interviews, and may have been mentally elsewhere for weeks. And keeping someone always costs something, such as a pay imbalance or reshuffled projects. Walk through the decision.

Decision walkthrough

4

Making staff leave: the performance improvement plan

Poor performance lowers your output, frustrates teammates and sets your bar. A PIP is a formal tool for forcing a resolution when ongoing conversations have failed. It is never a surprise, never treated lightly (the worst outcome is losing their job), and never a firing in advance: unachievable PIPs are unethical and may be unlawful. Either outcome resolves the situation: they improve, or they leave and you can hire.

Are these PIP objectives good?

Objectives must be measurable with evidence, achievable, and specific about times and actions.

5

Layoffs, and the hardest meeting

Redundancy

Nobody did anything wrong

A business decision from above, usually because targets were missed, and often a surprise to protect against rumours. Ask for a script and the severance details if you aren't given them; you shouldn't make it up. Stay calm, stick to the script, listen to anger without arguing, and be clear you can't change it. It will hurt you; it hurts them more.

Letting someone go

You do it yourself

Even if your manager offers. You were with them in good times; be there for the worst. Get HR and legal support. Write down what you'll say. Speak slowly, leave silences, expect emotion, stay centred: the decision is made. Afterwards, take a long walk before your next activity.
