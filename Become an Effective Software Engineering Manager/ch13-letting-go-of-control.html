Letting Go of Control

The habits that made you a strong engineer can trap you as a manager. Letting go of tasks, of being always on, and of work itself makes you more effective, not less.

1

The dark side of delegation

Friday night: you stare into space over dinner, scroll tickets in bed, refresh email though nobody will send any, then dream of compile errors and a man-eating bug ticket. Managers are bombarded with input and can become attached to it. Your work happens through other people, who will do it differently from you. Under stress the urge to grab control back is strong. Give in, and it eats you alive.

You already know the theory: delegation grows people (their proximal zone), builds autonomy and mastery, and multiplies your output. A quadruple win. But have you felt any of these?

2

Some things are up to us: the Stoic trichotomy

Stoicism (founded by Zeno around 300 BCE; Epictetus, Seneca, Marcus Aurelius) is a practical philosophy for eliminating anger, fear and worry through reason. Epictetus: "some things are up to us and some are not." William Irvine's A Guide to the Good Life splits that into three: things you control (your goals, your effort), things you don't control at all (the weather, the past), and things you partly control (winning a tennis match). Most managerial outcomes sit in the middle, because other people do the work. Sort these.

For the middle category, set internal goals (do my best, in this situation, now) rather than external ones (the outcome). Judge yourself on outcomes you can't control and you'll be unhappy all the time. Failure is acceptable; trying your best is the job.

3

Escape false productivity: make room for R-mode

Notifications breed an addiction to input: you feel productive only when every message is answered. Andy Hunt's Pragmatic Thinking and Learning models the brain as two CPUs on one shared bus. L-mode is linear and sequential: executing details. R-mode is asynchronous pattern matching: the shower insight. Only one runs at a time, and you can't command R-mode, only make space for it. (Kahneman's fast and slow thinking is a similar idea.)

Dual-CPU brain

Try blocking about 10% of your week for nothing: a walk, a sofa, no meetings, with a notebook for whatever pops up. Afterwards, turn the notes into to-do items for L-mode.

4

Plan for 85%, not 100%

Production blows up; someone needs to talk privately right now; budgets and reviews arrive. Plan your week at about 85% capacity so you can absorb the unexpected willingly and well, and be there for people. Managers running at 100% become the weak link: requests go unanswered, follow-ups get dropped. Try 30-minute buffers at the start and end of each day, take on 85% in sprint planning, and review a month of calendar to cut low-value activities.

And stop checking. Apps are designed for little dopamine hits. Batch email and chat into a few mindful sessions a day and close them otherwise. Try it tomorrow: when you finally look, had anything critical happened?

5

What you do outside work matters

Ignore the 4 a.m. "hustle" crowd; they're selling something. Around 13% of the world's population (about 971 million people in 2017) live with a mental disorder, and in 2019 the WHO classified burnout as an occupational phenomenon from chronic, unmanaged workplace stress: exhaustion, cynicism or mental distance from the job, and reduced effectiveness. If you think you may be struggling with anxiety or depression, talking to someone is not weakness: a partner, friends, family, your manager, HR, a doctor or a therapist. Many people do.

Sleep: the best performance enhancer

Matthew Walker's Why We Sleep: aim for eight hours. Sleep improves motor skills, stamina, mood, immunity and creativity; people tested after sleeping on a new skill do 20–30% better. You can't catch up at the weekend. Tick what you'll try:

Move your body

We didn't evolve to sit all day. Start small: walk or cycle to work, move five minutes every hour, hold walking 1:1s, take the stairs, then build up to whatever you enjoy, aiming at about 30 minutes a day.

Be present
