Good Housekeeping

Beyond your own team, a department needs good habits: structures that cross silos, a culture of sharing, learning from failure, and simple tools for recurring problems.

1

Didn't this happen before?

The storage cluster dies, again. Instances come back but nothing reconnects. A principal engineer wanders past, asks where the connection timeout default is, and points out there's a shared parent config every other team inherits. Restart, green. Days lost last month, all because nobody knew the config existed. Missed communication, poor documentation and crossed wires happen at every level. Without discipline, a department fragments into teams speaking different languages and repeating each other's mistakes.

You don't need to wait for the CTO. You can start these practices in your team, then spread them outward: a spotless house improves the neighbourhood.

Conway's Law: your org chart ships

In 1967 Melvin Conway observed that organisations design systems that copy their own communication structures. Eric Raymond's version: four groups on a compiler give you a four-pass compiler. Change the teams and watch the architecture follow.

4

Break silos with guilds

A guild is a group of people on different teams who share an interest or skill. Nobody needs permission to form one, so it's a tool to suggest to your staff, which builds their autonomy. Guilds advance best practice, share information across teams and raise a discipline's visibility. Spotify popularised squads, tribes, chapters and guilds; for a first attempt, just think "guild".

Do you need one? Hearing any of these?

Starting a guild

Guilds are multicast. Broadcast their output too: a monthly email, or a talk. And let them end, merge or split when their purpose changes.

5

Encourage a culture of talks

Speaking well needs practice, and most people rarely get any, so they pass up chances like a conference talk or a board presentation. You can create a safe place to practise. Start with team lightning talks: about five minutes, on a rota, maybe at the end of each sprint, sitting around a screen rather than on a stage.

Lightning-talk rota builder

Tips

  • Give prep time. Five minutes takes 30–60 to prepare. Put a ticket on the board.
  • Keep slides simple. People can read or listen, not both. Big words, few of them (the Takahashi method; PechaKucha is another format).
  • Go first yourself. Your first talk could be how to give a lightning talk.
  • Ask afterwards. Continue? Open it to the department?

Department talks of 20–30 minutes, maybe biweekly, need an owner: book the slot, gather ideas, advertise, make the AV work for remote people, record. The most valuable part is often the conversation afterwards.

6

Turn problems into learning: five whys

Fix the incident, then find the root cause. Ask "why?" until you reach it; five usually suffices. Write it up, create tickets, share. It teaches everyone, and signals that this was serious enough to stop and think.

Outage

The second case shows five whys used on assumptions, not just bugs. Humans work in abstractions and stop questioning them; break contentious decisions down to first principles and see whether they still hold.

7

Management bugs: process gets tickets too

During eBay's rapid growth, the CEO put a suggestion box in the office and answered the notes at all-hands. Good idea, but a physical box excludes remote staff, not everyone attends the meeting, and issues need tracking. So put "management bugs" in your ticket tracker: anyone raises one, anyone comments, a named person owns the flow, and resolutions are summarised and broadcast. It's often psychologically safer than walking into an executive's office.

8

Three tools for common problems

Why is this code like this? Architecture Decision Records

Capture each significant design decision when you make it, ideally checked into the codebase and proposed via pull request. Even when people leave, the context stays.

Status

Is the team improving? Health checks

Spotify's squad health check: a workshop where the team discusses perspectives, each with a "crappy" and an "awesome" example, and votes. Each becomes a traffic light. Repeat quarterly and watch the trend. Vote for your team:

Who's responsible? Directly Responsible Individuals

Things fall through gaps when nobody owns them, like a service in production with no monitoring. Apple's DRI is the person the buck stops with. They needn't do the work, but they champion it. It's a stripped-back RACI: simpler, and less likely to be forgotten.
