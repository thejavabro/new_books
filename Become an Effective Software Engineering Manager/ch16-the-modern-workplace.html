Look busy, the board is here

Your CEO wants everyone at their desks "hustling" for a board tour, and tells Paulo he can't leave for the school run. The board turns out to be five near-identical men in their fifties. One boasts of 18-hour days, and of missing his son's birth to raise a funding round. Blank faces. As a manager in this industry, you need to understand its pressing issues so you can build a strong, diverse team, fight prejudice and pay imbalance, and make your company better. The common thread: everyone deserves an equal and fair chance to thrive at work.

2

Diversity gets the invite, inclusion gets a seat

Diversity is who makes up your workforce. Inclusion is whether the environment lets everyone feel psychologically safe and contribute. Without inclusion, diversity is just a numbers game.

The dinner party

The numbers (figures as reported in the book, around 2017–2019)

Share of female staff at large tech companies, against a US population that was 51% female:

In AI research, women were about 15% of staff at Facebook and 10% at Google; Black workers 2.5% at Google and 4% at Facebook and Microsoft. Race gets even less attention than gender ("colourless diversity").

Why it matters: upside

McKinsey (2015, 366 companies): top-quartile ethnic diversity in management was 35% more likely to beat industry-mean returns; top-quartile gender diversity, 15%. Diverse R&D teams foster radical innovation. And humanity is diverse; settling for less is prejudice.

Why it matters: downside

Unrepresentative training data has produced webcams that couldn't see Black faces and photo apps that labelled people offensively. Homogeneity sustains bad cultures, power asymmetries, buried harassment, unfair pay and tokenism.

5

The shift toward remote working

At the time of writing: Mozilla had 46% of 1,000 staff outside offices, HashiCorp 85–90% of 800+, Automattic 100% of 1,150+. Most companies will become hybrids. Remote work widens hiring and frees people from commutes, but demands more writing, a rethink of interruptibility, and clear goals.

Organisational inertia?

Put your reputation on the line: pitch an experiment where anyone who wants works one day a week at home, ideally the same day for everyone. You own the output. Retrospect after a few months. Not supporting remote work increasingly means losing candidates.

Written and asynchronous by default

Work in the open (share tickets and PRs in the team channel), document your thinking in ticket comments, and write up decisions so everyone has equal access. When time zones barely overlap, give people ownership of unblocking themselves and more than one thing to work on.

Time-zone overlap

Trust: you haven't heard from Alice in six hours

Values and goals replace informal corridor guidance. Run a half-day values workshop asking, for each value: what it means to us, what it looks like in action, how it could be misread, how we'll evaluate it, and how it changes how we interact. Then give each iteration a clear, measurable goal (ship feature X; ingest 50% faster), perhaps as OKRs.

6

Face time still matters

Video for conflict and open-ended discussion, and for remote 1:1s. Once you have any regular remote staff, treat every meeting as having remote participants. A spotter watches for remote people wanting to speak. And meet in person a few times a year: expensive, but it builds trust instantly.

7

Protect your team from hustle culture

Growth-obsessed tech culture idolises 10x everything and slogans like "Don't stop when you're tired, stop when you're done." Inexperienced people absorb it and burn out. You can be the counterbalance: sensible hours, no reliance on out-of-hours messages, celebrate successes and useful failures, and say no to the unimportant. Increase the effectiveness of hours, not the number.

Walk the walk

Your actions set the norm. What does each signal to the team?

Culture is you

Culture isn't ping-pong tables. It's the effect you, your team and your company have on people, and the psychological safety they work in. Many new managers act like someone else to seem authoritative. Don't. Be yourself, follow good practice, and fix the weaknesses you discover along the way.
