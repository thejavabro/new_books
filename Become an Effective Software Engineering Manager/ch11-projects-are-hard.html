Projects Are Hard

Projects go wrong in familiar ways. Knowing the shapes of those failures, and the three levers you can pull, keeps a team out of trouble.

1

The Eye of Sauron

The launch is tomorrow. The team did everything right: met every sprint, involved stakeholders from the start, shipped almost daily. Then intermittent network failures, baffling log errors and data corruption appear. Marketing says the press release is out, "can't they just work harder?" The product owner asks how an "easy feature" got so hard. Your jaw tightens. You count to ten. "Right, let's look at the logs again."

High stakes, whether an announced headline feature or infrastructure on fire, bring the gaze of the whole business, like the Eye of Sauron in The Lord of the Rings. Handle it well and it's career-defining; fumble it and the next big project goes elsewhere. Tick the warning signs you see.

Under the gaze: six habits

Crunch time, the deadline is next week and it's looking 50-50. Apply these every day.

3

When the gaze moves on: a fallow period

The launch lands, the sales bell rings. Now protect morale. Festival fields are left fallow for a year to recover; teams need the same. Crunch isn't sustainable: teams always under the Eye burn out and leave. In a larger company, stagger high-pressure periods so teams take turns, and fight for recovery space if the business doesn't give it.

4

Victims of your own success

Growing companies feel slower: decisions, features, merges, deploys. Brooks's law (The Mythical Man-Month, 1975): adding people to a late software project makes it later. Knowledge work can't be chopped into independent pieces; it needs communication. "You're three times bigger, why aren't you three times faster?" It isn't that people got lazy: productivity per head falls. That's exactly the gap nimble startups exploit.

possible communication channels, n(n−1)/2

Success also makes business as usual harder: higher SLAs, security, monitoring, on-call, scaling. Yesterday's feature is today's technical debt. Your job: shield the team from arguments about speed, keep them about productivity rather than people, and encourage three behaviours.

Expose hidden complexity

"Just add a login integration" really means session expiry, two-factor, password and username changes… Build empathy for what quality takes.

Always show progress

Team newsletters, sprint demos open to the company, input from your network, so nobody assumes you're slow.

Be pragmatic

Ship user impact fast. Campsite rule. Reserve time for tech debt (try 20%). Delete features that didn't land or that nobody uses.

5

Scope, resources and time

Sales wants it now, product wants it all, engineering wants to rewrite it properly. All valid. Quality isn't negotiable, so you have three levers: scope (what you deliver), resources (how many engineers) and time (how long). Play with a project.

Will it make the deadline?

Scope: label each item (MoSCoW)

Model: one item can only use as many engineers as can work on it in parallel; every extra person adds coordination overhead. Illustrative.

ScopeHelp break work into a prioritised backlog. Label must, should, could, won't. In a pinch, cut everything that isn't a must, and revisit the musts as you go.
ResourcesConsider task parallelism, code vicinity (four spanners on one nut) and technical difficulty. Moving people is your weakest lever; borrow only for parallel pieces.
TimeYou can't bend it. Investigate what slipping actually means; even big keynotes announce "coming in a few months". If the date is immovable, pull the other levers.

Make your lever choices visible. Debate then centres on trade-offs rather than how hard people work, and other teams can see when to lend a hand.
