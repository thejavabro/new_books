Estimating in Ideal Days

An ideal day is how long something takes with no meetings, email or interruptions. It's far easier to estimate than calendar time, and it works as another measure of size.

1

Sixty minutes that take three hours

An American football game has four 15-minute quarters: 60 minutes of ideal time. On the wall clock it takes about three hours of elapsed time because of timeouts, injuries and commercials. Watch both clocks.

Nobody is shocked when a game slated to end at 4:00 ends at 4:15, an 8% overrun. So why are we shocked when a 12-month project takes 13? Ideal time ("60 minutes") is easy to estimate. Elapsed time is what's hard.

3

What an ideal day assumes

Estimating in ideal days, you assume three things. Real life breaks all three, and that's fine: velocity will capture the gap.

4

"Five days" is heard as a date

A manager asks "How long?" A developer says "five days" meaning five days if that's all I do. The manager marks a red X five days out. Switch views.

5

Ideal days as a measure of size

If you ignore organizational overhead, an ideal day is a size: one screen is one ideal day whether you work at a lean startup or a huge bureaucracy. What changes is how many calendar days it takes to deliver it. Compare two companies doing the same 30 ideal days of stories.

6

One estimate per story, not one per role

Some teams write separate numbers for each role on a card. Usually, add them into a single estimate instead. Per-role numbers push people toward "my part" thinking and force you to track velocity and remaining work per role.

The exception: when skills truly can't be shared, for example one team building identical Mac, Windows and handheld versions. Even then, know the bookkeeping cost.
