Prioritizing Desirability

When money is hard to model, use desirability. The Kano model sorts features into must-haves, linear features and delighters. Relative weighting turns benefit, penalty and cost into one priority number.

1

Three kinds of features

Think of a hotel. The bed and a clean bathroom are must-haves: missing, you're furious; present, you barely notice. A bigger room or a better gym is linear: more is better. A TV built into the treadmill is a delighter: you didn't know you wanted it, and its absence costs nothing. Slide how much of each is built.

delighterlinearmust-have

Plan a release like this: all must-haves (not necessarily first, and a basic version may be enough: one-level undo already buys a lot), then as many linear features as possible, then a few delighters if time allows.
2

Features drift down over time

Today's delighter becomes tomorrow's expectation. In-room wireless internet went from wow, to a selling point, to something you'd complain about not having.

3

Ask two questions

Survey 20 to 30 users. For each feature ask how they'd feel if it were present (functional) and if it were absent (dysfunctional). The answer pair maps to one category. Try it for "graph a swimmer's times over the season".

4

Reading the survey results

Aggregate everyone's answers and look at the distribution. The tallest bar names the category. Two tall bars mean different user groups want different things: split the results by company size, role or industry.

5

Relative weighting

An expert alternative, led by the product owner. Rate each feature 1 to 9 on benefit if built and penalty if not built. Value % ÷ cost % = priority. Higher is better. Adjust and watch the ranking.

Don't drop the penalty column. A compliance feature can have a benefit of 1 and a penalty of 9 (the CEO might go to prison). Benefit alone would bury it.
