Monitoring the Iteration Plan

Inside an iteration, two simple tools show where you stand: the task board and the iteration burndown of hours remaining. Track what is left, not what was spent, and never track individual velocity.

1

The task board

One row per story. Task cards move left to right. People sign up for a card only when they're ready to start it, so most of the To Do column stays unclaimed until late in the iteration. Click a card to move it along, tick Tests ready when the acceptance tests are written, or simulate a day.

2

The iteration burndown

Every morning, add up the hours on the board and plot one point. It works well for iterations of two weeks or more. With one-week iterations the iteration is over before a trend shows.

3

Bugs on the task board

Many teams start agile with a pile of legacy bugs. The product owner decides how much of the iteration goes to bugs ("fix ten high bugs"). The developers write and estimate a card for each, and they're worked like any other card. When an urgent new bug arrives, estimate it and remove the same amount of work from To Do.

4

Don't track effort spent

A boat that sailed eight hours isn't necessarily eight hours closer to port. What matters is the distance left. Comparing actual hours to estimates can also cause evaluation apprehension: nervous estimators switch from thinking to fight-or-flight, and estimates get worse. And some variability never goes away, like your commute.

5

Never track individual velocity

Day 8. Your story is nearly done. Sam is stuck on a story the release needs, and you know the fix. What do you do?

Pick an option.
You shouldn't even be able to compute individual velocity. Most stories need several people: a designer, a programmer, a database engineer, a tester. If one person can finish most stories alone, write them at a higher level.
