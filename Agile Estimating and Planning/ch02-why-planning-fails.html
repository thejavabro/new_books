Why Planning Fails

Most projects overrun, and most features in shipped products are rarely used. Traditional plans fail for a handful of predictable, fixable reasons. Each one gets a small simulation here.

1

Planning by activity, not feature

A traditional plan lists activities: design the database, code the middle tier, test. Customers get no value from finished activities. They get value from features. Same project, two progress reports:

A second trap: when we review an activity plan we hunt for forgotten activities, not missing features.

2

Activities don't finish early

Parkinson's Law: work expands to fill the time available. If the chart says five days, the work takes at least five, through gold-plating, side research, or fear of looking like you padded the estimate. Run 12 five-day tasks whose true effort varies.

3

Lateness is passed down the schedule

Testing can start once the UI and the middle tier are coded, and the middle tier waits for the database tables. For testing to start early, everything must go well. For it to start late, one thing going wrong is enough. Roll the dice.

4

Activities aren't independent

Coin flips are independent: a bad flip doesn't make the next one worse. Software tasks are not. If the first of five similar screens took 50% longer, the others probably will too. "I'll make it up on the rest" rarely happens.

5

6

Features aren't built by priority

If a plan assumes everything gets done, work is ordered for the team's convenience. When time runs short, whatever is left gets dropped, valuable or not. Drag the deadline earlier.

7

Ignoring uncertainty, and estimates as commitments

Every estimate carries a probability. "A new word processor by Friday" is 0% likely. "In ten years" is close to 100%. Treating an estimate as a promise ignores that. Pick a date and read off its chance.

The cure for uncertainty is to iterate: show working software every few weeks to learn what to build, and add missed tasks and fix estimates as you go. Express dates as ranges ("June to August") early on.

Recap on one page

Activity-based plansTrack activities, not features. Activities rarely finish early, lateness flows downstream, and tasks are not independent.
MultitaskingProductivity drops after two concurrent tasks, and work stays in process longer.
No priority orderLate in the project, features get dropped regardless of value.
Ignored uncertaintyAbout what to build and how. Single-date schedules hide it.
Estimates ≠ commitmentsAn estimate is a probability. A commitment is a date chosen after weighing risk.
