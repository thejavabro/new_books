Monitoring the Release Plan

Tracking a release is like navigating by dead reckoning. The team's velocity is the speedometer, but scope changes and re-estimates push the ship off course. Count only finished work, then show it with a release burndown, a burndown bar chart or a parking lot chart.

1

Forces on the boat

A boat's speedometer says 8 knots, but wind (leeway) and current (drift) change where it actually goes. On a project, velocity is the speedometer. Changed requirements and revised estimates are the wind and the current.

2

Velocity is all or nothing

Count a story's points only if it is complete at the end of the iteration: well written, refactored, checked in, meeting standards and passing all its tests. Click the stories to change their state.

Partial credit is tempting, but "coded, not tested" and "half coded, half tested" can't be compared. We're good at recognising not started and done; stick to those two.

3

The release burndown

Plot the work remaining at the start of each iteration. The line shows net progress: work completed minus work added. Lay a straight edge from the start through today to see where it hits zero. Play iterations yourself.

4

The burndown bar chart

One line mixes up two things: how fast the team goes and how much the scope moves. The bar version separates them. The top of each bar is driven by the team, the bottom by the product owner. Work added pushes the bottom below zero.

Work completedtop moves down
Re-estimatetop moves up or down
Work addedbottom moves down
Work removedbottom moves up

Your turn: what happens to the next bar?

Start new teams on the simpler line chart. And if people start arguing whether a change belongs on the top or the bottom, drop the bar chart and go back to the line.
5

The parking lot chart

One box per theme: its stories, its points, and how much is done. A whole release fits on a page. Colour shows status. Drag through the release.
