Estimating Size with Story Points

A story point is a relative unit of size. The number itself means nothing. What matters is that a 10 is twice a 5. Pair points with velocity and duration falls out on its own.

1

Ordering by relative size

At an unfamiliar restaurant you order a "large" soda without knowing the ounces. You only know it's bigger than a medium and smaller than an extra-large, and that "large" has been about right before. Estimating work can work the same way.

Click a cup. You never learn the ounces, but you always know the order.

2

Try it: dog points

Let one dog point stand for height at the shoulder. The Labrador is anchored at 5. Give the other breeds points by comparing to the Labrador, then reveal one reasonable answer.

Poodle and Terrier are vague: there are toy and standard poodles, tiny and tall terriers. With a vague story, make an assumption, take a guess, move on.

3

What goes into a point

There is no formula. A point estimate blends effort, complexity and risk into one number for the whole story. Start by calling a medium story a 5 (or the smallest a 1), then size everything else against it.

As a buyer, I can save my cart

The number is a team's gut feel for "how big compared with the reference story", not a formula. The sliders just show the ingredients nudging it.

4

Velocity

Velocity is the sum of points for stories finished in an iteration. If we finished 10 points last time, our best guess is 10 this time, whether that's two 5s or five 2s. Click stories to finish them.

5

Size first, duration derived

Estimate the size of everything. Divide by velocity for the number of iterations. Map that onto a calendar for the schedule. We don't estimate duration directly, we compute it.

6

Velocity corrects estimation errors

You're asked to paint a house from a floor plan with no dimensions. Small bedrooms get 5 points, the master (twice as big) gets 10. If the rooms turn out twice as long and wide, your estimates stay right relative to each other. Only your speed through them changes.

Same idea on a project: 200 points planned at 25 per iteration = 8 iterations. After a couple of iterations the team sees it really does 20. Without re-estimating a single story, the plan corrects itself to 10.
