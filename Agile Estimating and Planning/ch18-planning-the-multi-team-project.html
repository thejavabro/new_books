Planning the Multi-Team Project

Big agile projects use a team of teams, not one 100-person team. Coordinating them takes up to four extra techniques. Add them in order, and only as many as you need: a common estimating baseline, detail sooner, lookahead planning and feeding buffers.

1. A common basis for estimates

All teams should use the same unit (story points or ideal days), and a point must mean the same thing everywhere. Otherwise one team's 20 is another team's 100 and nobody can add up the release.

3

2. Add detail to stories sooner

A single team can go from a vague story to working software within one iteration. Several teams need a little more thought beforehand so they can coordinate. The most useful thing to prepare is the product owner's conditions of satisfaction: the high-level acceptance tests for each likely next story.

Analysts and designers don't work a whole iteration ahead. Their main job stays the current iteration; they just plan a few tasks that prepare the stories most likely to come next.

4

3. Rolling lookahead planning

Two teams build the swimming website. Each has a velocity of 20, there are 110 points, so three iterations should do. But 30 points are an API to the national records database, and 40 more (national records, meet results, personal records) need that API.

4. Feeding buffers

If your team needs something from mine at 8:00 tomorrow, I shouldn't plan to finish it at 7:59. A feeding buffer protects the start of another team's work. To add one, plan the delivering team at a lower velocity for that iteration. Then see what happens when the API turns out bigger than expected.

Team 1 isn't meant to relax in iteration 2. They're expected to finish the API, use only part of the buffer, and start national records early.

Sizing it

Few stories are involved, so the √Σ formula from Chapter 17 isn't reliable here. Default to 50% of the stories creating the dependency and adjust by judgment. A feeding buffer longer than an iteration means the hand-off is too big: deliver incrementally instead, with the receiving team acting as the delivering team's customer.
7

Isn't this a lot of work?

Yes, but so is a large multi-team project. A single team needs none of it. Three or four teams of about seven who talk often probably don't either. When you must commit to a date months ahead and teams depend on each other, a few extra hours of planning buy a more trustworthy date.
