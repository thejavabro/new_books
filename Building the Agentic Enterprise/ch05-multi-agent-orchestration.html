Multi-Agent Orchestration

Once you have several agents, someone has to decide who does what, settle disagreements and stop the whole thing from spinning out of control.

1

From one pioneer to an enterprise capability

Pioneer advantage is local. One expert holds a remarkable amount of coordination in their head: which prompt works, which output to distrust, what to check. A team cannot run on that instinct, and an enterprise certainly cannot. The knowledge has to move into shared harnesses, explicit roles and governed coordination. The author compares it to the microservices era: dozens of independent actors suddenly needed discovery, security and routing. Agents bring the same problem back.

Buying more AI seats does not make an AI-native enterprise. The consumption layer gives employees governed access; the platform layer is where teams build capabilities others can reuse.

2

Orchestration or choreography?

Start from the business process, not the agent boxes. Three migration-style choices frame it: lift and shift a working workflow onto agents, migrate and modernize by automating today's process then improving it, or modernize and migrate by reimagining the process first. Then decide where control lives.

Choreography still fits at system boundaries where loose coupling matters. And every orchestration decision is also a state decision: somebody has to hold the workflow's state.

3

Hierarchies and the supervision principle

"The agents reached consensus through message passing" is not an answer a risk manager accepts. Every worker should operate beneath an entity accountable for the outcome, and every layer needs an explicit escalation path. Keep hierarchies shallow: each extra non-deterministic layer adds latency and context movement.

4

Routing, delegation contracts and reuse

Three routing styles: by intent (what the request means), by capability (what each agent publishes it can do) and by load (round-robin, least-busy, quota-aware, priority). MREP's director routes by intent, is forbidden from answering itself, and sends anything ambiguous like "tell me what to buy" to a human. Quota-aware routing matters in earnings season, when filing APIs are throttled exactly when demand peaks.

Good routing still fails if the handoff is thin. Build a delegation and see what the worker is likely to do.

Research on intelligent delegation formalizes the same ideas: contract-first delegation, accountability that stays with the delegator, and cryptographic capability tokens that limit scope, duration and depth. Once a workflow works, A2A lets the agent itself become a reusable capability for other teams.

5

Conflicts, resolution and the three Cs

Four conflict classes recur: resource contention (two agents writing one record), conflicting conclusions (two analyses disagree), order priority (a step runs before its input is ready) and stale context (acting on yesterday's data). Three coordination mechanisms trade speed for trust.

Pick the resolution strategy

The three Cs of a handoffThe author's habit turned principle: ground the work in a vision and roadmap document, then at every checkpoint ask whether each artifact is Coherent with the vision, Consistent in structure and principles, and Complete enough for the next participant to act without guessing. He encodes them in supervisor instructions, eval criteria and handoff schemas. It works for human-plus-agent teams too.
6

Containing runaway autonomy

Agent A delegates to B, B to C, and C is three steps removed from the original intent, taking actions nobody approved. Scaling research found that adding agents helps parallel tasks but hurts sequential ones, and token use can differ by more than 200% between patterns for the same task. Five design-time mechanisms work together: scope constraints, delegation depth limits, cognitive limits (squad size), budget caps and approval gates. The platform as orchestration and governance layer

Leaders keep asking how one successful agent becomes sustainable enterprise capability. The book's answer is a platform layer: an agent registry so agents are discoverable, an agent gateway that mediates who may call what, anomaly detection that flags tool-call sequences, delegation depth or timing outside an agent's learned baseline, and sandboxes for risky tool calls. Internally, agents are reused through the registry, the employee surface and A2A; externally through managed APIs or A2A across company boundaries.

The ownership questionWho holds the mandate, the budget and the accountability for the platform? Treated as another delivery project, it becomes a silo wearing a platform badge. Settle ownership before staffing the build.

In ADK, the same four building blocks appear whether you use the sub-agent tree or the newer graph model (which adds conditional routing, partial resume and visualization). Illustrative sketch:

research = ParallelAgent(name="gather",

Anti-patterns and a lesson from MREP

MREP: brittle routingAfter launch to validation, just under 30% of client requests were landing in human escalation because intent rules were too rigid; clients phrased things nobody anticipated. The fix was an intent-clarification step: when a request is ambiguous, the director asks a short question (formal recommendation, or general view?) before routing.

Takeaway: somebody has to be in charge, and that somebody is a person and a team. Start with one orchestrator, delegate only when domains diverge, gate every handoff on the three Cs, and have orchestration observability (delegation traces, handoff metrics, loop detection) before Day 1.
