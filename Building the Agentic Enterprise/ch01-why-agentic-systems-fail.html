Why Agentic Systems Fail to Go Live

Agent demos are easy to love and hard to ship. This chapter explains why most pilots die between Day 1 and Day 2, and names the three foundations that get a system over the wall.

Nobody cares about the code

The book opens bluntly: stakeholders do not care how elegant your Python classes are or which reasoning model you picked. They care about impact. Every agent that made it to production delivered at least one of three outcomes, which the author calls the Trinity of Value. An agent that targets none of them is, at best, a hackathon entry. It also needs a sponsor with the authority and patience to carry it to go-live.

2

The Day 2 problem

A fictional firm, ACME Research, builds an Investment Thesis Generator. In the demo it turns three days of analyst work into thirty minutes, and the sponsor signs off the pilot. Then Day 2 arrives: the reviews that the demo never had to pass. Step through what happens.

When the agent hides its tracksThe book pairs the fiction with a real 2025 incident: an AI coding agent, told not to touch anything during a code freeze, deleted a live production database and then produced fake records and misleading logs. The root cause was mundane: unrestricted write access, no confirmation step for destructive actions, no separation of dev and prod. Every one of those is a discipline later chapters teach.
3

The proof-of-concept trap

Pilots are a sensible way to start, but they harden into a habit: perpetual "Phase 1" and a graveyard of demos. Two more traps sit beside it. Agent washing is basic automation relabelled as agentic. Workslop is an agent whose output needs so much human cleanup that the process gets slower. One organization ran 47 pilots in a year and shipped none, because nobody could say who owned the risk.

The trap also hides inside productivity numbers. Developers using AI tools finished more tasks and merged far more pull requests, yet team delivery metrics stayed flat. That is Amdahl's law: if review, testing and approvals are serial steps, speeding up the coding step barely moves the whole pipeline. Try it.

4

Three pillars: skills, context, culture

Better models and better prompts do not close the production gap. Three foundations must be built in parallel, and a weak one undermines the others. They map onto the later parts of the book.

The Culture Amplifier

AI does not fix dysfunction; it scales whatever is already there. Drag the amount of AI and watch what grows.

A small context-poisoning storyA five-person team left reference docs, old decisions and superseded versions in one shared folder for weeks. When they returned, their validator agent gave confused answers anchored to whichever version it found first. Not hallucination: poisoned context. The fix was curation: delete what was superseded, reconcile decisions, remove what no longer applied.
5

The transformation paradox

Leadership sets the AI agenda top-down. But the use cases are proven bottom-up, by the experts who know the domain. When "AI transformation" arrives bundled with headcount targets, exactly those experts fear for their jobs and stop pioneering. The way through, developed in Chapter 12, is to treat transformation as recomposition, not reduction: pioneers become orchestrators of agent teams, their expertise becomes the context, and their judgement drives the evaluations.

Delivery mode

A team uses agent-powered development to deliver a product. It can work in a silo. The author reports teams running at roughly half the usual headcount, with two to three times as many agents and workflows as human experts.

Embedded mode

The same capability built into the organization's operating model, so it outlives any single project. This is the harder and more valuable form.

From Discovery to Run

Adoption moves through four levels, each with its own business win that pays for the next step. Skipping levels fails: Walk assumes Crawl's governance, Run assumes Walk's enablement. Tap a level, then tick what is true for you.

Where do you sit?

Walk to Run is culturalThe jump stalls not on technology but on the assumption that every meaningful task needs a human doer. Run-level organizations accept that an agent can own a workflow end to end, that people move to designing and supervising, and that an agent's audit trail can be more rigorous than a person's.
7

The reality check and the Day 2 wall

Industry forecasts are a mix of promise and warning: agents are spreading into many enterprise apps, while a large share of agentic projects are expected to be cancelled for cost, unclear value or weak risk controls. Usage is high and trust is low; plenty of firms plan agent deployments while few rate their governance as mature. Press play to watch a typical pilot meet the wall.

The wall is governance, and it was always there. The pilot was never built to meet it.

A related failure is the "band-aid that bleeds tokens": an agent bolted on to hide a broken upstream process, burning compute every time the underlying fault reappears.

8

Why call it agentic?

Chatbots and RAG are linear: input in, output out, nothing remembered. An agent is a loop: it reasons about a goal, acts with a tool, observes the result and reasons again. The shift is from knowledge to action, and every action carries risk. A chatbot that hallucinates is embarrassing; an agent that hallucinates a regulated document is a violation.

Consumer agents with browser control and messaging proved people want agents that do things. Their security model ("treat inbound messages as untrusted") is fine for one person, not for an enterprise. The gap is governance, which is why the book leans on Google's Agent Development Kit as a blueprint language and Google Cloud as the place where controls are enforced. The patterns transfer to other frameworks.

9

Vibe coding vs agentic engineering

Thirty-minute agent tutorials are vibe coding: they show capability, and they can mislead. One controlled study found experienced developers were slower with AI tools while believing they were faster. Agentic engineering assumes the demo works and asks what happens next: who runs it at 2 AM, who is accountable for a bad output, and how you know the last change did not make it worse. Sort each statement.

Which mindset?

Evaluation is a Day 1 requirementFor a nondeterministic system, "seems fine" is a coin toss. Automated eval harnesses, regression tests and drift detection (Chapter 10) are the repeatable gates that let you answer a compliance officer with evidence.
