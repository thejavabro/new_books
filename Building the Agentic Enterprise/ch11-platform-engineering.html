The factory problem

The author has built the same platform three times in three organizations, and the story always starts the same way. One team ships an agent that works. A second team copies the idea, then a tenth. Each picks its own vector database, framework and pipeline. Then someone asks: "How many agents do we have in production, and what can they touch?" Nobody knows. In MREP's version, a 2 a.m. incident revealed agents that could not be inventoried, attributed or switched off, and the next meeting was about shutting them all down.

2

Day 2 ownership: from BUILD to OPERATE

The hardest questions are not technical. For every agent someone must answer: who curates its prompts and context, who reviews its evaluations, who owns its cost, who switches it off? The book draws two triangles with the same three layers: cloud foundations, the agent platform, and the reimagined business workflow on top. The BUILD triangle is well staffed by delivery specialists. The OPERATE triangle's top is often empty: who on the receiving side owns the reimagined workflow? Tap the layers.

3

CI/CD for software that answers differently each time

Traditional CI/CD assumes a build that passes once will pass again. Agents do not. The reference pipeline: deterministic unit tests, then trace evaluation against a golden set, then a decision gate that blocks the build below threshold, then infrastructure deployment. It fails closed: if the evaluation service is down, nothing ships. Three differences recur: prompts are code (version them), flakiness needs repeated runs, and full golden-set runs cost money, so run them nightly or on the risky paths. Run the golden set several times.

4

Releasing: shadow, canary, and what to split

Shadow runs the new version on live traffic but shows users only the old one; both answers are logged and compared asynchronously (stub any side-effecting tools, and pay for the extra inference). Canary exposes a small slice of users and promotes only if tool-error rate, loop counts and eval health hold. The book recommends at least 24 hours of shadow for major model upgrades. Then there is the question of what you release: one agent's model, one agent's revision, or the whole flow.

Hybrid answer: ship loosely coupled agents independently; release tightly coupled ones as a versioned whole-flow manifest. A model swap should clear both its own evals and an end-to-end handoff evaluation.

5

Make the governed path the easy path

A sponsor hears "standardize" and reads "cost centre". What the slide rarely shows is what bypassing costs: duplicated inference spend the shared routing could optimize, no shared context infrastructure, no shared audit surface until the regulator asks. The paved road is harness engineering at fleet scale: identity, registry, gateway, evaluation, context and observability provided once. Its one rule: if it takes more effort than a rogue deployment, teams take the shortcut.

The book's "sovereign DevEx" idea, which it calls "Lovable for Enterprise": self-service rapid prototyping where every deployment lands in a project that already has quotas, allowed APIs, billing labels, IAM, perimeter, secrets, guardrails, PII redaction and audit logging. Builders get speed; the organization gets governance by default. An AI Center of Excellence, the successor to the cloud CoE, owns that platform mandate.

The team multiplier and the cognitive ceiling

Equip the humans before deploying the agents that depend on them, or the agents inherit the friction. Research with BCG reported in HBR found productivity rising from one to two to three AI tools, then falling beyond three, with more major errors, more decision fatigue and more intent to quit. The book's operating unit respects that boundary: give people the right three tools and let orchestration handle the rest.

8

Seven platform anti-patterns

Centralized or federated?Default: a centralized platform with self-service APIs and paved roads; teams consume it rather than building their own retrieval, eval and deploy stacks. Federate only truly isolated use cases with no shared data or compliance needs. Centralized without self-service makes the platform team the bottleneck and breeds shadow IT; federated without standards gives you ten vector databases, security holes and impossible audits.
