1

Multiply the experts, don't replace them

Chapter 1 opened with a tension. Leadership says "transform or be disrupted" and the pioneers who could prove the use cases are the same people whose jobs appear on the efficiency slide. This chapter is where the book resolves it. The frame shift is from SaaS ("here is a tool, you do the work") to Service-as-Software: the system delivers the outcome itself. For MREP that means "investment intelligence", not a research dashboard. The resolution the author calls recomposition: experienced subject-matter experts move from doing the work to orchestrating agents that do it.

BCG research the book cites attributes roughly 10% of AI transformation success to algorithms, 20% to technology and data, and 70% to people and process. The operating model is the 70%.

2

The AI Squad: one human, three to five agents

The book's unit of hybrid work is one human SME who owns a small squad of agents built on a startup-team shape: a Product Owner agent (the why), a Delivery Manager agent (validation, the Three Cs), and a Coder/Executor agent (the how). A bigger squad adds depth inside those roles rather than more voices at the decision table. Two conditions make it work. First, parity of access: if a human can see a system, the agent must be able to reason over it, or it fails the "human sniff test" on day one. Second, the framing: agents are capacity with agency, not "virtual colleagues". Research the book cites found that treating agents as employees reduces individual accountability and review quality.

Why the ceiling is not just "three tools"The BCG study found productivity falling after a third unstructured AI tool. A squad can hold three to five because each agent owns a bounded domain, returns work in a standard shape and escalates along a defined path. The human reviews against a contract instead of juggling raw tools. Past five, the structure stops helping and review turns into rubber-stamping at machine speed.

Humans govern, agents execute

The book's org chart has three levels: Governors (humans: strategy, governance, the risk budget), Orchestrators (agents acting as team leads) and Workers (specialist agents). The accountability rule is the one threaded through since Chapter 7: an agent can be Responsible for doing the work, but a human is always Accountable. Pick an MREP activity.

From #AIReady to #AIFirst

The Maturity Pyramid has four stages: Discovery, Crawl, Walk, Run. The line that matters is between Crawl and Walk. Below it is the playground: copilots, personal productivity, and value that leaks away downstream. Above it is production, where agents sit inside delivery pipelines and governance is first-class. A survey the book cites found 11% of organizations with agents in production and 38% still piloting. Tap a stage.

Beyond AI-First sits AI-Native: systems talking to systems through well-orchestrated agents. The book's Triangle of Agentic Maturity puts Context Strategy, Platform Engineering and the Operating Model at the base, with Value Acceleration at the apex. It also notes that the same stage looks different at individual, team and enterprise scale. One public data point: at one software company only 4 of 13,000 staff used a newly acquired AI browser at first, and eight months later all of them did. The climb is the work.

When the customer is an agent

Business-to-agent (B2A) commerce is the longer horizon: agents that discover offers, negotiate and pay. The rails are already being built. AP2 uses signed "mandates" that prove a human authorized a purchase within limits. UCP standardizes discovery, cart and checkout across merchants. Next to MCP for tools and A2A for agent-to-agent delegation, they form a four-protocol stack. If a buying agent cannot machine-read your terms, to that agent they do not exist. Designing the operating unit

MREP's conservative version has three humans: the Research Director, the Platform Lead and the Client Success Lead. Each supervises three agent domains, nine in total, and that can grow toward fifteen as each orchestrator matures. The book suggests a time budget for each human: about 40% oversight and review, 30% exceptions, 20% improvement and 10% stakeholders. Its alarm: if exceptions take more than half the time, the agents need retraining or the scope is wrong. Move the exception rate and see how the week rebalances.

These are lagging signals. The Three Cs at each handoff are the leading ones: when coherence, consistency or completeness slip, escalations and overrides follow within days. Budget about ten hours of hands-on practice per agent domain before an orchestrator's intuition can be trusted, and budget it again after model changes.

7

How roles recompose

The developer row is already splitting in two. Some engineers become code experts who read, debug and verify what the agent wrote. Others become decomposers who cut problems into agent-sized tasks and watch cost and latency. Middle managers are the natural home for the orchestrator role. Bypassing them is one of the anti-patterns below. A study the book cites found the strongest deployments started bottom-up with power users and domain managers, paired with executive accountability.

8

Culture as Code

Every agent you use asks you to re-explain the same things: this quarter's priorities, brand voice, constraints and commitments. The book estimates orchestrators lose 30–40% of their time to this. Culture as Code turns organizational purpose into structured, version-controlled context that agents load by default, governed like infrastructure:

  • Versioned, because priorities change quarterly.
  • Scoped by role, so marketing does not see the R&D roadmap.
  • Audited, so you can trace which outputs a strategy change has made stale.

A minimal sketch of the idea (my own illustration):

purpose:
  version: 2026-Q4.2
  mission: "Research clients can act on, explained and auditable"
  priorities: [rates-outlook, emerging-markets-coverage]
  never: [personalised advice without suitability check,
          unsourced figures]
  heuristics:
    - when unsure, flag the gap rather than guess
commitments:
  - to: regulator
    what: "every published thesis has a named human approver"
access:
  research-agents: [purpose, commitments]
  marketing-agents: [purpose.mission, brand]

This is the context layer of the "Lovable for Enterprise" platform from Chapter 11. Every new agent starts out already aligned.

How transformation fails

The six failure modes share a root. Agents get added to the estate the organization already had, and the estate is never fixed. In the author's words, you cannot put agents on an old landing zone "and call it agentic".
