Training of Generative AI

Training a large model is a systems problem: fitting weights, gradients and optimizer states into memory, spreading work across thousands of GPUs, and keeping the run stable for weeks.

1

From raw text to a gradient update

A training step: a DataLoader streams tokenized examples; a collator builds a batch (input IDs, attention mask, labels); the forward pass produces logits; cross-entropy compares them with labels shifted by one; backward computes gradients; the optimizer updates weights. For causal LM, labels equal the inputs shifted left, and padded or prompt positions get the label −100 so they are ignored by the loss.

Position t predicts the label at t, which is input token t+1. "−100" = ignored by the loss.

2

Mixed precision: FP32, FP16, BF16

Low precision halves memory and runs much faster on tensor cores. FP16 has more mantissa bits but only 5 exponent bits, so tiny gradients underflow to zero; it needs loss scaling. BF16 keeps FP32's 8 exponent bits (same range, less precision), so it rarely needs scaling and is the default on modern GPUs. Master weights and optimizer states stay in FP32; reductions, softmax and norms are often computed in FP32 too.

3

Where GPU memory goes

Four consumers: weights, gradients, optimizer states (Adam keeps two per parameter) and activations. With mixed-precision Adam a common rule is about 16 bytes per parameter before activations: 2 (BF16 weights) + 2 (gradients) + 4 (FP32 master) + 8 (Adam m and v). A 7B model therefore needs about 112 GB just for training state. Sharding (ZeRO / FSDP) splits it across GPUs; activation checkpointing trades compute for activation memory.

4

Gradient accumulation, clipping and MFU

Gradient accumulation runs several micro-batches, sums their gradients, then steps once: a large effective batch without the memory. It costs time, not memory; activation checkpointing costs compute to save memory. Gradient clipping rescales the gradient when its norm exceeds a threshold, which stops rare loss spikes from wrecking a run. MFU (model FLOPs utilization) tells you how much of the hardware's peak you are really using.

Effective batch

MFU

MFU = 6·N·(tokens/s) ÷ peak FLOP/s. Peak taken as ~989 TFLOP/s (H100 BF16 dense). 30 to 55% is typical for well-tuned clusters.

5

Data, tensor and pipeline parallelism

Data parallel: every GPU holds the model (or a shard, with ZeRO/FSDP) and sees different data; gradients are averaged with all-reduce. Tensor parallel: each weight matrix is split across GPUs, which need fast links (inside one node). Pipeline parallel: layers are split into stages across GPUs; micro-batches flow through, and idle "bubbles" appear at the start and end. Large runs combine all three ("3-D parallelism"), plus sequence/context parallelism for long inputs and expert parallelism for MoE.

6

Training mixture-of-experts models

The router scores experts with a softmax and sends each token to its top-k. Two problems: expert collapse (a few experts get everything and the rest never learn) and overflow (each expert has a fixed capacity per batch; tokens beyond it are dropped or passed through the residual). A load-balancing auxiliary loss pushes routing toward uniform; capacity factor trades dropped tokens against wasted padding. With expert parallelism, experts live on different GPUs and tokens travel with an all-to-all exchange.

7

Knowledge distillation

A small student learns to imitate a large teacher. Soft distillation matches the teacher's full output distribution (softened with a temperature), using KL divergence, often mixed with the normal hard-label loss; the "dark knowledge" is in which wrong answers the teacher thinks are plausible. Hard (sequence-level) distillation simply fine-tunes on text the teacher generated, which is all you can do through an API. Distillation differs from quantization and pruning: it trains a new, smaller architecture.
