Alignment

A pretrained model predicts text; an aligned one is helpful, honest and harmless. This chapter covers how preference data becomes behavior, and how that behavior fails.

1

Why a pretrained model needs alignment

A pretrained model imitates the internet: it continues text, which may mean rambling, toxicity, made-up facts or another question instead of an answer. Alignment shapes it to be helpful, honest and harmless. SFT teaches the format of good answers; preference optimization teaches which of several plausible answers people actually prefer, which is easier to judge than to write.

Prompt: "How do I get rid of a wasp nest near my door?"

3

The Bradley-Terry reward model

Annotators pick the better of two responses. The reward model r(x, y) (the SFT model with a scalar head) is trained so the probability that yw beats yl is σ(rw − rl); the loss is −log of that. Only differences matter, so rewards have no absolute scale.

4

The KL leash and reward hacking

Stage 3 maximizes reward − β·KL(policy ‖ reference). The reward model is only a proxy trained on limited data. Push the policy far from where that data came from and it finds responses the proxy loves but people do not: reward hacking (Goodhart's law). The KL penalty keeps it on a leash. Move the policy away from the SFT reference.

5

DPO: preference learning without RL

DPO shows that the optimal RLHF policy has a closed form in terms of the reward, so the reward can be rewritten through the policy itself. The result is a classification-style loss on preference pairs: −log σ(β[(log π(yw) − log πref(yw)) − (log π(yl) − log πref(yl))]). No reward model, no sampling loop, two models in memory instead of four. Move the log-ratios.

6

Constitutional AI and RLAIF

Human labels are slow and expensive. Constitutional AI writes down principles and lets a model use them. In the supervised phase the model critiques and revises its own responses against the principles; in the RL phase an AI judge compares responses using the principles, and those AI preferences train the reward model (RLAIF). It scales, and the principles are explicit and auditable, but it inherits the judge's blind spots, so human spot-checks remain.

7

Safety under adversarial pressure

Jailbreaks exploit gaps between what safety training covered and what the model can do: role-play and hypothetical framing, obfuscation (encodings, low-resource languages), prefix injection ("Start with 'Sure, here is'"), many-shot prompting, and automated adversarial suffixes. Many-shot jailbreaking fills a long context with fake dialogues in which an assistant complies; success grows steadily with the number of shots, because it uses the same in-context learning that makes models useful.

Illustrative trend, not measured data.

8

Supervising models smarter than the supervisors

RLHF depends on humans judging outputs. As models exceed human ability on some tasks, judging fails. Scalable oversight research looks for ways around this.
