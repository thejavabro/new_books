Reasoning Models

Reasoning models spend tokens thinking before they answer. Reinforcement learning on checkable problems taught them to plan, verify and backtrack, and opened a new axis of scaling at inference time.

1

What makes a model a reasoning model

Reasoning means deriving new conclusions from given information through several steps: deductive, inductive, abductive, analogical, causal or commonsense. There are three ways to get it from an LLM: prompting (chain-of-thought, Chapter 3), training the model to deliberate (o1/o3, DeepSeek-R1 and successors), or neuro-symbolic pipelines that translate a problem into a formal language for a solver. Trained reasoning models dominate general use. Their defining trait: they produce thinking tokens before the answer, and spend more of them on harder problems.

Two phases: think, then answer

2

Verifiable rewards, outcome vs process

Humans are poor judges of long proofs, so reasoning RL replaces the learned reward model with a verifiable reward: is the final answer correct, do the tests pass? Typically R = Raccuracy + Rformat. It is cheap, scales to millions of problems and is hard to hack (but not impossible). An outcome reward scores only the end; a process reward model (PRM) scores each step, giving denser credit assignment but costing step-level labels and being itself hackable.

3

GRPO: advantages from a group, no value model

PPO needs a value model as large as the policy to estimate a baseline. GRPO drops it: sample a group of G answers to the same prompt, score each, and use the group's own statistics as the baseline: Ai = (ri − mean(r)) / std(r). Better-than-average answers are pushed up, worse ones down, with a PPO-style clipped update and a KL term. Click samples to mark them right or wrong.

Edge case interviewers loveIf every sample gets the same reward, std = 0 and every advantage is 0: the prompt contributes no learning signal. Too-easy and too-hard prompts are wasted compute, which is why recipes filter or curriculum prompts to a band where the model is sometimes right.
4

Self-verification and the multi-stage recipe

DeepSeek-R1-Zero, trained with pure RLVR from a base model, learned on its own to write longer reasoning, re-check its work and backtrack (the "aha moment"). But its output mixed languages and was hard to read. The full R1 recipe adds stages around the RL.

5

Inference-time compute scaling

A second scaling axis: spend more compute per query. Parallel methods sample many answers and pick (majority vote, best-of-N with a verifier). Sequential methods revise one answer step by step. Search methods (beam search over steps, Monte Carlo tree search) explore with a process reward model. The compute-optimal choice depends on difficulty: easy questions saturate quickly; medium ones gain most; very hard ones may need a bigger model, not more samples.

6

Challenges: error propagation, overthinking, faithfulness

If each step is right with probability p and errors are never caught, an n-step chain is right with probability pⁿ. Self-verification and backtracking break that multiplication. Long chains bring their own problems: overthinking (thousands of tokens on "2 + 3"), underthinking (switching approaches before finishing one), unfaithful chain-of-thought (the written reasoning is not what actually drove the answer) and logical inconsistency across related questions.
