Hello AI Engineering!

When a powerful model is one API call away, the hard part moves: it is no longer training the model but building a reliable, safe, affordable system around it. This chapter maps the field and where you might fit in it.

1

From building the model to building around it

Classical ML engineering was model-centric: collect and label data, engineer features, train, validate, deploy, monitor, retrain, repeat. AI engineering starts from a foundation model that already exists (an API, an open-weight release or a managed platform) and puts the effort into the system around it: prompts, retrieval, tools, guardrails, evaluation, latency and cost.

The one-line answerML engineering asks "how do we build and operate the model?". AI engineering asks "how do we build a reliable system around a powerful model?".
2

Where language models came from

Generative AI sits inside deep learning, inside machine learning, inside AI (a field named at Dartmouth in 1956). Its engine is language modeling: predicting the probability of a word from its context. The field moved through three eras, and the clearest way to see the difference is how much context each one can use to predict the next word. Pick an era.

3

Three architecture families

Every LLM is one of three shapes. The quickest way to tell them apart is the attention mask: which tokens each position is allowed to look at. Rows are the token being computed, columns are tokens it may read. Blue squares are allowed.

Open-weightParameters are downloadable to inspect, fine-tune or self-host under a license (Llama, Mistral, Qwen, Gemma, DeepSeek). "Open-weight" is more precise than "open-source" because training data and code are often not released.
ClosedWeights stay with the provider; you reach the model through an API or product (GPT, Claude, Gemini). Usually strongest hosted capability and reliability, less transparency and control.

5

Bigger is not automatically better

On public leaderboards, scores rise with size, but instruction tuning lifts a model more than a jump in size often does: a well-tuned 13B frequently beats a raw 70B on practical tasks. And the leaderboard is silent on latency, cost, calibration, robustness and safety. Weigh what matters for your product and see which of three made-up candidates wins.
