Data Preparation

Data is the part of the model you can actually control. Where pretraining text comes from, how it is filtered and mixed, and how it is cut into tokens decide much of what the model can do.

Where pretraining data comes from, and how it is mixed

Pretraining corpora draw on web crawls (Common Crawl and filtered derivatives), books, Wikipedia and encyclopedias, code, scientific papers, forums and dialogue, and increasingly synthetic data. The key interview point: the size of a source in the raw pool is not its weight in training. Mixture weights are a modeling decision. GPT-3 is the classic example.

2

A cleaning pipeline for web data

Raw web text is mostly boilerplate, spam, duplicates and pages in languages you did not plan for. A typical pipeline applies cheap filters first and expensive ones later. Step through it; the counter shows how much of a raw crawl might survive (illustrative).

Can filtering hurt?Yes. Aggressive quality classifiers trained on "Wikipedia-like" text drop dialects, informal registers and minority topics. Toxicity filters can remove text about marginalized groups, making the model worse at discussing them. Filter, then measure diversity and downstream quality, not just loss.
3

Deduplication with shingles and MinHash

Duplicates waste compute, cause memorization and leak test data into training; in some web datasets around half the documents are duplicates. Exact dedup hashes whole documents. Near-dedup breaks documents into overlapping word n-grams ("shingles"), estimates their Jaccard similarity with MinHash signatures, and uses locality-sensitive hashing (LSH) to find candidate pairs without comparing everything to everything. 4

Temperature sampling across sources

Each source starts with a natural weight proportional to its token count. Raising weights to the power 1/T and renormalizing reshapes the mix: T = 1 keeps natural proportions, T > 1 flattens toward uniform (small sources sampled more), T → ∞ gives every source equal weight. Multilingual models use this to up-weight low-resource languages. The risk: small sources get repeated many times, which invites memorization.

6

Data scheduling and annealing

Order matters too. Static scheduling keeps a fixed mix. Dynamic scheduling adjusts ratios from validation signals. Curriculum scheduling starts broad and shifts toward cleaner, harder or more specialized data. Many recipes end with annealing: a short final phase, while the learning rate decays, that up-weights the highest-quality data (curated, math, code, instruction-like), which gives a large boost for little compute.

7

Tokenization levels and vocabulary size

Word-level tokens are short sequences but explode the vocabulary and break on new words. Characters never go out of vocabulary but make sequences long. Subwords (BPE, WordPiece, Unigram) balance the two. Vocabulary size is a three-way trade: bigger vocab gives shorter sequences (more text per context, faster), but a larger embedding table and rarer, less-trained tokens.
