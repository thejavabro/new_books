Fine-Tuning

Fine-tuning changes how a model behaves, not what it knows. The skill is knowing when it is the right tool, and doing it cheaply with LoRA without breaking what already worked.

1

Prompt to instruct, RAG to ground, fine-tune to standardize

Diagnose the bottleneck before you train. Fine-tuning is good at making a behavior the default: consistent formats, reliable tool calls, a house style, domain conventions, and removing a long "prompt tax". It is poor at storing facts: knowledge that changes, long-tail facts and citations belong in RAG. And it mostly sharpens abilities the base model partly has; it rarely invents new ones. Sort the requests.

Which tool fixes it?

2

How much of the model do you train?

Fine-tuning varies by how many parameters change. Full fine-tuning updates everything: most freedom, most cost, most risk of regressions. Selective fine-tuning unfreezes a subset (last layers, norms, head). PEFT freezes the backbone and trains small added parameters: cheap, modular (one adapter per task or customer) and easy to roll back. It is the industry default.

3

Where each PEFT method plugs in

All PEFT methods freeze the original weights; they differ in where the new parameters live and whether they add inference cost.

4

LoRA: a low-rank update you can merge

LoRA keeps a weight W frozen and learns ΔW = B·A, where A is r×d and B is d×r with a small rank r. The forward pass becomes h = Wx + (α/r)·BAx. B starts at zero, so training begins exactly at the base model. The bet is that the change needed for a task lives in a low-dimensional subspace. After training, BA can be merged into W, so inference costs nothing extra; or kept separate to hot-swap adapters.

5

QLoRA and the memory bill

QLoRA stores the frozen base in 4-bit NF4 (a data type shaped for normally distributed weights), with double quantization of the scale constants and paged optimizers for memory spikes, and trains BF16 LoRA adapters on top. The LoRA math is unchanged; weights are de-quantized on the fly. Result: fine-tuning a 65–70B model on a single 48–80 GB GPU.

Rough training memory before activations: full FT ≈ 16 B/param (mixed-precision Adam); LoRA ≈ 2 B/param frozen BF16 + small adapter state; QLoRA ≈ 0.55 B/param + adapters.

6

SFT: the objective and the data

SFT uses the same next-token cross-entropy as pretraining, but on (instruction, response) pairs formatted with the model's chat template, and the loss is usually computed only on response tokens. Training on the prompt wastes capacity on text the model will never need to write, and can teach it to imitate user turns. Quality beats quantity: a few thousand clean, diverse examples (LIMA used about 1,000) can go a long way.

7

Catastrophic forgetting and shipping

Fine-tuning moves weights toward your task and away from everything else. Push too hard (high learning rate, many epochs, narrow data) and general skills degrade: catastrophic forgetting. Watch a target metric and a "do-not-break" metric together and pick the checkpoint where both are acceptable.

Serving 100 fine-tuned variants

One base model in GPU memory; small adapters loaded on demand (from CPU or disk), cached by popularity, and applied per request inside the same batch (multi-adapter kernels as in S-LoRA or LoRAX). Merging is better when one adapter serves all traffic.
