Evaluation

If you cannot measure it you cannot ship it. Evaluating open-ended generation is the hardest unsolved part of AI engineering, and interviewers know it.

1

Evaluation has five axes, not one

"Is this model good?" needs several answers: quality (correct, relevant, faithful), robustness (does it survive paraphrases and messy input?), calibration (does its confidence match its accuracy?), efficiency (cost and latency for that quality) and safety. Compare two made-up models.

2

Calibration and expected calibration error

A well-calibrated model that says "80% sure" is right about 80% of the time. ECE groups predictions into confidence bins and averages the gap between accuracy and confidence, weighted by bin size. Pretrained models are often fairly well calibrated; RLHF tends to make them overconfident, because raters reward confident-sounding answers. Push the slider.

3

Perplexity, BLEU, ROUGE and BERTScore

Perplexity is exp(average negative log-likelihood): how "surprised" the model is by held-out text. Lower is better, but it is only comparable between models with the same tokenizer and says little about usefulness. BLEU (precision of n-grams, for translation) and ROUGE (recall of n-grams, for summarization) compare against a reference and miss synonyms. BERTScore matches tokens by embedding similarity instead. Pick a candidate.

Reference: "the meeting was moved to friday afternoon"

4

Benchmarks and their shelf life

Benchmarks saturate, then leak into training data. The defense is a moving target: fresh, held-out or continuously updated tests, plus your own task evaluation.

Contamination in one picture

Detection: n-gram overlap between test items and training data, perplexity that is suspiciously low on test items, and performance drops on fresh rewrites of the same questions.

5

Hallucination: intrinsic vs extrinsic

Intrinsic: the output contradicts the source. Extrinsic: it adds claims the source does not support (true or not). Detection checks faithfulness: split the answer into claims and verify each against the context with NLI or an LLM judge, use self-consistency across samples (SelfCheckGPT), or retrieve evidence. Classify each summary sentence.

Source: "The study enrolled 200 adults and tracked blood pressure for six months. Systolic pressure fell by 8 mmHg on average."

6

LLM-as-judge and its biases

A strong model grades outputs with a rubric: pointwise scores, pairwise comparisons or reference-guided checks. It is fast and scales, but it has predictable biases: position, verbosity, self-enhancement (preferring its own family) and format. Simulate position bias: the judge compares two equally good answers many times.

7

Human preference at scale: arena ratings

In an arena, users chat with two anonymous models and vote for the better answer. Ratings are fitted from these pairwise votes (Elo, or a Bradley-Terry model). Strengths: real prompts, hard to game by memorizing a test. Weaknesses: the user population and prompt mix skew results, style and length sway votes, and new models need many battles before their rating settles.

8

Evaluation in practice

Public benchmarks pick a shortlist; your own evaluation decides. The workhorse is a golden test set: representative, versioned inputs with expected outputs or rubrics, covering common cases, hard edges and known past failures. It runs in CI on every change, then larger evaluations, shadow traffic and production monitoring follow.
