Continuous Delivery

Getting changes to users quickly and safely is a team habit. Small batches, feature flags and staged rollouts make faster releases also the safer ones.

1

Why time to deployment matters

A product's ability to compete, keep its quality, or adapt to regulation is limited by how fast changes reach users. No software is perfect at launch and you'll have to update it, quickly. Code delivers value when users can use it, not when it's submitted, so Google aims to "launch and iterate": shrink the time between "code complete" and real feedback. The chapter's counterintuitive thesis is that faster is safer.

2

Small batches: faster is safer

The fewer changes between one release and the next, the easier it is to find what broke. Slide the release cadence and watch what each release carries. The numbers are illustrative, assuming a team that lands 30 changes a day.

3

Velocity is a team sport

As teams grow, a familiar antipattern appears: a subteam branches off to avoid stepping on others, then struggles with integration and culprit finding. Google prefers developing at head with CI, automatic rollbacks and culprit finding (Chapter 23).

YouTube's vicious cycleA large monolithic Python app; releases needed Build Cops, release managers and volunteers, nearly every one had cherry-picks and respins, and a remote QA team ran a 50-hour manual regression cycle per release. High release cost makes you wait to test more, then someone adds "one more feature", and the release gets slower and riskier, until the experts burn out and leave.
Tempting fixes (resist)Slow down the cadence, go back to heavyweight planning, add governance and risk reviews, and reward low-risk, low-value features. These buy short-term stability and cost long-term velocity.
Best-return investmentArchitectural change: migrate to microservices, sometimes rewrite from scratch with modularity built in. Painful for months; pays back over years.
4

Evaluate changes in isolation: flag-guard features

Many features at different stages coexist in one binary. A flag guard lets new code ship alongside the old path, enabled per build and per audience, and switched by a dynamic config update independently of the binary. It also lets you flip a feature on right before a press release instead of having it leak out with the rollout.

new_checkout flag

Not a perfect safety netFlagged code can still be scraped from the binary, not everything can be flagged without a lot of complexity, and flag changes themselves need careful, gradual rollout through a configuration service.
5

Striving for agility: the release train

Search, Google's oldest binary, once managed a weekly release only with luck: days of building, integrating data, and manually triaging every bug for quality, UX and revenue risk. Developers never knew when their feature would ship. Years of automation, feature deadlines, and simpler plug-in and data integration got it to a reliable release every other day. Two ideas made that possible.

No binary is perfectWith hundreds of contributors, you can't fix every bug before shipping. KPI metrics with clear thresholds let imperfect builds launch and make contested calls clearer. Sometimes the answer is still "wait": a bug that blanked results for a dialect spoken on one small Philippine island delayed a major release, because every user deserves reliable results.
Meet your release deadlineIf you're late, the train leaves without you. Even the panicked Friday-night "we have a contract!" request costs a release engineer four hours to cut and test a new binary. With frequent trains, missing one costs hours, not days.

You just missed the cutoff. When's the next train?

6

Quality and user focus: ship only what gets used

Successful products bloat, and a fast release train magnifies it. For client apps the user's device pays in space, download and data for features they never use; developers pay in slower builds and odd bugs. Modular code enables dynamic deployments that ship only what a given device needs, and A/B experiments make trade-offs between a feature's cost and its value explicit. Google often staffs teams just for ongoing product efficiency.

Decouple "can release" from "does release"Users may dislike frequent app updates. A smooth CD process separates how often a viable release is produced from how often users receive one. Choose the latter deliberately.
7

Shifting left: data-driven decisions earlier

With more than two billion Android devices, comprehensive qualification is impossible. A release manager's reframe, device diversity is "not a problem, but a fact", led to: representative testing instead of comprehensive, staged rollouts to growing percentages, and automated A/B releases that prove quality statistically. Since merely pushing an update shifts user metrics, larger apps A/B test the release itself against a placebo: the old version shipped again.

8

Changing team culture: discipline in deployment

Small teams take turns deploying. As they grow to hundreds, changes and risk per release rise superlinearly, and a release can take a week to roll out. "Always Be Deploying" keeps divergence from a known-good state small. On Google Maps, features matter, but very seldom is one so important that a release should wait for it. One job of release is to protect the product from the developers: urgency to launch never outranks the experience of existing users.
