Code Review

Every change at Google is reviewed before it lands. Review catches bugs, but its bigger payoffs are code others can understand, a consistent codebase, and knowledge that spreads.

The code review flow

At Google, essentially every change is reviewed before it's committed. A change is called a changelist (CL), and reviews happen in a tool called Critique (Chapter 19). Step through the typical life of a change.

Code is a liabilityCode has to be maintained by someone forever after, like fuel weighing down an airplane: necessary, but costly. New code should solve a real problem, not add another alternative. Some Googlers say: "If you're writing it from scratch, you're doing it wrong."
2

Three kinds of approval

Every change needs three things: an LGTM from another engineer (correctness and comprehension), owner approval from someone listed as an owner of that directory, and readability approval from someone certified in the language. Usually one reviewer covers all three, and the author's own status counts. Set the author's status and see who's needed.

3

Why review code?

Catching bugs is the obvious benefit, but not the biggest. Studies suggest review catches a meaningful share of defects early; tests and static analysis now catch many correctness issues anyway. Tap to see the five benefits the chapter lists.

The first readerA reviewer is the first person besides the author to read the code. If they can't understand it, future readers won't either. That's why the reviewer's confusion is valid feedback, even when the code is correct.
4

Write small changes

Small changes are the single most important practice. They get faster, more thorough reviews, are easier to reason about, and make bugs easier to trace. Google suggests "small" means roughly 200 lines or fewer; about 35% of changes touch a single file, and reviewers are expected to respond within a working day. Slide the size (illustrative curves).

5

More best practices

Be polite and professional

Reviewers: be prompt, ask why before assuming it's wrong, and don't drip-feed comments round after round. Authors: you are not your code; it belongs to the team. Treat each comment as a TODO to address, even if you disagree, and explain why. Suggest an alternative and ask the reviewer to take another look (PTAL).

Keep reviewers to a minimum

Most reviews at Google have exactly one reviewer. The first LGTM matters most; each extra reviewer adds little and costs a lot.
