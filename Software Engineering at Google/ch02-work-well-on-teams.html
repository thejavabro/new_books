How to Work Well on Teams

Software is a team sport. The biggest obstacles are rarely technical: insecurity makes us hide work, and ego makes us bad collaborators. Three social pillars fix most of it.

1

"Help me hide my code"

Engineers often ask for ways to keep work private until it's "ready": hide a branch, delay a launch until it's perfect, avoid showing a half-built design. The common thread is insecurity, the fear that others will judge an unfinished idea. Underneath that is the Genius Myth: the story that great software comes from lone geniuses who vanish and return with a masterpiece.

Who really built it?

tap a famous name

We all love a hero. But the people we lionize became leaders of communities, not solitary coders. Real work happens in teams, and hiding works against them.

2

Hiding considered harmful

Working alone in secret feels safe, but it raises your risk. You might be building the wrong thing, repeating something that already exists, or going down a path a colleague could have warned you off in five minutes. The book's image: you're a bike designer who reveals a brilliant new gear shifter, only to find someone patented it years ago. Get feedback early. Slide how long you work before showing anyone.

Tight feedback loopsProgramming already has fast loops: the compiler, the tests. The same idea applies to people. Show work early, take small steps and course-correct. One caveat from the author: too much feedback before you know your own direction can be noise, so share once you have a clear goal.
3

The bus factor

The bus factor is the number of people who would have to be hit by a bus before your project is doomed. If only one person understands a component, your bus factor for it is one. Sharing knowledge, pairing and writing things down raise it. Click people to take them off the project (a vacation, a new job, a bus) and see which components lose everyone who knew them.

4

A note on offices

Twenty-five years ago, conventional wisdom held that engineers need private offices with closed doors. The author calls that dangerous: uninterrupted time is wasted if you spend it on the wrong thing. But the modern swing to giant open floors, a hundred people with no walls, is just as bad, because people stop talking to avoid disturbing everyone. The suggested middle ground: rooms of four to eight teammates, plus a team signal for "busy, only interrupt for emergencies" (a spoken "breakpoint" protocol, a token on the monitor, headphones). Headphones all day, though, wall you off as surely as a door.

5

The three pillars: humility, respect, trust

"Software engineering is a team endeavor." To work well with others, stop focusing on yourself. Nearly every social conflict comes down to a lack of one of three things:

HHumilityYou are not the center of the universe. You are not omniscient or infallible. You're open to self-improvement.
RRespectYou genuinely care about the people you work with, and appreciate their abilities and accomplishments.
TTrustYou believe others are competent and will do the right thing, and you're okay letting them drive.

Blameless postmortems: fail fast, learn always

Failure is an option, as long as you learn from it. A blameless postmortem looks at root causes without hunting for a culprit, and it is shared widely so others learn too. Build one: tick the parts that belong in a good postmortem and leave out the bad ones.

Seven ingredients belong; three don't.
Patience and influenceThe author recalls pairing with a colleague whose top-down style clashed with his own bottom-up style; it took patience to make it work. And being open to influence matters: the more you're willing to be changed by others, the more you'll be able to persuade them. Admitting you were wrong, or saying "I don't know", builds credibility rather than costing it.
