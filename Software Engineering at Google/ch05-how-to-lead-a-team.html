How to Lead a Team

Leading engineers is less about control and more about removing obstacles. Here are the roles, the traps new leaders fall into, and the habits of leaders people want to work for.

1

Managers, tech leads, and both

Every team needs a leader, but at Google that can mean two different jobs. The engineering manager looks after people: performance, careers, happiness, while keeping the product's needs in view. The tech lead (TL) looks after the technical side: design, priorities, velocity, often while still writing code. On small, new teams one person may do both: a tech lead manager (TLM). Pick a team size.

Influence without authorityLeadership doesn't require reports. The chapter's examples: a famously influential senior engineer who directly led only a small fraction of engineering, and the author's own team of fewer than six engineers that got more than 50 Google products exporting data through Google Takeout, with no executive mandate. How? Identify a strategic need, tie it to the company's mission, and make joining easy.
2

From individual contributor to leader

Many engineers avoid leading because they've seen bad managers, or fear losing time to code. The chapter is honest: you'll write less code, and your wins become slower and fuzzier. But "the only thing to fear is everything" gives way to a quieter satisfaction: helping a whole team do more than you could alone. Leadership here means servant leadership: create an atmosphere of humility, respect and trust, remove obstacles, and help the team reach consensus. Management is maintaining the team's technical and social health, not driving people.

"Manager" as a four-letter wordOld-style management came from factories, where workers were treated as interchangeable and pushed with carrots and sticks. Engineers aren't assembly-line workers, and treating them that way drives the good ones away.
Failure is an optionLet your team take risks. Some will fail. Treat failure as a chance to learn, and celebrate ambitious attempts, so people keep trying hard things.
3

Be a zen master: your reactions are amplified

Picture the org chart as a chain of gears: each level up is a bigger gear. A small turn at the top sends the small gears at the bottom spinning. A leader's offhand worry becomes the team's panic. Stay calm, keep skepticism quieter, and when someone asks for advice, ask questions that help them find the answer rather than handing it over.

5

Track happiness, and ask the unexpected question

Ask people how they're doing, really. One colleague starts one-on-ones by asking reports to rate their happiness from 1 to 10. Another useful habit: end a conversation with "Is there anything you need?" And remember that people have lives outside work; a little slack during a hard time earns loyalty. Try a one-on-one.

6

People are like plants: motivation and direction

Some plants want sun and little water; others the reverse. Giving everyone the same treatment is equal, but nobody gets what they need. Team members likewise need different amounts of motivation and direction. Tap a quadrant.

For motivation, the chapter leans on Dan Pink's Drive: extrinsic rewards (money) matter less than intrinsic motivation, which grows with three things. Adjust them for a hypothetical engineer.

7

Delegate, but get your hands dirty

New leaders often keep doing everything themselves because it's faster. It is, once. But each delegated task teaches someone, and next time they're quick. Seek to replace yourself: if your team can't run without you, you can't grow. Still, pick up an unglamorous task now and then; it earns respect. Compare over repeated tasks.
