Engineering for Equity

Products built by a narrow group tend to fail everyone outside it. Bias is the default state, so equity has to be engineered on purpose, in hiring, in process, and in what you choose to ship.

1

Bias is the default

Everyone carries unconscious biases, and organizations carry them too. When the people who build a product are mostly alike, the product quietly fits people like them and fails others. This is not usually malice; it is the default outcome unless you design against it. The chapter's starting point: Google's engineering workforce has not looked like its global user base, and that has shown up in products.

Case study: an image classifier failsIn 2015 a user pointed out that Google Photos had labelled photos of his Black friends as "gorillas". The causes the chapter names: training data that didn't represent the population, too little Black representation in the teams designing the system, a target market defined too narrowly, and tests that didn't catch it, so users did. Google's response was slow and incomplete, and the underlying problem was still not properly fixed years later.
2

When the data leaves people out

A model learns from the examples it sees. If one group is rare in the training data, the model does worse for that group, and if your test set has the same skew, the tests won't notice. Play with an illustrative model (not real figures).

The chapter makes a similar point about facial recognition: if both the training data and the builders represent a narrow slice of people, you cannot expect valid output. Better to slow down and gather more complete data than to ship something that harms some users.

3

Exceptional engineers build multicultural capacity

A computer science degree is a foundation, but it doesn't teach you how a product helps or harms different groups. The chapter argues that an exceptional engineer also has discernment: knowing when to build something and when not to. That means learning who your users are, especially people unlike you, including people who might use your product to cause harm. It's the employer's job, too, to provide continuous, multidisciplinary training.

4

Make diversity actionable, and reject single fixes

It is easy to say inequity is a centuries-old social problem, too big for one team. The chapter calls that a detour. Every lead and manager can act: balanced candidate slates, fair distribution of growth opportunities. And no single fix works. The popular idea that the hiring pipeline alone will fix representation ignores retention and progression. Compare two strategies over time (illustrative model).

5

Design for the user least like you

Building for the majority first and patching for "edge cases" later gives the already-advantaged a head start. Instead, start research with multilingual, multicultural groups across countries, incomes, abilities and ages, and design for the person who will have the most difficulty. It usually makes the product better for everyone. Pick features and see who benefits.
