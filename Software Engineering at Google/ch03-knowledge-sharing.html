Knowledge Sharing

An organization should be able to answer most of its own questions. That takes people willing to ask, experts willing to teach, and systems that turn one-off answers into lasting knowledge.

1

Why learning is hard in a big organization

Your organization knows its own problems better than any random person on the internet, so it ought to be able to answer most of its own questions. That takes two things: experts who know the answers, and ways to spread what they know. As Google grew, it ran into the same obstacles many companies do. Tap each.

PhilosophySoftware engineering is multi-person development of multi-version programs. Code is only part of it; knowledge is the other part. Human experts give tailored help but don't scale. Documentation scales but goes stale and can't answer a new question. You need both, plus a culture that rewards sharing.
2

Setting the stage: psychological safety

Learning requires saying "I don't know". If asking a basic question gets you mocked, people stop asking and start guessing. Google pairs new hires with a volunteer mentor (at Google over a year, not on the newcomer's team), so questions feel safe. In larger groups, the tone of everyday replies sets the culture. Ask the same question and compare the replies.

Which social rule does this break?

Google points to the Recurse Center's social rules: no feigned surprise, no "well-actually"s, no back-seat driving, no subtle "-isms".

3

Growing your own knowledge

Start with yourself: ask questions and understand context. The more senior you are, the more your asking normalizes it for others. Before changing something odd, find out why it exists. The book invokes Chesterton's fence: don't remove a fence until you know why someone put it there. But don't let unexplained code become a "haunted graveyard" either: once you understand the reason, you are free to change it.

5

Scaling the organization's knowledge

At the level of the whole company, Google relies on culture and systems rather than individual heroics.

Respect is part of the jobExpertise doesn't excuse being a jerk. The leadership section of Google's engineering job ladder says leaders improve the people around them and the team's psychological safety, and states plainly that jerks are not good leaders. Senior leaders back this with a widely cited internal "No Jerks" document.
6

Readability: standardized mentorship through code review

Every change at Google needs readability approval from someone certified in that language. Engineers earn readability by sending changes through a central pool of volunteer reviewers who coach them on idioms and best practice until the comments dry up. Around 20% of engineers are in the process at any time, as authors or reviewers; about 1 to 2% are readability reviewers. Step through a typical journey.

Why botherCodebase-wide consistency: code in a language looks the same across tens of thousands of engineers and decades, so readers focus on what it does. People can switch teams easily, and large-scale changes become possible. Google's research team found changes by authors with readability take significantly less time to review and submit, and graduates report learning from it.
What it costsTeams with no readability holders need outside reviewers; authors in the process face extra review rounds; and a human process only scales linearly. Google judges the trade worthwhile and keeps tuning it.
