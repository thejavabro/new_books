Measuring Engineering Productivity

Measuring engineers is expensive and easy to get wrong. Google's research team first asks whether a measurement would change anything, then traces every metric back to a goal.

1

Why measure engineering productivity?

As an organization grows, adding people adds communication overhead, so output per engineer tends to fall. Google's answer is to make each engineer more productive, and it has a dedicated team of engineering productivity researchers, mixing software engineers with social scientists, to find out what actually helps. Their first lesson: measurement is expensive. It takes people, can slow everyone down, and can change behavior in ways that hide the real problem.

The running exampleGoogle's readability process (Chapter 3) costs authors and reviewers time. With autoformatters and static analysis improving, some suspected it had become a hazing ritual. The readability team asked: are the costs of going through readability worth the benefits?
2

Triage: is it even worth measuring?

Before any study, the researchers ask a series of questions. If the answers show no one will act on the result, they don't measure. Walk through them for your own question.

3

Goals, signals, metrics (GSM)

Pick metrics last. Start from the goal (the end result you care about, without any mention of how to measure it), then a signal (how you'd know you achieved it, even if unmeasurable), and only then a metric (a measurable proxy for the signal). This avoids the streetlight effect (measuring what's easy to see) and metrics creep (collecting numbers nobody needs), and makes every metric traceable to a goal.

QUANTS: cover every side of productivity

Improving one aspect often hurts another: speed up reviews and quality may suffer. To catch trade-offs, Google checks goals across five components, remembered as QUANTS. Tap a letter.

5

Validate metrics with data, quantitative and qualitative

A metric has to be checked against reality. The team once measured each engineer's median build time, then surveyed engineers right after a build started (experience sampling). Some replied that they hadn't started a build at all: automated tools had. Those builds didn't block anyone, so the metric was changed to exclude them. Toggle to see the effect (illustrative numbers).

QuantitativeLogs give scale and power across the company and over time, but no narrative: they can't say why someone used an old tool.
QualitativeSurveys and interviews give the why. If qualitative and quantitative results disagree, the quantitative metric is usually the one that's wrong.

For readability, the team combined three sources: a survey right after people finished the process, a large quarterly survey on outcomes readability should affect, and fine-grained tool logs of review and submit times.

6

Taking action and tracking results

Research ends with recommendations, ideally built into tools and incentives rather than "please change how you work". For readability, the result was clear: worth it. Engineers who earned readability were satisfied, felt they learned, and their changes were reviewed and submitted faster, even with fewer reviewers. The study also surfaced pain points, and language teams improved the tooling and transparency.
