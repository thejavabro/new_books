Leading at Scale

As a leader moves up, problems become broader and vaguer. Three habits keep that sustainable: make trade-off decisions, build teams that run without you, and guard your own time and energy.

1

Three habits for leading leaders

Once you lead a team of teams, you're further from the code and the problems are broader and vaguer. The chapter boils the job down to three "Always Be" habits. Tap each.

2

Always be deciding

At this level, few problems have a correct answer. Your job is to find the right trade-offs for the moment, and iterate. The chapter opens with a parable told by a friend who flies a lot. Step through it.

The angry passenger optimized hard for the wrong thing because he couldn't see the whole picture. Leaders do the same. The method: identify the blinders (assumptions nobody questions), identify the key trade-offs, then decide and iterate.

3

Case study: the latency of Web Search

Years of small quality improvements made Search results richer, and slower. Latency hurts engagement, so it crept up until it canceled out the quality gains. The old fix was an occasional "code yellow": everyone drops everything to optimize, latency drops, then creeps back within months. The blinder: treating latency as a side effect rather than a goal. Once leaders saw three linked quantities (quality, latency, serving capacity), they could trade them deliberately.

4

Always be leaving: build a self-driving team

Your job is not to solve the problem yourself but to build an organization that solves a whole class of problems without you. That raises the bus factor of leadership itself. How: divide the problem space into subproblems, delegate them to leaders (letting them make mistakes), and adjust and iterate. For Search latency, the split was work on the symptoms (making things faster) and the causes (making it harder for latency to creep in), each with its own leader.

Anchor identity on a problem, not a productA team named after its product dies with the product. A team defined by a problem ("make Search fast", "help developers find code") can outlive any single solution, and is free to replace its own product with something better.
5

Always be scaling: the cycle of success

Success brings a reward: a new problem, usually without new people. So the cycle of success is really a spiral. Step through it.

6

Important versus urgent: learn to drop balls

Leaders drift into pure firefighting: every channel feels like a denial-of-service attack on their attention. Eisenhower's line about urgent versus important problems applies. The chapter's tactic: picture your incoming balls in three piles. Roughly the bottom 20% are neither urgent nor important and easy to drop. The top 20% are critical and only you can handle them. The middle 60% is a mixed bag. Do the top 20% and give yourself permission to drop the other 80%: what matters will come back. Sort these balls.

Protecting your energy

Stamina grows with time, but leaders also learn to recharge on purpose.
