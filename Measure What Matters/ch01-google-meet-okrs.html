1

A gift at the ping-pong table

In the fall of 1999 John Doerr, then a venture capitalist who had just put $11.8 million into a year-old search start-up, stood in front of roughly thirty Googlers gathered around a ping-pong table. The founders, Larry Page and Sergey Brin, had big dreams (Larry talked of $10 billion in revenue) but little management experience. Doerr's gift was a goal-setting method he had learned from Andy Grove at Intel: OKRs, short for Objectives and Key Results.

His mantra explains why he cared: ideas are easy, execution is everything. OKRs are not a substitute for judgment, leadership or culture. But when those are in place, they turn intentions into a short, public, checkable plan.

Sergey's verdictGoogle had no organizing principle yet, he said, so this one might as well be it. The fit turned out to be deep: a data-loving, open company met a data-driven, transparent method.

5

Goals gone wild, or goals that work?

A 2009 Harvard Business School paper, "Goals Gone Wild", listed disasters blamed on goal pursuit (the Ford Pinto, Sears auto repair, Enron) and proposed a warning label:

WARNING!Goals may cause narrowed focus, unethical behavior, increased risk taking, decreased cooperation and decreased motivation. Use care.

Doerr grants the point (any system can be run badly) but sides with the evidence. In 1968 psychologist Edwin Locke showed that hard goals beat easy ones and specific hard goals beat vague ones. Over a thousand studies since then have backed him; about 90 percent of field experiments found clear, challenging goals raise productivity. Try the two dials.

The curve is a teaching sketch of Locke's two findings, not data from the book. Past a point, a goal becomes impossible and people give up, so the line bends down at the top end.

Key results = howSpecific, time-bound, aggressive yet realistic, measurable, verifiable. Usually three to five.

EngagementWritten, shared goals are a top driver of engagement; most workers are disengaged.
