The annual review: costly and mostly futile

Annual performance reviews take, on average, 7.5 hours of manager time per direct report. Only 12 percent of HR leaders call them highly effective; only 6 percent think they are worth the time. Recency bias, stack rankings and bell curves make them unfair anyway. Einstein's line fits: not everything that counts can be counted. Work out what reviews cost your organization.

2

CFRs: OKRs' younger sibling

The replacement is continuous performance management, delivered through CFRs: Conversations (real exchanges between manager and contributor about performance), Feedback (two-way or networked, to judge progress and guide improvement) and Recognition (appreciation for contributions of every size). They give OKRs a human voice. Back to football: tap the field.

Why they need each otherBetterWorks CEO Doug Dennerline: talk only about whether a goal was hit and you lose the context (was it harder than expected, was it the right goal?). But without goals, what are you even talking about?

Annual versus continuous

About 10 percent of the Fortune 500 had already dropped the annual review when the book was written. Many large firms run both side by side for a while. Flip between the models.

The nonprofit Pact killed its annual review when it adopted OKRs. The fourth element is a habit: when someone says "nice job", ask "thanks, what one thing did you like about it?"

4

An amicable divorce: OKRs and compensation

Step one is blunt: separate raises and bonuses from OKRs. Pay is a backward-looking conversation, usually once a year. OKR talk is forward-looking and ongoing. Try the book's dilemma.

Contributor ASet extreme stretch goals and reached 75% of them. Pushed hard all quarter.
Contributor BReached 90% of modest key results. Didn't push himself and skipped several important team meetings.
If bonuses follow OKR scores, who gets more?

That doesn't mean goals and reviews never meet. A data-driven record of what someone achieved is a good antidote to bias. At Google, OKRs are a third or less of a performance rating, behind cross-team feedback and context, and raw OKR scores are wiped from the system after each cycle. Conversations: the one-on-one

Grove made one-on-ones mandatory at Intel, estimating that ninety minutes of a manager's time could lift the quality of a report's work for two weeks. It is the subordinate's meeting: their agenda, their tone; the manager is there to learn and coach, including on heart-to-heart issues. 6

Feedback only works when it's specific

Sheryl Sandberg defines feedback as an opinion, grounded in observation, that tells us what impression we make. Today's workers want to give it to managers, not wait a year to receive it. In mature organizations it is ad hoc, real-time and runs every direction, including 360-degree peer feedback, private when it's for growth, routed to HR when it reveals organizational problems. Pick the more useful version.

8

Ditching annual reviews: the Adobe story

Every February, after review season, Adobe's voluntary attrition spiked. In 2012, jet-lagged on a trip to India, executive Donna Morris told a reporter Adobe would scrap annual reviews and stack rankings. She hadn't yet told her HR team or the CEO. Her follow-up intranet post ("if we did away with the annual review, what would you like in its place?") became one of the most discussed in company history, and produced Check-in. Compare before and after.

Done well, corrective feedback is the greatest gift you can give someone.
