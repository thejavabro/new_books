Operation Crush: An Intel Story

In late 1979 Intel was losing the 16-bit chip war to Motorola. Within a month, one cascade of objectives and key results had the whole company marching the same way.

1

Intel under siege, late 1979

This chapter is told largely by Bill Davidow, the Intel executive who led the campaign. Intel's 16-bit chip, the 8086, was losing to Motorola's 68000 and Zilog's Z8000, which were faster and easier to program. Intel had already lost the lead in memory chips; microprocessors were its best hope. Step through how fast the company moved.

2

Same chips, new story

Not one Intel product was changed. There was no time to rebuild the 8086. Instead the task force changed the terms of the fight: the chip you choose today is your most important decision for the next decade, so choose the company, not the instruction set. Toggle whose pitch you're hearing.

3

How a key result becomes someone's objective

The nine programs of the plan each became a company key result. Then each department took the key results it owned and made them its own objectives, with its own, finer key results. Tap the highlighted key result to drill down a level (these are the book's real Q2 1980 OKRs).

"As measured by"Davidow added this connective phrase to Intel's corporate OKRs: we will achieve this objective, as measured by these key results. It made the link between what and how explicit to everyone.

The cascade ran both ways. Top management couldn't know every mechanic of the battle; someone at the bottom might say "our benchmarks are weak, I'll write better ones." You can tell people to clean up a mess, but should you tell them which broom to use?

4

Everyone marching to Switzerland

Jim Lally, a sceptic until Grove sat him down, later explained the point with geometry: tell everyone to go to the center of Europe, and if some march to France, some to Germany and some to Italy, the vectors cancel out. Point them the same way and the results add up. Drag the alignment slider.

5

Turning a billion-dollar company on a dime

At the kickoff about a hundred managers heard Robert Noyce and Grove say: we will win in 16-bit microprocessors. The message reached two levels of management at once, a third within a day. Davidow's point: rallying speeches usually fizzle because people don't know what to do next. OKRs told every group exactly what to do and gave black-and-white criteria for judging it.

Culture countsCrush started because a district sales manager sent an alarmed eight-page telex, and his boss forwarded it to the president. Without people who feel free to say "you don't understand the market" without retribution, there is no Operation Crush. Grove's line: bad companies are destroyed by crisis, good ones survive, great ones are improved by it.
