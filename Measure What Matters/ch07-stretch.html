Superpower #4: Stretch for Amazing

Some goals must be hit in full; others are set so high that 70 percent is a triumph. Knowing which is which unlocks 10x thinking. Chapters 12, 13 and 14 combined.

1

Innovate, don't just iterate

Bill Campbell liked to say companies that stop innovating die, and he meant innovate, not iterate. Conservative goals stifle that. Jim Collins's name for the alternative is the BHAG, a Big Hairy Audacious Goal: clear, compelling, unifying, like the moon landing. Doerr's favorite definition of entrepreneurs fits: people who do more than anyone thinks possible, with less than anyone thinks possible.

Locke again: harder goals, higher performanceAcross a dozen studies, people with very hard goals hit them far less often than people with easy goals, yet consistently performed at a higher level. They were also more engaged: challenge makes work more interesting.

Two baskets: committed and aspirational

Google splits OKRs into two kinds. Committed goals (releases, bookings, hiring, revenue) must be met in full, on time. Aspirational goals are bigger, riskier bets on the future, from any level; they fail about 40 percent of the time, and that's expected. Sort these (invented) goals.

Committed · 100%

Aspirational · ~70%

The mix is a cultural choice that can change each quarter. Are we in survival mode, or do we have cash to bet big? Agile and daring to crack a new market, or conservative to firm up our position?

3

Grove and the need to stretch

Grove admired Abraham Maslow's hierarchy of needs: once basic needs are met, people move up toward self-actualization, testing the outer limits of their abilities. Some people do this unprompted. For everyone else, Grove said, stretched goals draw out peak performance. Tap a level.

4

Operation Crush: the road to Tahiti

During Crush, the task force first proposed 1,000 design wins in a year, already 50 percent above the prior year. Somehow it became 2,000: one win per salesperson per month, for a chip customers were hanging up on. Davidow wanted a rallying point. Step through the year.

5

The gospel of 10x

Larry Page argues that a 10 percent improvement means you're doing what everyone else does; you won't fail spectacularly, and you won't succeed wildly. Ten times better forces you to rethink the problem from physics up. Compare the two mindsets.

At Google, as in Grove's day, aspirational OKRs aim for about 60 to 70 percent attainment. Living there means moonshots, some colossal misfires (Doerr names Helpouts and Google Answers), and harder questions: what radical action, what to stop, where to find resources. Often a healthy share of the "impossible" goals get fully met.

Stretch with careStretch too far, too fast and a team snaps. Leaders must show the outcome matters and that it's attainable. Not everyone can afford Google's 40 percent miss rate: at one firm full of straight-A students, anything short of 100 felt like failure. MyFitnessPal treats every OKR as committed. Doerr's advice: aim for at least a modest stretch, and let precision and ambition grow with experience.
6

Stretch: the Google Chrome story

Sundar Pichai grew up in Chennai, where his family waited years for a rotary phone; it changed their lives. At Google his first big win was growing Toolbar users more than 10x. In 2008 his team set an objective to build the next-generation client platform for web apps, with one key result he privately thought impossible: 20 million seven-day active users in year one, starting from zero. Larry Page wanted people "uncomfortably excited". Watch the misses and the eventual hit.

The misses weren't wasted. Each "what will we do differently?" produced something: distribution deals with PC makers, the company's biggest offline ad campaign ("Dear Sophie"), Mac and Linux versions. Today Chrome has over a billion mobile users alone.

7

Stretch: the YouTube story

By 2012 YouTube's pace of innovation had slowed. Engineering leader Cristos Goodrow found teams writing thirty or forty objectives for ten people, finishing under half. Shishir Mehrotra brought in Stephen Covey's big rocks: put the big things in the jar first. Try both orders.

Next, the right metric. Goodrow's 2011 email was titled "Watch time, and only watch time." His thought experiment: someone searches "how do I tie a bow tie". Which video should rank first?
