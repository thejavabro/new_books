Superpower #3: Track for Accountability

An OKR is not set and forgotten. Regular check-ins, honest scoring and the freedom to change course keep goals alive. Chapters 10 and 11 combined.

1

Set them and forget them? Meet the zombies

Ordinary business goals get written once and filed. OKRs are meant to be alive: they move through three phases, setup, midlife tracking and wrap-up. Without regular updates the gap between plan and reality widens every day, and by quarter's end you have zombie OKRs: words on paper with no life in them.

Tools matter at scale. One Fortune 500 company kept 82,000 people's annual goals in Word files; going quarterly would mean 328,000 files a year. Public in theory, but if nobody can find a goal, is it really transparent? Drag the slider to see how fast a goal drifts when nobody looks at it.

2

Every flock needs a shepherd

The system only works if everyone on the team uses it: no opt-outs. Late adopters and procrastinators need an OKR shepherd. At Google that was product chief Jonathan Rosenberg, famous for reminder emails both nice and threatening. Once compliance hit nearly 100 percent he stopped sending them, and a quarter later several product managers missed their OKRs. His conclusion, roughly: it isn't the tone of the note that matters, it's that a note gets sent at all. And this isn't busywork; it's how priorities get set.

Shape of the story, not real figures.

3

Midlife: progress is the best motivator

Like a fitness tracker, OKR tools show progress visually. Daniel Pink, author of Drive, calls making progress in one's work the single greatest motivator. OKRs don't need daily tracking, but weekly check-ins are best to prevent slippage.

A California goal study

share of goals achieved, relative

People who wrote goals down and sent weekly progress reports to a friend achieved 43 percent more than those who only thought about their goals.

At any point in the cycle you have four options: continue a green goal, update a yellow one, start a new OKR, or stop one that has outlived its use. Try them on this mid-quarter dashboard (invented example).

A real "stop": Remind's payments flopRemind's first paid feature, peer-to-peer payments, solved no clear problem and nobody used it. The team changed the goal to an event system ("field trip next week: coming? want to pay?") and it took off. Goals serve the purpose, not the other way round. Just tell everyone who depends on a goal before you drop it, and note what you learned.
4

Wrap-up 1: scoring

The simplest way to score an objective is to average the completion of its key results. Google uses a 0 to 1 scale: 0.7–1.0 green (delivered), 0.4–0.6 yellow (progress, fell short), 0.0–0.3 red (no real progress). Here is the real Operation Crush OKR from Q2 1980, with the actual grades preloaded. Move the sliders.

Establish the 8086 as the highest-performance 16-bit microprocessor family
Objective score

Why 0.625 was respectableIntel's board judged it a bit below expectations, not far, because they knew how aggressive the goals were. Intel entered each quarter expecting to miss some. A department that came close to 100 percent was assumed to be aiming too low, and there would be hell to pay.
5

Wrap-up 2: the number isn't the whole story

Raw scores need human judgment. Same key result, "bring in ten new customers", four very different quarters (from the book's table). Tap one.

Another example: a PR team's key result was three national articles. They got two, but one was a Wall Street Journal cover story. Raw score 67 percent; honest self-assessment, 9 out of 10. Facilitators help recalibrate people who grade themselves too harshly, or too kindly. There are no judgments, only learnings.

6

Wrap-up 3: reflect, then celebrate

Endless action becomes a hamster wheel. Doerr's recipe for satisfaction: set aggressive goals, achieve most of them, pause to reflect, repeat. As John Dewey put it, we don't learn from experience; we learn from reflecting on it. Tap through the closing questions.

An unfinished objective may roll into next quarter with fresh key results, or be dropped because its moment has passed. Then throw a party. You've earned it.

7

Track: the Gates Foundation story

In 2000 the Gates Foundation became a $20 billion start-up that had to give away at least a billion dollars a year, with a mission as big as it gets: everyone deserves a healthy and productive life. CEO Patty Stonesifer heard Doerr's pitch at an Amazon board meeting and brought OKRs in by 2002. With limited time while still at Microsoft, Bill Gates relied on the green-yellow-red reviews; twice he turned down a grant because its goals weren't clear enough.

Mission ≠ objectiveGates: a mission is directional. An objective is a set of concrete steps you are actually trying to achieve. A too-aspirational target (a team once predicted malaria would be gone by 2015) hurts credibility.
Ambitious and disciplinedStonesifer: when key results showed no progress, capital moved elsewhere. Quarterly and annual beats tracked huge goals like ending Guinea worm disease.
