1

An intern meets Andy Grove

In the summer of 1975 Doerr, a business-school student who could write software, talked his way into an internship at Intel. He soon learned who ran the place: Andy Grove, then executive vice president, a Hungarian refugee who had arrived in the US at twenty with no money, little English and poor hearing, and who would become CEO and one of the most admired managers of his era.

At Grove's in-house strategy course Doerr heard an idea that stuck. Grove's old employer, Fairchild, prized expertise; Intel prized results. It almost didn't matter what you knew, only what you could actually get done with it. The company slogan was "Intel delivers". Then Grove sketched his system for making that happen.

Grove's own example, paraphrasedObjective (the direction): dominate the mid-range microcomputer component business. Key result for this quarter (a milestone): win ten new designs for the 8085 chip. Whether you "dominated" can be argued for years; whether you won ten designs is a yes or no at quarter end.

3

What Grove changed

Grove called his version "iMBOs", Intel Management by Objectives, but nearly every feature was the opposite of the classic MBO. Flip the switch.

Why did MBOs fade? Goals trickled down slowly, went stale, hid in silos, shrank into soulless KPIs, and, worst of all, were tied to pay. If missing a goal costs you your bonus, nobody risks an ambitious one.

4

Measure output, not activity

Grove's big move was to bring factory thinking to knowledge work: value output, not busyness. Drucker called the opposite the "activity trap". On an assembly line the difference is obvious; at a desk it isn't. Slide from activity to output and watch what a goal looks like.

5

A first OKR, and the freedom to say no

Within days, Doerr wrote his own. His job was to prove Intel's 8080 chip beat Motorola's 6800. He typed it up and pinned it to his cubicle where anyone could read it, and found everyone else's, up to the CEO, were just as visible.

Objective: Demonstrate the 8080's superior performance compared with the Motorola 6800.as measured by1. Deliver five benchmarks.2. Develop a demo.3. Develop sales training materials for the field force.4. Call on three customers to prove the material works.

The surprise was how liberating it felt. When someone asked for unrelated work mid-quarter, his written priorities backed him in saying no. Try it: requests arrive at an intern's desk. Accept or decline based on the OKR above. (The requests are invented.)
