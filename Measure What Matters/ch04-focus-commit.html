Superpower #1: Focus and Commit

The first superpower is choosing. A handful of well-made OKRs says yes to what matters and no to the rest, and leaders have to commit to them in public. Chapters 4, 5 and 6 combined.

1

Focus begins with a hard choice

Measuring what matters starts with one question: what is most important for the next three (or six, or twelve) months? Strong organizations pick a handful of initiatives that can make a real difference and defer the rest. A wrong decision can be corrected once results come in; a non-decision teaches nothing.

Top-line OKRs are leadership's job, and leaders must commit in public. Doerr treats "all my goals are team goals" from a CEO as a red flag. But good ideas also rise from below: a YouTube product manager, Rick Klau, proposed a six-month OKR to fix the site's login experience. Larry Page made it a company-wide OKR, with one catch: three months, not six. With every eye on that team, other teams rushed to help. They finished on time (and shipped a week late).

Mostof 11,000 senior executives and managers surveyed couldn't name their company's top priorities; only half could name even one
Say the why, then say it againPeople want meaning, not just milestones. LinkedIn's Jeff Weiner: when you're tired of saying it, people are starting to hear it.
2

Pick a rhythm short enough to learn from

Grove insisted feedback must arrive soon after the work it measures. If you plan yearly, set OKRs at least quarterly. Doerr's default is a quarterly cycle, often paired with annual OKRs for the longer view, but there is "no religion": six-week cycles to match engineering sprints, or monthly for a young start-up still searching for product-market fit. Compare how many chances to course-correct each rhythm gives you.

Pair every quantity with a quality

The Ford Pinto shows what single-minded targets can do. Ford's goals were "under 2,000 pounds and under $2,000"; safety was not on the list. A one-pound, one-dollar plastic part that stopped the fuel tank being punctured was cut as extra cost and weight. Hundreds died. Decades later, one-dimensional sales targets at Wells Fargo pushed staff to open millions of accounts customers never asked for.

Grove's fix: measure effect and counter-effect. Pair each output key result with one that guards quality. Match the pairs below (from the book's table).

From a weak OKR to a strong one

Same objective, three levels of key results. Notice how "strong" mixes outcomes (lap speed, pit time, errors) with inputs that drive them (wind-tunnel tests, practice).

Don't let the perfect be the enemy of the good. Sundar Pichai described spending ninety minutes on a single OKR line, but OKRs can be edited or dropped during the cycle. They are works in progress, not commandments in stone.

5

Less is more: three to five

"If we try to focus on everything, we focus on nothing," wrote Grove. Every commitment uses up a chance to commit to something else. Larry Page's version: put more wood behind fewer arrows. Set how many objectives a team takes on with the same effort. (A toy model: each objective needs a minimum push to get over the line.)

6

Focus: the Remind story

Brett Kopf grew up with ADHD and dyslexia. A teacher, Denise Whitefield, changed his life with one habit: list today's work, then pick one thing and finish it. That habit, plus the link between his mother and his teacher, became Remind: safe text messaging between teachers, students and parents, with no personal phone numbers exposed. Step through how focus shaped the company.

Objective: Support company hiring.1. Hire 1 director of finance and operations (talk to at least 3 candidates).2. Source 1 product marketing manager (meet 5 candidates this quarter).3. Source 1 product manager (meet 5 candidates this quarter).
Saying no to a beloved featureTeachers kept asking for repeating messages ("bring your novel every Monday"). A classic delight feature, but would it move engagement? No. So it was shelved, a hard call for a teacher-obsessed company that the OKR discipline made possible.
7

Commit: the Nuna story

Jini Kim signed her family up for Medicaid at age nine, after her autistic brother Kimong's first seizure. Years later, as a former Google product manager, she founded Nuna and won a contract to build the first database covering all Medicaid members: 74.5 million people. She knew OKRs from Google, yet the first rollout failed. Choose a rollout plan to see why.

The second time, Kim chased people by email, then Slack, then text, then in person. Most powerful of all, she shared her own OKRs at an all-hands meeting, invited critique, and graded herself on Google's 0 to 1 scale:

Continue to build a world-class team.Recruit 10 engineers [0.8]Hire commercial sales leader [1.0]100% of candidates feel the process was well organized and professional, even without an offer [0.5]
Create a healthy, productive workplace as we scale past 150 people.100% of staff complete the review/feedback cycle [1.0]100% score their Q3 OKRs within the first week of Q4 [0.4]
Sometimes private is rightWhen hiring a sensitive new VP role, Kim set a private OKR that only she and her cofounder could see. It still deepened her commitment and got the search moving. And her cofounder's advice: you won't get it right the first, second or third time. Persevere and make it your own.
