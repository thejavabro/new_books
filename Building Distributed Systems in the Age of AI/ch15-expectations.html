Changing and Increasing Expectations

Users increasingly expect to ask rather than navigate. When the interface dissolves, identity and authorization have to travel with the agent acting on the user's behalf.

1

When the result is all that matters

Interfaces used to be the only bridge from intention to action: a website to shop, an app to check a balance. More and more of those actions can now be delegated. Not all of them: gaming, drawing, chatting with friends are activities where the process is the point. Booking a table, comparing insurance, scheduling a meeting are chores; people want them done. It also depends on the moment: the same person may browse a clothing catalog for fun one day and want one specific item fast the next. Set the dials.

Two paths, one productA system often has to serve both modes at once: filters and product pages for browsers, MCP, APIs and chat for delegators, and sometimes a mix (delegate the research, keep the final choice). These must not become two products with different rules. They share the same domain capabilities, permission checks, audit model and business constraints.
2

Start from where the user is

"As a user, I want to find a product that meets my criteria so I can buy it." Teams usually solve this assuming the user is already on the website, which yields a search box and filters. A modern team first asks where is the user? Each context brings constraints: little attention, one free hand, people nearby, noise, no patience for long forms. Pick a persona.

Generations differ too. For people who grew up with web pages and email, "the internet" meant websites; an operating system was even built around the idea that a browser is all you need. Apps then became the new tabs, and now users expect apps to work together: a ticket mention rendered inline in chat, a restaurant picked in a messenger sent straight to navigation. Ecosystems already do some of this without AI (flight emails becoming calendar events, cross-app shortcuts). AI widens it towards an old mantra, "the best interface is no interface": the expectation is fewer interfaces, not better ones.

One capability, any surface

The shared implication: serve users without assumptions about the interface. A browser request, a voice command, an MCP call and a partner API must all reach the same capability. Separating business logic from presentation is old advice, but now it needs at least three layers: invokers (infrastructure) expose use cases (application), which compose business capabilities (domain). Different personas and journeys may still need distinct use cases, even though use cases themselves don't care who invokes them. And each channel needs its own policy.

5

Identity without interfaces

Login gives a token; requests carry it; policy checks it. Consent-based delegation (OAuth and friends) lets an app act on a user's resources. But what if the user only ever talks to a voice assistant that calls your API, or an agent submits a form at the city office for them? Two intertwined problems appear.

Agents in the identity modelAn agent is a third party with its own identity and trust level. OAuth clients are usually pre-registered; dynamic registration exists but moves the problem to "should I trust this unknown self-registered client?" Blanket consent ("use my card") fails: the user wants to approve amount, merchant and goods. In multi-hop chains, OAuth's two-party model can record "A acting for B for C" only as information, not for access decisions.
Cross-boundary authorizationAn older enterprise pain (federated SSO, SAML bridges, token exchange) that now scales to everyday users. Each extra integration adds a trust boundary between identity providers. And without a browser there are no redirects, login pages or cookies: how does a voice assistant prove to your bank that it's really you, within what you authorized?

Decouple internal identity from how people log in

Whatever standard wins, one principle holds: backend services should not know or care whether a caller used a social login, a corporate provider, a voice biometric, an API key or an agent's delegation token. An authentication gateway validates them all and passes on one internal identity, so a new channel or agent protocol changes the gateway, not every service. Pick a way in.


  

  

  
What you can do todayCarry identity context and intent through every action. Log the full delegation chain at every service boundary. Keep internal authorization independent of any one external protocol. When a standard settles, adopt it without a rewrite.

What remains for engineers: the hard part

  
Many high-value products have been built at least once, and AI has learned from decades of that software, so simple things are cheap now. What's left is hard, often hidden behind a casual sentence. Grow one: "Plan my vacation considering my budget, work schedule and preferences."

Simplifying such an architecture would mean simplifying what it can do; remove a layer and you remove a capability. No one-shot vibe-coding session delivers orchestration, integration, resilience, data, intelligence and observability woven together. The problems that need a professional are now at the system level. The bar has risen, and for those who clear it, the book ends, the work has never been more interesting.
