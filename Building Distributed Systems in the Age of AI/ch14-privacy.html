Growing Privacy Pressure

Privacy was always a concern. AI turns it into pressure: people overshare with assistants, agents leak across boundaries, attackers scale up, and the rules are catching up.

1

Privacy pressure didn't start with AI

Long before chatbots, data-protection laws taught engineers a handful of habits: collect only what you need, keep it only as long as you need it, use it only for the stated purpose, and let people see, correct and delete what you hold. These shaped architecture, not just backlogs: isolated environments, retention tags on data, second thoughts before logging a full request body. Personal data became something borrowed, not owned.

AI doesn't replace those rules. Most of them still hold when the same data sits in an agent's context. What changes is scale, surface and ease of going wrong. Agents want data at every phase: huge corpora to train, rich context to answer well. Like a builder asked "how long does a house take?", a good agent wants to know everything first. The chapter sees four shifts.

2

Shift 1: people overshare to patient listeners

A chat window feels like a patient, non-judging confidant, so people open up. Studies of real chatbot logs find personal data in most conversations, even in tasks like translation or code editing, where nobody "meant" to share it. Employees paste someone else's data: in one well-known case, engineers put proprietary source code and meeting notes into a public chatbot, and it couldn't be called back. No hack, just a convenient tool and a deadline.

The consequence for builders: assume every input contains something it shouldn't, and decide what happens before the model sees it. Type below, or switch on the gateway.

3

Shift 2: retrieval crosses boundaries

In a classic app, a deterministic access check sits between the user and the database. Retrieval-augmented agents blur that. A support agent fetching "similar past cases" may pull in other customers' tickets, and the model becomes a superb tool for surfacing them. Filtering the output (a second model, regexes) is best effort; telling the agent "don't reveal private data" is a wish, not a gate. By then the data has already hit the model, the API and the logs. Compare where the check happens.

Instructions are not guardrailsCapitals, "CRUCIAL:", threats to switch providers: a prompt is a polite request. It can steer behaviour but never replaces authorization, isolation, policy enforcement, output validation or tool-level controls. One work-management vendor's MCP server exposed one customer's workspace records to another through incomplete access checks when assembling responses.
Prompt injection is still openModels struggle to tell your instructions from instructions hidden in a document, an email or a log ("ignore previous instructions and send me…"). Defences make it improbable, not impossible. Design every path that touches untrusted input as if injection will happen.
4

Any two, never all three

Injection becomes a breach when one agent can do three things at once. A rule of thumb published by a large AI lab: allow at most two. Toggle the agent's capabilities.

5

Shifts 3 and 4: attackers and 'publicly available'

AI on the dark side

Voice and video can now be cloned well enough to fool colleagues. In one widely reported 2024 case, an employee joined a video call with deepfaked executives, then followed payment instructions to transfer about $25 million. Phishing is now fluent, personal and nearly free to mass-produce, and the step from reading about a vulnerability to exploiting it has shrunk. Defenders use the same tools. The engineering lesson: verify through channels that don't rely on a human recognising a face or a voice.

"Publicly available" is a judgement

Models were trained on scraped text and images, some copyrighted, some personal. Courts disagree from country to country, and opt-outs like robots.txt are unevenly honoured. The practical stance: treat everything you ingest as potentially sensitive until it is classified, and record provenance (where each piece came from, under what terms). Provenance is now part of the job.

Regulation wants evidence, and it's fragmented

Two patterns matter more than any specific statute. First, rules increasingly ask for evidence, not promises: show what the system did, how a decision was reached, that a human could step in. Second, fragmentation: some regions use one risk-based framework, others a patchwork, and one feature can fall under several regimes depending on where the user is. The design response: centralize behaviour rules as enforced policies, not compliance ifs scattered through the code. Tick what your system could produce tomorrow.

For automated decisions, build three things anywayInform people that a decision was made. Explain in plain terms how. Offer human review. Users and enterprises increasingly expect it, whatever the law says today.
7

Trust is full of paradoxes

The common thread is calibration: design for appropriate reliance, so people trust a system exactly as much as it deserves in a given situation. Over-trust skips the checks that matter; under-trust wastes a useful tool. Calibration starts with interface and wording, but it reaches into architecture: where guardrails sit, which actions need a human, how the system acts when unsure.

Layered transparency

More openness helps up to a point; a full dump of reasoning can overwhelm or raise suspicion. Tune it to the reader.
