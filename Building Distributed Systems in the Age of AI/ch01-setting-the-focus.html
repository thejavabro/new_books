Setting the Focus on the Right Things

Architecture is an investment in tomorrow. The ground has shifted again: cheap cloud, plug-in APIs and large language models have raised every expectation at once, and that changes what deserves an architect's attention.

1

Architecture is a bet on tomorrow

Today's load can nearly always be served by something simpler and cheaper than what an architect draws. The queues, load balancers and batched writes are there for tomorrow: back pressure that hasn't arrived, traffic that hasn't spiked. That makes architecture an investment, and like any investment it has to be justified to the people paying for it.

The author's own story makes the point. Around 2010 he argued for an abstraction layer in front of the database so it could be swapped. A senior colleague asked why anyone would ever swap MySQL. In big monoliths full of hand-tuned queries and stored procedures, nobody did. He was right in theory and wrong in practice.

Would you ever swap the database?

The lessonAn abstraction's value depends on how often the thing behind it actually changes. Microservices made change frequent, so abstraction and anti-corruption layers moved from academic to essential. Something similar is happening again with AI.
2

What shifted, and who expects what

There is no single start date for the current shift. It is the sum of 4G and 5G networks, powerful phones and laptops doing edge computing, elastic cloud platforms, plug-and-play APIs for payments or messaging, backend-as-a-service, and finally large language models. Together they raised expectations from three directions at once. Tap each group.

And all three want the system to be consistent, available and reliable at the same time, which sounds like it breaks the CAP theorem. Try it.

Why there's still hopeMost products are fine with eventual consistency, and the business meaning of "available" (an uptime SLA) is far weaker than the theorem's strict version, where every request gets a non-error answer even during a network partition. That gap is the room architects work in, though even eventual consistency plus high availability is hard to get right.

Don't start with the monolith

The familiar advice is to start with a monolith and split it when scaling demands it. The author disagrees. Growth usually arrives in "wartime": value is proven, competitors are close, and every engineer is needed for features and expansion. That is exactly when nobody can afford a rewrite, and the monolith quietly becomes a big ball of mud. Drag the slider to see why waiting is costly.

And beyond microservicesMany applications fit serverless even better: small functions triggered by events, or workflow and data platforms that offer serverless-like abstractions and increasingly bake AI into their workflows. The author still says "it depends". If your case is different after reading the book, trust that feeling.
