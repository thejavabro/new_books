A Full-Stack Team for Distributed Systems

AI makes everyone look full-stack and makes coding cheap. It also breaks estimates, blurs ownership and puts the bottleneck back on people. Teams have to adapt on purpose.

Are we all full-stack now?

"Full-stack" once meant someone who could build a non-trivial app end to end and reason about latency and data flow across layers. Then it inflated into job ads demanding mastery of everything. With coding agents, a backend engineer can scaffold a working frontend in an afternoon, and a frontend developer can wire up a database and an API. So yes, sort of. But the systems grew too, and quality limps wherever real expertise is missing. Producing working output is not the same as owning a production system. Which of these can an agent "scaffold" versus which need an owner's expertise?

Scaffold or own?

The shape of a skill set

A full-stack AI teamNot just full-stack engineers: every role (product owner, UX, engineering, QA) uses AI, and the knowledge is shared rather than locked in heads. That needs shared agreements: approved tools, trusted context sources, how generated work is reviewed, how assumptions are written down. Ideas, the thinking itself, remain human; and deep spikes (consensus, partitions, performance under real load, security, regulated domains) are where a plausible model answer can be dangerously wrong. Think of a generated retry loop that triggers a thundering herd.
2

Faster engines, same traffic jam

Giving a team faster code generation doesn't automatically make it deliver faster: a faster car on a jammed highway is still stuck. The studies the book cites are mixed, and all are correlational or narrow. Note the gap between feeling and measurement.

3

Broken estimations

Story points blend complexity, effort and uncertainty. AI cuts effort sharply and often uncertainty too (models know many APIs and digest docs fast). But it adds a new risk: a pattern that doesn't fit, a senior review that demands a redesign, outdated code that becomes debt. The average may drop while the spread widens. Set a task's profile.

Toy model. Its point: AI shifts the mean down and fattens the tail, so velocity-based planning gets shakier. Watch cycle time, review load, escaped defects, rework rate and deployment confidence instead, and talk to engineers more.

4

When agents work while you sleep

Autonomous agents can work 24/7 on well-described issues. But each finished task lands on someone: a reviewer, a tester, a product manager who must plan the release. In Scrum it looks like scope creep; in Kanban it breaks the work-in-progress limit. Let the background agent run.

WIP limit on Review: 3

What gets lostDelivery used to come with learning. Reviewing an agent's plausible solution, an engineer checks safety and correctness, but rarely steps back to ask: does it fit the architecture? Is there a generic solution to a whole class of problems? Should it exist at all? Speed goes up, depth of team expertise goes down.
5

Commitment can't be automated

An AI note-taker summarizes the retrospective, extracts action items, files tickets and pings owners. Convenient, until the team stops formulating outcomes itself. Summarizing together is when someone says "we didn't decide X, we were going to try Y", or notices an important topic was skipped. Assigning an item in the room makes people check availability and whether it solves the problem. Sort the jobs.

Agent or team?

6

The king is dead, long live the king

Framework bodies mostly treat AI as a tool inside unchanged processes; some companies have cut agile roles and treat ceremonies as theatre. The author argues agile principles matter more than ever (short feedback loops, continuous improvement, responding to change), while rigid rituals (hour-planned sprints, velocity charts, long point debates) age badly. Teams shrink to outcome-focused squads of three or four; the organization then needs a light process that gives direction, not walking instructions.

Teams and alignment

Patterns are observed, not inventedSpec-driven tools, vibe coding, plan-act-verify loops mostly repackage Extreme Programming and Lean ideas. Scrum and Kanban grew from years of real teams; agent-era patterns need the same time. Meanwhile: inspect and adapt.
7

Agents on the team: the octopus

Somebody configured every agent trigger, so somebody delegated, and accountability stays with them. Yet bot identities on pull requests hide the sponsor, the prompt and the context; the runtime is gone, only a diff remains. "The agent made a mistake" is the agentic passive voice. An engineer with agents is like an octopus: semi-independent arms, one accountable brain. Make this PR traceable.

Software ≠ product5–10× gains happen on well-scoped tasks in tidy codebases, then the backlog runs dry. Products need market sense, risk judgement, ambition, and saying no. Apply AI not to engineering but to engineering systems; automating people's work mostly makes them less happy.
PresenceAgents don't attend refinements or hear hallway talk, so tacit knowledge doesn't reach them. Socialize them or treat them as remote colleagues: whatever the team learns gets written where humans and agents can both find it (instruction files, skills, MCP integrations), and maintained like living documentation.
