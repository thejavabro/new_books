Introduction

Latency is the delay between a cause and the effect you observe. Before optimizing it, you need a feel for its scale, from a third of a nanosecond to a transatlantic round trip, and for what it trades against.

1

Latency: from a cause to its effect

The book's working definition is short: latency is the time between a cause and the effect you observe. That sounds obvious, but it forces two decisions every time you measure: which cause and which effect. Pressing Enter in a browser and seeing a page is one latency. A packet hitting the network card and becoming readable by your program is another. Both are "latency", and they differ by six orders of magnitude.

The author's favourite example is a smart light bulb. Tap "on" in the phone app and there is a visible pause, because the command hops over Wi‑Fi to a hub and then over a low-power radio to the bulb. Step through the path.

Latency vs. response timeSome authors split response time into service time (doing the work) and wait time (queuing, and only that part is "latent"). Real systems queue in so many places that the split is hard to draw, so the book uses latency in the broad sense: request latency is the whole response time.

The latency constants worth memorising

Back-of-the-envelope estimates need a ladder of rough numbers. Each rung is about ten times slower than the one above it. A CPU cycle is a fraction of a nanosecond; a New York to London round trip is tens of milliseconds, about two hundred million cycles. Physics sets the floor

No optimization beats the speed of light, about 300,000 km per second in vacuum and roughly a third slower in optical fibre. Grace Hopper famously handed out pieces of wire about 30 cm long: the distance a signal can travel in one nanosecond. A microsecond is a coil of wire about 300 m long. 4

Why latency matters

Three reasons come up again and again: user experience, real-time deadlines, and efficiency. For people, a rough rule of thumb is 0.1 s feels like no delay, 1 s is noticeable but still feels responsive, and 10 s feels slow no matter what. Try it: press a button and the "light" turns on after the chosen delay.

Pick a delay, then watch the bulb.

Companies that ran A/B tests report that slower pages convert and engage less. Industry reports in the book's survey put numbers on it, such as a few percent fewer conversions per extra 100 ms. When a task truly takes 10 s or more, stop shaving milliseconds and give feedback instead: a progress bar, or streaming the first tokens of an LLM answer as soon as they exist.

5

What latency is not: bandwidth and throughput

Bandwidth is the capacity of a channel, how many bits it can move per second. Throughput is how much actually gets through, and bandwidth caps it. The key asymmetry: you can always buy more bandwidth by adding links, but you cannot buy your way out of a long link. Model a transfer as a fixed latency plus size divided by bandwidth, and see which part dominates.

6

The laundry trade-off: latency vs. throughput

Washing takes 30 minutes, drying 60. Do one load at a time and each load is done in 90 minutes. Start the next wash while the previous load dries and you finish more loads per hour, but each load now waits for the dryer, so it takes longer from dirty to dry. Pipelining wins throughput and loses latency, the same trade-off CPU pipelines make.

7

Latency vs. energy

Low latency often costs power. Busy polling keeps a core spinning so it notices new work instantly, with no wake-up delay, but it burns energy when nothing arrives. Sleeping saves power but each wake-up costs time and a burst of energy. Which is greener depends on how often work arrives.

The numbers in this toy model are made up to show the shape of the trade-off, not measured.
