Improving, Deploying, Responding

Shipping is not the finish line. Improve existing products by moving metrics rather than piling on features, roll out change without abusing users, and keep the team together right after launch.

Improving productsGentle deploymentRapid responseQuick check
1

Improving a product is not about adding features

Most product teams are feature factories: the roadmap is a list of ideas and customer demands ("buy six features and we'll sign"). Added features often make things worse. Instead, start with a clear goal and the metrics that show it. The book's example is an online insurance application where only 7% of people who start finish. Raise that to 15% and you have changed the business. New features are justified only by how much they move the metrics. Pick changes and see where completion goes.

Your tools are site analytics and testing on real users, supported by NPS, customer service, sales and win/loss analysis. Live with these numbers daily and ask what would move them. What matters isn't what one customer, a survey or a focus group says to add, but what actually moves the needle.

2

Gentle deployment: don't abuse your users

"User abuse" is shipping changes your users didn't want, in ways that hurt them. Users don't like change: they want the product to improve but not to relearn what they can already do. With online reviews and communities, a bad release now brings a fast, public backlash. Common reasons users resent a change:

No warning: it caught them by surpriseNo time to learn it now, and no way to keep the old versionThe change doesn't workIt breaks compatibility, such as with old dataIt feels gratuitousChange fatigue from recent updatesIt breaks the processes they built around you

The answer isn't to stop changing but to deploy change smartly: communicate ahead, invest heavily in QA so you never have to roll back, and contain the risk. Compare rollout strategies for a big redesign used by a million people.

Curves are illustrative. If users like you, you have a reserve of goodwill. Save it for when you really need it.

3

Rapid response: the week after launch

In many companies the team scatters the moment a product launches, just when the chance to learn and correct is highest. The book's fix is simple: extend every project with a rapid response phase starting at launch and lasting a few days to a week. Even with prototypes, validation and good QA, some issues only appear live. The question is how fast you respond.

  • Decide in advance which business metrics matter, and what counts as success or a problem.
  • Consumer services: instrument everything and watch analytics daily. Enterprise: send PM, engineers and designers to the customer site until the customer is live and referenceable.
  • The whole team (PM, designer, lead engineers, customer service, marketing) meets at least daily, prioritizes, and pushes hot fixes or extra help content.
