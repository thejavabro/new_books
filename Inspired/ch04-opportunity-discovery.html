Opportunity, Discovery and Principles

Before building anything, frame the problem. Then discover a product that is valuable, usable and feasible, and use clear, ranked principles to make the many choices along the way.

Ten questions before you build anything

New ideas come from everywhere, and too often the decision to build one rests on gut feeling, an executive decree, or a big customer offering to pay. The traditional Market Requirements Document is meant to describe the opportunity, not the solution, but it is usually slow, unread and blurred into a spec. The author's lightweight replacement is the Product Opportunity Assessment: ten questions. Its job is either to stop a bad idea cheaply, or to focus the team on what success looks like. Tap a question to see a worked answer for an invented product: a tool that helps small landlords collect rent online.

Notice what's missing: the solutionNone of the ten questions describes the product. That is deliberate. If you fuse the problem with your first solution, you will drop a good opportunity the moment that solution gets hard. The hardest question is usually number 1: most PMs answer it with a list of features instead of one clear problem.

Present the assessment to senior management and get a clear go/no-go. If the CEO says "we're doing it anyway", do the assessment regardless. There may be strategic reasons, and at least you will know what you are getting into.

2

Build new, or fix what you have?

The author doesn't use a fixed split between new products and improvements. Each is just a product opportunity, judged on its merits. But the best opportunities are often hiding in existing products that underperform, and those are frequently the easiest to fix with a bit of prototyping and testing. Companies tend to assume their product is about as good as it gets, so they spend on marketing or support instead. Try it with a sign-up funnel.

Make a friend in financeMost product managers, especially ex-engineers, only vaguely know how their product makes money: the revenue model, total cost, what a new customer costs, lifetime value, return. A friend in finance can help you (1) understand your product's economics, (2) understand your customers from transaction and billing data, and (3) build the business case. Finance people are rarely asked, and often surprisingly glad to help.
3

Discovery, then execution

A software project has two stages: figuring out what to build (discovery) and building it (execution). In discovery you welcome new ideas, talk to many users and test concepts. Once engineering starts, the mindset must switch to focus and getting it shipped. In many teams the switch never happens: the spec keeps changing ("churn"), dates slip, features get cut, quality drops. The fix is to run two releases in parallel: as soon as 1.0 goes into execution, start discovery on 2.0, so new ideas have somewhere to go.

4

Can you schedule discovery?

A familiar scene: engineers free up in four weeks, so you have "all the time you need", as long as it's four weeks. Users turn out to be lukewarm, the prototype confuses them, but time is up, so engineering spends months building exactly the unconvincing product you saw. Discovery is creative work, more like drug research than building a house: finding the market is often easy, but finding the solution is hard and unpredictable.

Management fears idle engineers. Yet nearly every company does discovery anyway, just with the whole engineering team building production releases and live customers as unwitting test subjects. Compare the two ways to learn the same lesson.

Model assumptions (illustrative): a prototype iteration is a PM, a designer and one prototyper for about a week; a production release is ten engineers for about four months. The author notes companies often need three or more releases over one to two years to get something that makes money, which is exactly what kills cash-limited startups.

5

Product principles: decide what matters, in order

Product principles are a public statement of what the team believes. They aren't features and aren't tied to one version. They speed up discovery by settling trade-offs in advance. An example from the book's spirit: a movie site that believes its community's opinions matter more than professional critics'. When a studio asks to place reviews, the principle answers. The critical part is ranking: almost every product wants to be both easy and secure, so which wins when they conflict? Resolving product arguments

Product decisions attract endless meetings, second-guessing, vetoes and executive "drive-bys". Everyone cares about the product, and many colleagues think they are more like the customer than they are. The PM usually has no authority over the team, so must persuade.
