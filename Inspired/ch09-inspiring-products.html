What Makes a Product Inspiring

Inspiring products serve emotions, not feature lists. These chapters look at Apple's layers, the trap of custom deals, reinventing old categories, and why anger and frustration predict big wins.

Lessons from Apple: three layers, each serving the next

The author is an open Apple fan and calls Steve Jobs one of the best product managers ever. He rejects the idea that Apple wins through marketing. Writing just after the first iPhone appeared, he sees three higher-order lessons, each layer serving the one outside it. Tap a ring.

Most competitors copy the functionality and miss what actually matters.

2

Beware of specials

A "special" is a big check from one customer or partner, on condition that you build exactly what they ask: seven features for an enterprise deal, or placement rules for a big ad sponsor. It's tempting, especially for a startup short of cash. But it confuses customer requirements with product requirements. Customers don't know what they need until they see it, don't know what's possible, and don't compare notes with each other. Every special also delays other work, and has to be built, tested, documented and supported forever. Accept or decline deals and watch your release speed.

3

The next big thing is usually an old thing done much better

Companies often believe they must create a whole new market. Usually the winner is a new version of something old, so much better, faster or cheaper that it redefines the category. Web search looked mature, with dozens of engines, until one gave consistently useful results. There were over a hundred MP3 players before the iPod. Two methods: understand where current products fall short (usability-test competitors' products as well as your own), and track what has just become possible. Drag the slider to watch a "mature" category get redefined.

The end of innovation?A journalist once asked the author if any good opportunities were left. Three reasons to say yes: (1) as long as products drive you nuts, someone can do better; (2) what's possible keeps changing; (3) today's applications become tomorrow's foundations, as the browser became the base for e-commerce, calling and payments, and as social networks became platforms.
4

People buy for emotional reasons

Product people mostly come from science and business, yet the job is largely about psychology. In enterprise markets the main emotions are usually fear and greed: competitors will beat me, hackers will get in; or I'll make or save money. In consumer markets they get personal: loneliness, love or lust, greed, pride. Pick an emotion to see how it reframes a product, and who your real competitor is.

Different user types bring different emotions: a power seller on an auction site isn't the bargain hunter or the buyer who wants the thrill of winning. After a usability test, use the conversation to find out which emotion drives this person, and how well you meet it.

5

The emotional adoption curve

Geoffrey Moore's technology adoption curve runs from innovators and early adopters, across the "chasm", to the majority and laggards. In an interview in the book, Jeff Bonforte (then a Yahoo! executive) relabels each group by the emotion that drives it. His core idea: angry people decide the future of technology. Tap a segment.

Look for anger, exasperation and frustration: the industries people love to hate (telecoms, banks, airlines, health care, bureaucracy) are full of opportunity. Compare two products with similar technology but very different latent frustration.

The "freshman test"Bonforte's trick: remember your first day of high school, when you felt small, unknown and insecure. If a product touches one of those raw, universal feelings (loneliness, insecurity, fear, frustration, anger), you're on the right track. The deeper the need sits in Maslow's pyramid, the stronger the pull.
6

Usability and aesthetics: you need both

Why do companies that invest in design still ship bad sites? Visual design agencies often have beautiful sites that are hard to use; interaction design firms have usable sites that are dull. They are different disciplines, as different as product management and product marketing. Expecting one "designer" to do both usually fails; having the PM or a UI engineer do it fails worse. And visual design isn't fluff: the same functionality shown as wireframes and with good visual design gets very different reactions, because visual design creates emotion.
