Succeeding with Agile methods

Agile methods such as Scrum and XP bring real benefits, but they were designed for custom software, and product teams struggle at first to apply them. The book's top-ten list for product teams boils down to this picture: product and design work one or two sprints ahead of engineering, and sprint output collects in staging until it adds up to a release worth shipping. Step through it.

2

Why Agile needs adjusting for product software

Scrum dates from 1986 (Japan), and it grew up in custom software: one client, an internal or contracted team, a client who "knows what they want" until they see it. That world rarely had product managers, designers or dedicated QA, and its small internal systems rarely needed to scale. Agile helps it a lot: short iterations, better communication, fewer unread documents. Product software is different. Toggle to compare.

Can't the early sprints just be the prototype?For custom software that may be the best you can do. For products, no. (1) A sprint is far too slow to test an idea that will most likely be wrong; a disposable prototype takes days. (2) Engineers have more important work: building production software. (3) Once a team has put long hours into an architecture, changing direction is hard, however Agile they are. When choosing an Agile coach, make sure they understand product software.
3

Succeeding with Waterfall

Under many names (SDLC, phase-gate, stage review, milestone-based), Waterfall is still the most common process: phases in order, each ending with a review and sign-off. Managers like its apparent predictability and its documents, though you can't run a document to see if it works. The product manager's problem is that validation comes too late, and changes are costly. Drag the moment when real users first see the product.

If you're stuck with Waterfall: prototype and test with real users, and have engineering settle feasibility risks, before design and implementation begin. When change is needed later, count the cost of the follow-up release you'll need if you postpone it. Fixing sooner is often cheaper.

4

Startups: discover first, then hire engineers

The usual startup recipe: raise seed money, hire engineers, the founder designs on the fly in stealth mode. Six months later the first demo goes badly, the burn rate is high and the money is running out. The alternative: hire a product manager, an interaction designer and a prototyper (roles can be combined), run weeks to months of fast discovery with dozens of prototype versions tested on target users, and only then hire engineers to build from a stable, living spec. Simulate the runway.

Assumptions (illustrative): engineering team burns $70K a month; the discovery trio $25K. Engineering-first needs about two big tries to find product–market fit; discovery-first finds it in about three months, then builds once in about five.

5

Five ways to innovate in a large company

Large companies become conservative because they have more to lose, but they still need innovation, and they bring enormous launch power. The biggest factors are culture and your manager. Pick a technique.

6

Getting things done in a large company

Two facts first: large organizations are risk averse by design, and they usually share resources through a matrix, so you must secure people from pools. The book's ten techniques: learn how decisions are really made (often one key person, and how they decide: demo, data or customers?), build relationships before you need them, skunk works, just get it done yourself if needed, pick your battles, build consensus before decision meetings, guard your time, share information, put your manager to work, and evangelize constantly.

Try technique 6. Six stakeholders will attend Thursday's decision meeting. You have time for some private conversations first. Once someone opposes you in public, it is hard for them to switch.

The chapter closes with a friend's line that most people complain about the dark instead of learning where the light switches are.
