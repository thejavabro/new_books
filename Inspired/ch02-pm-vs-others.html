Product Management vs. Everyone Else

Product management is often confused with the jobs next to it. Four short chapters draw the borders: what the product manager owns, and what marketing, project management, design and engineering own instead.

1

Product management is not product marketing

Industry folklore says most product releases miss their goals. The author traces much of the waste to a badly defined product manager role. His position is simple: every product needs one accountable product manager who owns the product definition (requirements plus user experience). Product marketing has a different job: telling the world about the product. He keeps meeting three broken setups. Pick one to see what goes wrong.

2

Product management is not project management

In the shipped-software world, one release followed another months or years apart, so the product and the project were the same size and a product manager could also run the schedule. Internet services broke that. They ship small, frequent releases to one shared code base, and a project usually takes longer than one release interval. Work runs in parallel and leaves on a release train. Each train carries features from many projects and needs its own strong project manager (a "conductor"). Watch the difference.

The author learned what great project management looks like from Lynn Reedy at eBay, whose process was productive, demanding and built on very strong project management. Project management is not the same as knowing Microsoft Project. He lists seven traits. Tap each.

3

Product management is not design

A good product needs a good user experience, and that needs product management and design working closely together. Many companies have no designers: the UI engineers or the product manager do what they can, or a visual "skin" is outsourced just before QA. The book names four design roles (roles, not necessarily four people). For each one, decide whether you'd outsource it, then check the book's view.

Small teams can double upOne consumer startup the author worked with had a three-person team: a product manager, an interaction designer who also ran usability tests, and a visual designer who also built prototypes. They turned out and tested many prototypes quickly. An architect still has to review the work for feasibility.
4

Product management is not engineering

The product manager is responsible for building the right product; the engineering lead for building the product right. They are peers, and neither reports to the other. Each can help the other a lot.

Engineers can help you

  1. Bring them to meet users and to prototype tests. Seeing people struggle firsthand often sparks better solutions.
  2. Brainstorm with them about what new technology now makes possible.
  3. Involve a lead engineer or architect from the start of discovery for early cost estimates and better ideas. Don't throw a finished spec over the wall.

You can help engineers

  1. Keep the focus on the minimal product, not the ultimate one.
  2. Keep "churn" (changing requirements) to a minimum once building starts.
  3. Answer their questions during implementation as fast as humanly possible.
5

“We need to stop and rewrite!”

Engineering tells you the code is a house of cards and all new features must stop. The author says this happened to eBay in 1999, to Friendster and to Netscape, and that most companies never recover. It is usually product management's fault: years of pushing for maximum features while the infrastructure decays. The cure is to pay your taxes: give engineering a fixed share of capacity, which eBay called "headroom", to spend on refactoring, re-architecture and scale. Set the share and run five years.

A toy model, not data from the book: code health decays as features pile up and recovers with headroom. When health hits the floor, features stop for a rewrite. The book's guidance is at least 20%, 30% or more if you're already in trouble. If you're in the crisis already: (1) check every line of the rewrite schedule, because rewrite estimates are wildly optimistic; (2) do it in increments while still shipping some user-visible work, even with only 25 to 50% of capacity; (3) pick those few features very carefully. eBay later rebuilt its whole site in a new language over several years while still shipping, like changing the engine in mid-flight.
