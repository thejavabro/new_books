Why Products Fail, and Who Builds Them

A technically brilliant product that nobody bought taught the author his first lesson: great engineering is wasted without something worth building. This page sets up the book and the cast of roles on a modern product team.

1

A great product nobody bought

In the mid-1980s the author was an engineer on a strong HP team. They spent more than a year making expensive specialist software run on a cheap general-purpose workstation. It had patents, top quality, translations into several languages, a trained sales force and glowing reviews. It launched, and nobody bought it.

Hunting for the root cause led to one person: the "product manager", who sat in marketing and decided what got built. The lesson is the thread through the whole book: how good your engineers are doesn't matter if they aren't given something worth building. Step through how a launch can tick every box and still fail.

Building right vs. building the right thingDoing the engineering well is necessary but not enough. Someone has to discover a product that is valuable (people want it), usable (they can work out how to use it) and feasible (it can be built). Those three words come back on nearly every page.
2

How the book is organized

When the author moved into senior management at Netscape, his days split into three areas. The book uses the same three parts, and each topic inside a part stands alone, so you can read them in any order.

Who it's for: anyone on a software product team who helps decide what gets built (product managers, founders, designers, lead engineers, project and marketing managers), on consumer or business, new or existing, Agile or Waterfall products. It is not meant for non-software products or one-off custom software projects.

4

The modern product organization

Chapter 1 defines the roles the rest of the book refers to. Companies use different titles, but the most successful ones cover all of these. Tap a role in the diagram to see what it owns. The arrows show the usual flow of work: decide what to build, design it, build it, run it, tell the world about it.

What about Agile teams?In Scrum the product manager usually acts as the Product Owner and the project manager as the ScrumMaster. The other roles stay the same. Agile teams still need to fit in user experience design and handle releases carefully, which later chapters cover.

A side note in the book: at Microsoft, the people who define products are called "program managers", because "product manager" there means product marketing. Titles vary, so look at what people actually do.

5

Right-sizing the team

To keep engineers busy with work that is worth building, a certain amount of product and design work has to happen first. That gives some rough ratios. Move the slider to size a team using the book's guidelines.

PMs per engineer:

  • One product manager for every 5 to 10 engineers.
  • One interaction designer can support about two product managers; one visual designer about four interaction designers.
  • Dedicated project managers for any significant project (more than about 5 engineers), and definitely one per release if you use the "train" model, where a release leaves every one to four weeks and unfinished features catch the next one.
