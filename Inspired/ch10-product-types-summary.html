Product Types and the Summary

Checklists for three kinds of product, followed by the author's own summary of the ten practices that matter most and the questions a strong product manager never stops asking.

1

Keys to consumer internet services

Large consumer services are a joy to build, with nothing between you and millions of users, and ideas can be tested in near real time. They are also very hard. The book's ten keys are below. Rate your own product (or one you know) on each to see where it is exposed.

2

Keys to enterprise products

The author finds fewer good products in enterprise software than anywhere else. Customers aren't satisfied; they're tired of paying for disappointing products and months of consulting just to make them work. (He counts mid-size businesses as enterprise, but treats small businesses as closer to consumers.) The same exercise with his enterprise keys:

What counts as a product, and as a solution?Software only counts as a product when people pay for it (money, ads or support), it works across many customer installations (it isn't a special), the sales channel can sell it, the company supports and improves it, and customers or partners can install and configure it. A solutions product adds: it solves a business-level problem, often for an industry, from pre-integrated components, certified with partners. A how-to guide or a set of field scripts isn't a solution. Solutions marketing speaks to business problems and industries, with live customer stories.
3

Keys to platform products

Platforms are foundation software that developers build applications on: operating systems, runtimes, web APIs, game and application platforms. They are high-leverage and hard. If it has no API, or nobody has shipped commercial products on it, it isn't a platform yet; it's an unfinished product. There are three constituencies: application providers (businesses betting on you: viability, pricing, licensing, support), developers (productivity, languages, tools) and end users (only the result counts). Rank them for prioritizing trade-offs, then see what happens.

Developers are the loudest and most relatable group, and providers write the checks, so they usually come first. The book argues that's backwards: some very successful platforms were painful for developers but won because end users loved the applications. Support is demanding too, since every customer depends on you. Delivery and customization models (embedded, hosted, co-branded and so on) are topics in their own right.

4

The ten practices that matter most

The summary chapter picks out the author's ten most important practices, each covered earlier. Rate how well your team does each one; the chart shows your profile and points you to the page to revisit.

5

The product manager's worry list

A strong product manager keeps asking herself ten questions about the product, now and in future. That's why she needs real thinking time every day, and why the job takes over your life. Draw one to think about today.

6

Where to go next

The book ends by pointing readers to the author's Silicon Valley Product Group website, with a blog, newsletter, examples of the artifacts described (opportunity assessments, principles, roadmaps, prototypes, personas, test scripts) and workshops. The acknowledgments credit colleagues from HP, Netscape, AOL and eBay. Those online examples aren't in the PDF and aren't reproduced here.
