Prototypes as the Spec

The heart of the book: replace the paper spec with a realistic prototype, design before you build, cut to the minimal product up front, and test with real users until six in a row get it.

Reinventing the specDesign before buildMinimal productValidationPrototype testingQuick check
1

The prototype is the spec

Whatever it's called (PRD, MRD, BRD, functional spec), the typical paper spec takes too long to write, is rarely read, skips the hard questions, and worst of all, its mere existence tells everyone that things are fine. The book sets out what a good spec must do. Compare the two forms.

The answer is a high-fidelity prototype: a realistic simulation of the proposed user experience, with the back end faked. The author's view grew from prototyping a few key screens to prototyping nearly everything: all pages and major use cases. Business rules, performance and supported platforms go in a supplement (a wiki was his choice at the time), plus a few key use cases. The crucial difference: you can test a prototype. A spec isn't ready for engineering until it has passed both usability and value testing.

Engineers: an unambiguous referenceQA: knows the expected behaviourMarketing, sales, support: learn it earlyExecs: demo to investors and partners
2

Design and implementation shouldn't overlap

Many things should run in parallel: requirements with design, coding with testing. One thing shouldn't: user experience design with implementation. Once code starts, big changes get harder for technical, psychological and practical reasons. Designers need to try dozens of ideas in days, and a two-to-four-week sprint is an order of magnitude too slow. Prototype code must be disposable; production code is a boat anchor. And an experience can't be designed one stub at a time. Compare the cycle times.

How many ideas you can try in a month with each feedback loop (illustrative: two ideas a day with a disposable prototype versus one per two-week sprint).

The fixRequirements and design together, then implementation and test together. Agile teams use a "sprint zero" so product and design stay a sprint or two ahead. An engineer still reviews designs from the start for feasibility and cost. If designers are about to revolt, give engineering a release cycle of back-end infrastructure work while design builds a backlog.
3

Cut features before you build, not after

The familiar movie: a spec stuffed with features marked P1, P2, P3; an engineering estimate months too long; then haggling over estimates, features and test time. What ships isn't a coherent whole. The book's alternative: (1) the PM and designer prototype the minimal functionality that meets the objectives; (2) an architect reviews from day one, so trade-offs are made jointly and the final estimate is one engineering commits to; (3) the prototype is validated with real users. After that you strip the P1/P2/P3 labels, because the spec describes one whole product. Try removing a part of a validated product.

So when a feature takes longer than estimated, the normal response is a date slip, not a feature cut: you already did the cutting. Slips should be smaller and rarer, because estimates based on a prototype are better and there is less to build. For the same reason, the PM can't keep adding requirements during the build. Most mid-build changes come from not thinking things through earlier, and the prototype surfaces those early.

4

Three tests before you hand over the spec

Product validation means getting evidence that the spec describes a winner, without building and shipping it. Teams are usually far too confident in their specs and plan to "adjust after beta", which is far too late. Prototypes are now cheap enough that the old debate between high and low fidelity no longer matters, and most managers understand a scale model isn't the house.

FeasibilityCan it be built with the technology, time and money available? Engineers and architects investigate early. Learn about dead ends before the money is spent.
UsabilityCan target users work out how to use it? Expect several iterations. Testing also reveals missing requirements, and ones you don't need.
ValueDo they care? Would they choose it, buy it, recommend it? Usually tested in the same session with the same prototype.
5

How to test a prototype yourself

The author calls testing ideas with real users probably the single most important thing a product manager does. Labs charge thousands of dollars per round, so learn to do it informally. You won't be as good as a trained usability engineer, but you'll find the serious problems.

Cut no-shows with a phone call

Prepare the tasks (focus on the main ones) and the value questions. Use the one-time chance to see how a person tackles the problem without your product: start from an empty browser. Then check whether the landing page alone tells them what you do. Afterwards, have a one-to-one conversation: how does this compare with what you use today, how likely are you to recommend it, what would you pay (even if you never charge)? Use numeric scales so you can track averages as the prototype improves. A coffee-shop table and a laptop are enough. The PM attends every test.

Practise moderating

When are you done?

You don't need to freeze the prototype for a full round. If two or three users hit the same wall, fix it now. You are done when six users in a row understand the value and complete the key tasks. Simulate a test campaign.

If you can't get people to care however you present the problem, shelve the idea. That isn't failure: you saved the cost of building and shipping a loser. Credit goes to Steve Krug's Don't Make Me Think and to Creative Good's "listening labs".
