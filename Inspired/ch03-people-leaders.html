What to look for in a product manager

CEOs often ask where to find great product managers. Often they're already in the company under another title: engineer, designer, sales engineer. The book separates traits, which are very hard to teach and should be the foundation of any search, from skills, which can be learned by anyone who has the traits. Score a candidate (real or imagined) from 1 to 5 on each.

Where to find them: the author's favourite approach is to spot people with the right traits anywhere in the company (engineering, design, customer service, professional services, sales, even the user community) and develop them with training and mentoring. MBAs can work, especially with a technical first degree, but business schools rarely teach product management.

2

Domain experience and age matter less than you think

A friend asked whether a candidate who was great at enterprise software could handle consumer internet. Four years earlier, someone had asked whether the same person, then known for infrastructure software, could handle enterprise. He'd studied finance. What made him excellent was how fast he learned new domains.

The author estimates about 80% of product management skill carries across product types, and that it takes one to three months to learn a new domain well enough to set strategy. Too much domain expertise can even hurt: experts start to believe they are the customer. Some products are exceptions (a defibrillator needs someone who knows cardiac care). In interviews, ask what a candidate had to learn for each product, how long it took and how they used it.

Age, accent, backgroundPlenty of strong product leaders are in their early twenties: anyone 25 or older has been online about as long as everyone else. Passion and intelligence don't depend on age. The author once reported to Netscape's very young co-founder and soon forgot about his age. Watch for subtle hiring bias too, such as filtering for native English when a stronger candidate speaks it well enough.
3

Leading a team of product managers

The head of product (Director or VP) holds one of the most important jobs in a tech company. It tends toward big success or big failure, with little in between. There are two responsibilities: build a strong team of product managers, and own the product strategy and portfolio (vision, principles, cross-product conflicts, the portfolio roadmap and relationships with executives, especially the CEO).

Every new product manager needs about three months of hard learning (users, technology, market) before leading a product, even an experienced one. After that, the key choice is how much to trust them. Tap each quadrant.

Measuring product managers with Net Promoter Score

The only true measure of a product manager is the success of the product, but how do you measure that? The author likes the Net Promoter Score. Ask customers how likely they are to recommend the product, from 0 to 10. Promoters (9 to 10) minus detractors (0 to 6), as percentages. It keeps the focus on happy customers, who do your marketing for free. It also separates good revenue from bad revenue: an ad deal or a "special" that hurts the experience shows up as a falling score. Drag the bars to shape your responses.

Where should product management live?Not under marketing (based on the false idea that you get products by asking customers, and it tends to merge with product marketing), and ideally not under engineering (built for execution, not discovery). The author prefers a product organization on a par with engineering and marketing, including design, led by a VP of Product with a seat on the executive team. In large companies with business units, put product and design inside the units. Still, the right people can make any structure work.
5

Tell people what, not how

General Patton's advice was to tell people what to do, not how to do it, and let them surprise you with their ingenuity. This applies twice for product managers. Customers will tell you how the product should work when you need to understand what problem it should solve. And you should tell designers and engineers what the product must do, not how to design or build it. Sort each statement.

The design version of this mistake is common because there are so few designers, and in many companies design is a service called in after the spec is done, which wastes the designer's real value early in discovery. Include designers in customer visits, persona work, brainstorming and strategy.

6

Find the smartest people in the room

Product people are in the idea business, and relying only on your own ideas limits you. The author's biggest lesson: find the smartest people in the company, wherever they are, and treat them as "deputy product managers". His examples (names changed) came from surprising places. What usually gets in the way is ego: thinking the product manager must come up with every big idea. What matters is shipping great products, whoever had the idea.

Ten ways to manage up

Especially in big companies, direction shifts weekly as managers, their managers, competitors, deals and budgets all pull. You can't remove this, only reduce it. The first technique is to measure "churn": how much of your time is lost to rework and changed plans. Tick the techniques you already use and watch your estimated forward progress.

The progress figures are illustrative only. Barksdale's line from Netscape sums up technique 8: if a decision is going to rest on opinions, the boss's opinion wins, so bring data.
