The product council: timely, definitive decisions

The author dislikes committees, but makes an exception for the product council, a concept he credits to Maynard Webb at eBay. It is a small group of senior leaders (CEO or GM, and the heads of product, design, marketing, engineering, site operations and customer service, ten people at most) who set product strategy, allocate investment and oversee the product work. They don't design products. They make go/no-go decisions at four milestones. Tap a gate.

  • Small companies need one council; big ones, one per business unit. Minor fixes take an expedited path.
  • Meet as often as the work needs: an hour a month or two hours a week. Don't let policy debates crowd out decisions.
  • Review business results three to six months after launch so the council learns which bets were good.
  • The product manager presents, after briefing each member privately beforehand.
2

When to estimate cost

Management needs a cost early; engineering can't estimate well until the solution exists. The result is either wildly wrong early numbers or sticker shock later. The book's answer: at the opportunity stage give only Small, Medium or Large. Once a validated prototype exists, engineering gives a detailed, high-confidence estimate the whole team can commit to. Move through the stages to see the uncertainty narrow.

3

Charter user programs

Launching with no reference customers is a red flag to buyers; launching with one or two suggests you built something custom. A charter user program (also called a customer advisory board) solves two problems at once: deep insight into target customers during development, and real references at launch. The goal is at least six live, happy, referenceable customers from the target market, so you recruit about eight to ten at the start.

What members get

  • Real input into a product for a problem they feel
  • Early access to relief
  • Little or no cost

What you get

  • Customers on call for questions
  • Access to their offices and users (or developers, for a platform)
  • Periodic group sessions at your office
  • Prompt deployment of test versions, with feedback
  • A public reference, if they're happy

What market research can and can't tell you

Market research tools have become much more powerful, and every product should use them. But they answer some questions and not others. Tap a question to see which tools help. The two questions at the bottom are the ones that matter most for a new product.

No winning product came from market researchThe author's examples include search engines, marketplaces, music players and social networks. Winners combine a deep understanding of user needs with an equally deep understanding of what has just become possible. Ask customers what they want and you get, at best, small improvements or a pile of patches. Research is for refining a product, not conceiving one.
"Don't talk to customers"? Leave.Some companies keep PMs away from users (sales owns the accounts, marketing owns the customer, channels sit in between). Push to change the policy; if that fails, find a company where you can do the job. Reports, surveys and sales-engineer summaries are fine inputs but no substitute. The PM should attend every user interview, site visit, usability test and charter meeting for the product, and keep the most insightful users' contact details close.
5

Personas: who is this release for, and who is it not for?

A persona is an archetype: an imaginary but very plausible user built from real interviews, capturing behaviours, attitudes and goals. The technique comes from Alan Cooper. Designers and marketers use personas in slightly different ways; the product manager should help create them early and be at the interviews. The power is in choosing: one primary persona per release. Pick one for an invented recipe app and watch the feature list sort itself.

  • Benefits: prioritization, stops the team confusing itself with customers, sorts out many user types (end users, admins, managers), explains the product to everyone, and helps hundreds of small decisions go the right way.
  • Pitfalls: making personas but not choosing a priority; building them from stereotypes instead of real users. For prototype tests, still recruit a range of users, because real usage won't only come from the primary persona.
