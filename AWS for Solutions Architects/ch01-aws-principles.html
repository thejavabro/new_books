Understanding AWS Principles and Key Characteristics

Cloud computing means renting someone else's data center by the minute. This chapter explains what that buys you: elasticity, security at scale, high availability and freedom from hardware cycles.

1

Cloud computing is renting, not buying

Cloud computing hands your hardware and much of your software infrastructure to a provider. You stop buying servers and start leasing capacity: compute, storage, databases, networking, analytics, machine learning. You pay as you go, much as a household pays for electricity instead of running its own generator. The provider spreads the cost of huge data centers over millions of customers (economies of scale), so each customer pays less than it would on its own.

With serverless services such as AWS Lambda you go further: there is no server to size at all, and you pay per function call. A bank like Capital One can focus on banking instead of running data centers.

Lock-in is a sliding scaleSome services are easy to move between clouds (Amazon RDS running MySQL holds a standard database you can export). Others, such as DynamoDB, exist only on AWS. Multi-cloud keeps options open but costs you volume discounts, depth of skills and the tight integration of a single provider's services.
2

Public versus private clouds

A private cloud serves one customer, like a company's own data center with cloud-style tooling. A public cloud is multi-tenant: many customers share the provider's hardware, kept apart by strong isolation. The book compares this to owning a house versus renting in a big apartment block: the block is cheaper per family because the shared parts are paid for once.

Isolation when you need itPublic clouds still offer isolated options: AWS GovCloud (US) regions for regulated US government workloads, dedicated instances and dedicated hosts that run only your workloads, and AWS Outposts racks inside your own building. Dedicated options cost noticeably more than ordinary on-demand instances.

5

Scalability and elasticity

Scalability is the ability to keep working correctly as load grows. Elasticity is the ability to grow and shrink automatically with demand, and stop paying for what you shrink. On-premises you must buy for the peak and leave it idle the rest of the week. Move the peak and see the difference.

The book's examples: an online storefront with weekly and holiday peaks, batch jobs whose servers shut down when the job ends, developer desktops (Amazon WorkSpaces) stopped at night and on weekends for roughly 70% savings, and object storage that simply grows with your files.

6

Scaling up versus scaling out

Two ways to add capacity. Scaling up (vertical) means a bigger machine: more CPU, memory or network throughput. Scaling out (horizontal) means more machines sharing the load. Scaling out suits multi-tier designs, because you can grow just the busy tier. You can do both at once.

7

Availability, measured

Availability is the share of time a system can be used: uptime ÷ (uptime + downtime). The book's example: 732 hours up and 12 hours down in a month gives 732 ÷ 744 ≈ 98.38%. High availability is not the same as fault tolerance: a highly available system may blip briefly during a failure; a fault-tolerant one keeps going with no interruption, at a higher price.

On-premises, removing single points of failure means buying standby servers that may never be used. In AWS you spread instances and data across Availability Zones and Regions and pay for spare capacity only when you start it. Data centers also carry their own UPSes and backup generators.
