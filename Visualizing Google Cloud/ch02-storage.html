Storage

Every app needs somewhere to keep its bytes. Google Cloud gives you three shapes of storage, objects, blocks and files, and a set of knobs for cost, location and durability.

1

Objects, blocks and files

Cloud storage promises a few basics: data is encrypted at rest and in transit, kept durable and available, scales without you buying disks, and supports compliance and business continuity. Under those promises sit three very different shapes of storage. Pick one to see how data is addressed.

2

Cloud Storage classes: pay for how often you look

Cloud Storage keeps objects in buckets, which belong to a project inside an organization. Every object has an ID, metadata, attributes and the data itself, and one API reads all classes with the same millisecond latency. The four classes differ in price: the colder the class, the cheaper it is to store a byte and the more it costs to read it back, with a minimum storage period.

Where the bucket lives: region, dual-region, multi-region

The location decides how far apart the copies of your data sit. Every location can hold every class.

4

Let objects cool down on their own

Data is usually hot when it's new and cold when it's old. Object Lifecycle Management moves objects to cheaper classes, or deletes them, when they reach an age or match other rules. Object versioning keeps older copies so an accidental delete or overwrite can be undone. All data is encrypted by default, and you can supply your own keys.

5

Getting data into Google Cloud

People move data for migrations, ML training sets, backups and archives. The book lists four routes. The deciding factors are how much data and how much spare bandwidth you have. Try the calculator: at 100 Mbps, a petabyte takes years online but about forty days by appliance.

6

Persistent Disk and Local SSD: disks for VMs

A VM needs a disk that survives a restart. Persistent Disk is network-attached block storage: it lives apart from the VM, can be resized on the fly, snapshotted incrementally and attached to several VMs. Local SSD is physically attached to the host: much lower latency, but ephemeral.

7

Filestore: a shared drive for many machines

Filestore is managed NFS. Mount the same share on many Compute Engine VMs or GKE Pods (or anything that speaks NFS), and they all see the same files with low latency. It is the easy path for applications that expect a filesystem, so you can migrate them without a rewrite. Basic tiers suit general use such as dev and test; High Scale uses a scale-out design for performance-critical work spread over many clients.
