Networking Foundations

Under every Google Cloud project sits a global private network. This page covers how it is laid out, how you get onto it, and how you carve it into your own virtual networks.

Regions, zones and the private backbone

Google Cloud is divided into regions (geographic areas where VM-to-VM round trips are typically under 1 ms) and zones inside them, each an isolated failure domain. When the book was written there were 29 regions, 88 zones and 146 network edge locations; the number grows every year. All of it rides on the same network that serves Search, Maps, Gmail and YouTube, built from three layers:

Two pieces of software make it programmable: Jupiter, the data center fabric with enormous bisection bandwidth, and Andromeda, the software-defined networking layer that turns that hardware into your private virtual networks. The book groups the networking services into five jobs: connect, secure, scale, optimize and modernize. This page covers connecting and the virtual network itself; the next page covers the rest.

2

Premium or Standard tier?

Google Cloud was the first major public cloud to let you choose how traffic travels between users and your resources. Premium Tier (the default) brings traffic onto Google's network at the point of presence nearest the user and keeps it there; it's required for global external IPs and global load balancers. Standard Tier hands traffic to the public internet near your resource's region: cheaper, comparable to other clouds, and fine for single-region apps that aren't latency sensitive. You choose per resource, so you can mix.

3

Connecting your own network

Several ways connect an on-premises network or another cloud to your VPC. The right one depends on bandwidth, whether you can reach a Google colocation facility, whether you need encryption over the internet, and whether you need Google Cloud at all or only Google Workspace and public APIs.

4

VPC: your private network, global by default

A VPC network is a virtual version of a physical network, logically isolated from everyone else's. Unusually, a Google Cloud VPC is global: one network can have subnets in many regions, and VMs in different regions talk over internal IPs without VPNs. VPCs carry traffic for Compute Engine, GKE and other VM-based services, provide internal load balancing, connect to on-premises through VPN and Interconnect, and every VPC is a distributed firewall: rules are defined for the network but enforced at each VM, even between VMs in the same subnet.

5

Sharing networks across projects

Shared VPC

A central host project owns the network; service projects attach to it and create VMs in its subnets. A network team keeps control of subnets, routes and firewalls, while app teams keep their own projects, billing and IAM. Everyone talks over internal IPs.

VPC Network Peering

Connects two separate VPCs, even across projects or organizations, over internal IPs. Traffic stays on Google's network: lower latency, better security and lower egress cost than external IPs or VPNs. Each side administers its own network. Peering is not transitive.

Packet Mirroring clones the traffic (headers and payloads) of chosen VMs and sends it to a collector, an instance group behind an internal load balancer, for intrusion detection or troubleshooting. Mirroring happens on the VMs, so it uses their bandwidth.

It's always DNS

The book opens this section with the sysadmin haiku: it's not DNS, there's no way it's DNS, it was DNS. DNS turns names into addresses, and every request starts there. Step through a lookup.

Cloud DNS is Google's managed authoritative DNS (not to be confused with 8.8.8.8, Google Public DNS, which is a recursive resolver). It serves zones from anycast name servers worldwide with a 100% availability SLA and scales to millions of records.
