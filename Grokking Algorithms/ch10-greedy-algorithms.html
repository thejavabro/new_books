Greedy Algorithms

Greedy algorithms take the best-looking option at every step and never look back. Sometimes that is perfect, sometimes it is merely close, and for some problems close is the best anyone can do fast.

The classroom scheduling problem

You have one classroom and a list of classes, some overlapping. You want to hold as many classes as possible. It sounds hard, but the solution is almost too simple: pick the class that ends soonest; then, among classes that start after it, again pick the one that ends soonest; repeat. That's a greedy algorithm: at each step take the locally best move. Here, the locally best choices add up to the globally best answer.

When greedy isn't perfect: the knapsack

A thief with a 35 lb knapsack wants the most valuable haul. Greedy says: grab the most expensive item that fits, then the next. Try it, then try to beat it.

Greedy grabs the $3,000 stereo, and nothing else fits. The laptop and guitar together are worth $3,500. Greedy isn't optimal here, but it's pretty close, and sometimes "pretty good" is good enough. Chapter 11 solves the knapsack exactly with dynamic programming.

3

The set-covering problem

You're launching a radio show and want to reach listeners in every state on your list, using as few stations as possible. Each station covers a set of states, and the sets overlap. The exact method is to try every subset of stations and pick the smallest one that covers everything. With n stations there are 2n subsets: O(2n).

The greedy approximation: repeatedly pick the station that covers the most states not yet covered (overlap with covered states is fine). Stop when every state is covered. That runs in about O(n²).

while states_needed:
    best, covered = None, set()
    for station, states in stations.items():
        hits = states_needed & states        # set intersection
        if len(hits) > len(covered):
            best, covered = station, hits
    states_needed -= covered                   # set difference
    final_stations.add(best)

Spotting a likely NP-hard problemWarning signs: it's fast with a few items but blows up with more; you must consider "all combinations" or "every order"; it involves a sequence (like a route) or a set (like stations) and has no obvious shortcut; or it looks like set covering or the salesperson in disguise.
