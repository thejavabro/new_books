Appendix A: how tall can an AVL tree get?

Chapter 8 said AVL trees give O(log n) search. True, but there's a subtlety. A perfectly balanced tree fills every level before starting the next, so each level doubles the nodes: heights 1, 2, 3 hold 3, 7, 15 nodes. An AVL tree allows the two sides of any node to differ by one level, so it can have holes, and the same number of nodes may need an extra level. Both are O(log n), but the perfect tree's log is base 2, while the AVL tree's worst case is log base φ (the golden ratio, about 1.618).

The sparsest legal AVL tree of this heightdashed = holes

Why φ? The thinnest AVL tree of height h is a root plus the thinnest trees of heights h−1 and h−2, so the node counts grow like the Fibonacci numbers, whose growth rate is φ. Slightly slower growth means slightly taller trees: close to perfect, but not identical.

Appendix B: decision problems

Set cover and the traveling salesperson have something in common: nobody knows how to solve them without, in effect, checking every possibility. They are NP-hard. The vocabulary (P, NP, NP-hard, NP-complete) is famously confusing, so here's the roadmap:

Decision problems→SAT→P vs. NP→Reductions→NP-hard→NP-complete

A decision problem has a yes-or-no answer. "Find the shortest route" is an optimization problem; "Is there a route of length 5 or less?" is its decision version. NP-complete problems are always decision problems, so from here on "traveling salesperson" means that yes/no version.

The satisfiability problem (SAT)

Four friends order one pizza. One insists on pepperoni. One wants pepperoni or sausage. One wants olives or onions. One refuses onions. Can a single pizza satisfy everybody? Written as a boolean formula:

(pepperoni) and (pepperoni or sausage) and (olives or onions) and (not onions)

SAT asks: is there some way to set the variables so the whole formula is true? It was the first problem proven NP-complete, in 1971. Pick toppings:

Hard to solve, quick to verify

Often checking an answer is far easier than finding one. Write a palindrome containing "cat" and "car"? That takes a while. Check that "Was it a car or a cat I saw?" reads the same both ways? Seconds. SAT is like that: given an assignment, you just evaluate the formula.

NP is the class of problems whose solutions can be verified in polynomial time (run time no worse than a polynomial like n² or n³; 2n and n! are not polynomials). They may or may not be quick to solve.

P is the class of problems that can be solved (and so also verified) in polynomial time. P sits inside NP.

The famous P vs. NP question: is every problem that's quick to verify also quick to solve? If so, P = NP. Nobody knows.

The picture assumes P ≠ NP, which most researchers believe but nobody has proven.

Reductions, NP-hard and NP-complete

Faced with a hard problem, change it into one you know how to solve. Asked to multiply binary 101 × 110, most people convert to decimal (5 × 6 = 30) and convert back (11110). That's a reduction.

×

The punchline: since every NP problem reduces to any NP-hard problem in polynomial time, a fast algorithm for any one NP-hard problem would give a fast algorithm for every problem in NP. That's why chapter 10 recommends approximation algorithms instead.
