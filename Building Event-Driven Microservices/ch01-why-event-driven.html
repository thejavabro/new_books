Why Event-Driven Microservices

Most organizations bolt data access onto their services as an afterthought. Event-driven microservices fix that by making a durable stream of business facts the way data moves between teams.

1

An event-driven microservice, in one picture

A microservice is small and purpose-built for one business job: small enough to fit in one person's head and owned by a small team. An event-driven microservice is one whose work is driven by a loop of consuming and producing events. The key difference from older message-passing systems: events are not destroyed when someone reads them. They stay in the stream for any other consumer that needs them later.

Synchronous means the caller waits for an answer. Asynchronous means it carries on with other work. This book's asynchronous tool of choice is the event stream.

2

Domains, subdomains and bounded contexts

Domain-driven design gives the vocabulary for drawing service boundaries. A domain is the whole problem space a business works in. A subdomain is one part of it, often mirroring the org chart. A domain model is the business's own representation of that part of reality, with its own terms. A bounded context is the logical boundary around one subdomain's inputs, outputs, events, rules and data models.

There is no fixed countA bounded context may need one microservice or several, each with different technology. The only rule is that the services inside fulfil that context's purpose.
3

Align on business needs, not technical layers

Teams that own a whole business capability, code and data together, can change it without asking anyone. Teams organized by technical layer (one owns the app, another the database) create explicit dependencies between teams and hidden ones between business requirements. A small change ripples across both.

The trade-offBusiness alignment can mean some repeated code and similar data in several services. That repetition is usually far cheaper than the tight coupling you get when one service tries to serve everybody's data needs. Shared technical concerns such as authentication, logging and monitoring are the exception: don't rebuild those per service.
4

Three communication structures

Teams, systems and people communicate to get work done. The book calls the resulting web of dependencies a communication structure, and distinguishes three.

5

Conway's law, and the carrot and the stick

Conway observed in 1968 that organizations produce designs that copy their own communication structures. Teams build services along team lines, and those services reach easily only into their own bounded context. But business concepts cross every boundary, so services keep needing data that lives elsewhere. A weak data communication structure shapes design in two ways:

The stickGetting shared domain data out of another system is so hard that people avoid creating new, separate products at all.
The carrotThe data you already have is right there, with deployment and monitoring already set up. So new features get stuffed into the existing service, and monoliths grow.

7

Why point-to-point gets out of hand

With request-response, every service that needs data calls the service that owns it. Connections multiply as the company grows. With event streams, each producer writes once and each consumer reads what it needs, so you add one stream per producer and one subscription per need, with no calls into the producer at all.

What events give you, in the book's terms: a durable, canonical, replayable record; producers responsible only for writing good events, not for serving everyone's queries; consumers free to build their own models at their own pace; independence from any one implementation (swap the producer, consumers never notice); and services that map neatly to business needs. You don't have to publish everything, only what other teams need.
