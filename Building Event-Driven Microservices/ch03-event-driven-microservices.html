Events turn the wheel

An event-driven microservice is an ordinary application: compute, storage, network, source control, a build and deploy pipeline, logs and monitoring. What makes it event-driven is that events drive its business logic, the way a stream turns a mill wheel. With no events (and no timer, which is also an event), it sits idle.

Each consumer tracks its own offset, its position in each partition, so many consumers progress independently. A consumer group lets several instances act as one logical consumer. When instances join or leave, partitions are redistributed in a rebalance. Each instance receives events only from its assigned partitions.

How small should a microservice be?

  
The goal is not the highest service count; every service costs overhead. Size it so one team can own it, one developer can hold it in their head, and a newcomer can understand it in a day or two. The bounded context and the business need come first; "micro" is secondary. Which of these instincts match the book's advice?

Where services run

  
Containers, container management and infrastructure as code (think Docker, Kubernetes, Terraform) changed how applications are deployed, scaled and monitored. A service should deploy as a single unit, scale vertically (bigger instances) and horizontally (more instances). Compare the isolation options.

Paying the microservice tax

  
The microservice tax is everything you spend (money, people, opportunity) on the platform that makes microservices possible: the broker, container management, deployment pipelines, monitoring and logging, plus the learning curve. It's unavoidable. The only choice is whether it's paid once, centrally, or again by every team.

  

    
    

    

  

  

    
Small organization?A modular monolith may suit you better. Move to microservices when scaling and growth problems arrive.

    
Not all or nothingInvest incrementally. Fully managed cloud services (brokers, container platforms, monitoring, catalogs, pipelines) have cut the tax a lot since 2020. Use what you have before buying more.

Service contracts

  
Request-response services formalize their boundaries with a service contract, typically written in OpenAPI or with Swagger tooling. Event streams need something similar but different: the data contract of the next chapter. A service offering both APIs and streams needs both.
