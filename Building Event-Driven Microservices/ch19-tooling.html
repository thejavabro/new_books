Build or buy the platform?

Your tools will follow your existing cloud, languages and skills. Law and data-residency rules may force self-hosting. Otherwise, the author's view is to use fully managed services where you can and self-host only where you must. A business succeeds by serving customers, not by running platforms. The monthly bill looks higher, but it hides nothing: self-hosting adds engineering, debugging and upgrade time that never shows up on an invoice. Renegotiate, or bring pieces in-house, once you're big enough for it to pay off.

Illustrative numbers only: a managed bill of 40k per month, against self-hosted infrastructure of 15k per month plus 15k per engineer per month.

Creating streams, tagging them, capping them

Teams should create their own streams (internal ones automatically) and pick partition count, retention and replication to suit the data:

Metadata tags (kept in tools such as Apache Atlas, Amundsen or OpenMetadata) make streams searchable and governable. Only the owning team can edit them. Useful tags: owner service PII financial namespace deprecated and your own custom tags. A deprecated tag lets existing consumers keep reading while new subscriptions are blocked, and tells the owner when the last consumer has left so the stream can be deleted.

Quotas

A new consumer group replays a huge stream from the beginning. Without a quota, it can starve every other client of broker I/O.

A 25% global cap is a reasonable starting point. Allow up to 75% for surge-prone services, and consider no cap for producers fed by external sources, which would otherwise drop data or crash when throttled.

4

Permissions protect bounded contexts

ACLs assign permissions to each client one by one (Kafka and Pulsar ship with these). RBAC bundles permissions into roles, which suits large organizations and is usually a vendor feature. Either way, identify every client from day one, because adding identities later means touching every service. Toggle permissions for the pricing service and its neighbour:

Only the owning team grants access to its streams, and sensitive tags can route requests through a security review. Logging every grant and revoke as an event gives an audit trail. Streams with no consumers, and services writing streams nobody reads, show up as orphans you can retire.

Offsets, resets and lag

Owner-only, self-service controls for consumer offsets:

Application reset toolDelete the service's internal and changelog streams, wipe its state stores (including supported external ones), and reset input offsets to the start or a chosen point. Self-service, but a team can only ever reset its own services.
Lag monitoringLag is the number of events between the newest event and the last one processed. A simple rule: if lag stays above N for M minutes, double the instances. On very busy streams, compare with historical lag rather than a single reading, add hysteresis, and first check the service isn't simply failing.

Streamline service creation: one action should create the repo, wire up CI, add webhooks, assign an owner team, request input permissions, create output streams with ownership, and generate code from a template. Otherwise people copy an old service and inherit its mistakes. Expose CMS controls to teams: environment variables, target cluster, CPU, memory and disk, autoscaling rules, manual scaling.

Dependency tracking from permissions

Self-reported dependency maps always have gaps. Permissions don't: a service can't read or write without them, and they update automatically. From that graph you get data lineage (even as of a past date, by replaying the permission events), team overlays, data discovery, and a measure of how tangled your teams are. Click a service to hand it to another team, and try to cut cross-team links.

Fewer cross-team links usually means fewer dependencies, but not always: a team whose job is sourcing data will naturally have many. Check how each service's business purpose fits the team's goals.
