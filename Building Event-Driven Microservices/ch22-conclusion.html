The data communication structure

The main idea of the book: separate who owns and produces data from who reads it. Producers publish to streams and stop serving everyone else's queries. Consumers build exactly the models they need. A producer outage only delays new data; it doesn't block access. Compare:

Align services with business bounded contexts (one service for a small context, several for a large one) so they can change independently. Not every service has to be micro: there is no prize for the most services or the fewest. Do three things: publish important entities and events, treat the broker as the single source of truth, and make services easy to create.

Event design and schemas, recapped

Denormalize more than you would in a database: joins are cheap at rest but expensive in streams. Bigger events cost storage and producer effort, but consumers avoid stream joins and can use simpler frameworks. Schemas make producers and consumers face the real data, prevent misreadings and bad data, evolve safely, support field-level encryption and value constraints, generate code, and make data discoverable through the registry. Liberate the most-used, most-critical data first, weighing load on the source against staleness and exposing internal models.

Testing and deploying, recapped

Schemas make it easy to compose test inputs with exact order, timestamps, partitions and edge cases. Run brokers in-process or in containers locally, and use disposable environments filled with production copies for smoke and performance tests. Deployments must respect SLAs, state rebuilds and the load a replay puts on the broker and downstream consumers.
