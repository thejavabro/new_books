Eventual Consistency

In an event-driven system, services agree eventually rather than instantly. The question is not whether there is lag, but how you design so it does no harm.

1

It's really about convergence

The person who coined "eventual consistency" later said it should have been called eventual convergence: once every message has been delivered, all replicas agree on the stored values. Event-stream consumers read at their own pace, so at any moment two of them may disagree. Given the same events, they end up in the same place. A consumer can tell you what it has, but not what it hasn't seen yet. What it can say is which offset it has reached.

Each consumer lives in its own time bubble, set by the timestamps of the events it has consumed, not by the wall clock. Play the stream, then have consumer 2 ask consumer 1 about sunflowers.

Two root causes, which can happen together:

Consumer lagOne service is behind another in consuming, processing or storing.
Producer lagA producer's own state is current, but a bug or outage keeps it from writing to the stream. Consumers agree with each other but not with the source.

Usually neither matters: services sit at the head of their streams unless they're new or have been reset. The real trouble is mostly when one independent context queries another directly.

2

Example: assigning drivers to trucks

A delivery firm needs a driver for every truck that leaves. Compare a synchronous design with an event-driven one in which the truck service has fallen 7 hours behind.

Putting a driver and a truck into one atomic event would enforce consistency, and is worth doing when the domain allows it. Here it doesn't: some drivers have no truck and some leased trucks come without drivers. If two facts must line up exactly in time, put them in the same event.

3

When the streams themselves disagree

Even an infinitely fast consumer can't join what hasn't been produced. A flower-pot builder joins flowers with preferred soil (a foreign key). Sunflower and poppy have no soil record yet: the producer may be down, the domains may have different SLAs, or the network may be split. Your choice: wait (and perhaps emit a partial result to update later), or give up after a deadline. Giving up is final.

4

Strategies for living with it

1. Prefer state events

Delta events leave consumers to reconcile changes themselves, which gives more room for interpretation errors and so for divergence. State events are easier to apply identically.

2. Expose it in the server response

Like a booking site's "please wait to confirm" spinner, but between servers. Set the service's lag and pick a policy to see its reply.

Use offsets, not "time since the last event"

3. Communicate with events, not requests

When you need drivers and trucks together, build a service that materializes both streams and query that. Inside one service you can reason about time, lateness and consistency.

4. Prevent the failures that cause lag

Crashes, broken network, broker and storage connections will happen. Good DevOps, monitoring, headroom to scale, testing and fast fixes for stalled producers all shrink the window of inconsistency.
