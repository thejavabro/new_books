Introduction to Agents

An agent is a model that can decide what to do next and then actually do it. This chapter sorts out what counts as an agent, when you need one at all, and what to build it with.

An agent is a loop, not a single answer

The book defines agents as systems that analyze their environment, decide what to do and act, with little human help. What makes this possible now is that models no longer just write prose: they can produce a structured function call (which tool, which arguments). Wrap that in a loop and the model can look at the result and choose the next step.

Protocols such as the Model Context Protocol (tools, chapter 4) and Agent-to-Agent (chapter 8) let agents reach remote tools and other agents. The author pairs that opportunity with a warning that runs through the whole book: the more an agent can do, the more carefully it has to be designed, measured and governed.

2

Why pretrained models changed the game

Classic machine learning is bound by its dataset: most of the work is collecting and labeling examples for one narrow task. Foundation models flip this. They arrive already trained on huge corpora, so one model can be pointed at many tasks with a prompt instead of a new training run.

Classic MLOne model per task. Collect, clean and label data; train; deploy. Great at the exact task, blind outside it.

Many things are called agents

"Agent" now covers a wide range of products. The book lists seven common kinds, plus voice and video agents that are growing fast. Its own focus is the core case: language-model agents working with text and code.

Choosing a model: start simple

There are many capable models from commercial labs and open source. The book shows a general-purpose leaderboard (HELM, August 2025). The striking thing is how close the top models are.

Mean score across benchmark scenarios

The practical adviceLarge general models work out of the box and take you far. Squeezing out the last few points is rarely worth the effort unless scale or cost forces it. Later, try smaller models, fine-tuning or retrieval to cut cost and latency on well-defined tasks.
5

From waiting in line to working in parallel

Traditional software often works synchronously: do step one, wait, do step two. Agents are built to work asynchronously, juggling several tasks and reprioritizing as results arrive. Emails can arrive with draft replies already written; invoices with payment details filled in. People shift from doing tasks to managing them.

Code, workflow, RAG or agent?

The most useful decision in the chapter: an agent is not always the answer. Four factors decide: how variable the inputs are, how complex the reasoning is, what latency or compliance constraints apply, and the maintenance burden you can carry. Move the sliders for your use case.

Five principles and how to organize

The chapter names five principles for agent systems. Tap each for the failure it prevents.

Organizing teamsEarly on, let teams explore freely with different models and architectures. As patterns that work emerge, standardize on them. Avoid vendor lock-in with open standards (for example OpenAPI) and modular designs, share lessons from failures as well as successes, and keep governance light: principles over mandates. It is a cycle you revisit, not a one-time decision.
