1

The human brain and an AI agent

Classic AI follows explicit rules, generative AI recombines statistical patterns, and agentic AI turns those generators into goal-driven actors that plan and act. It is tempting to call that "thinking like a person". The chapter compares the two carefully. Pick a dimension.

2

Agency: a governed capability, not a free hand

Agency is the capacity to act with some autonomy. In an enterprise no agent acts alone, so agency also decides how responsibility is shared, how agents negotiate competing goals, and how outcomes feed shared learning. Crucially, agents may not grant each other unlimited authority. Toggle the governance layer to see what changes.

3

Designing for the user, the community and society

AI has produced breakthroughs (protein structure prediction is the chapter's example) and real harm. Responsible design means thinking beyond the individual user to the second-order effects on communities and society. Click a ring.

4

Rough-edged and sharp-edged problems

Rough-edged problems have many acceptable answers; getting 80 to 90% of the way and letting a person finish is valuable. Sharp-edged problems have one correct answer and zero tolerance; "almost right" is wrong. Today AI can reliably solve far fewer sharp-edged problems than rough ones. Sort the tasks.

Design adviceAvoid pitching an agent that "will make complex decisions perfectly, end to end". Start with rough-edged work or narrow, well-bounded sharp tasks; wrap sharp steps with deterministic checks, verification against systems of record and human approval.
5

The six core components of an agent

An agent is not a model that answers questions. It is a closed loop that senses, understands, predicts consequences, acts and learns. The chapter's running example is insurance policy issuance: a broker asks for same-day issuance with a last-minute vehicle added. Click a component.

Memory is layered

Bars show relative trust and durability. The operational ledger holds only verified commitments: policy number issued, transaction posted, who approved, with read-back confirmation.

6

Function, architecture, enablement

A thermostat already senses, decides and acts. What makes an enterprise agent powerful is the coherence of three layers: cognitive functions (the organs: perception, reasoning, memory, planning), the cognitive architecture (the nervous system that connects and sequences them) and technology enablement (models, vector stores, tools, runtimes that implement them). Not code, a model, or a clever prompt on its own.

7

Five agentic workflow patterns, working together

Like classic design patterns for object-oriented code, agentic patterns tame complexity: reflection, tool use, planning, multi-agent collaboration and human-in-the-loop. The chapter shows them combined in an RFP-response agent. Step through it.

8

Multi-agent state: write only in your jurisdiction

Many agents, one workflow. The enterprise pattern keeps one layered workflow state and each agent may write only its own section: the risk agent writes risk findings, not the issuance decision. Try some writes.

9

RAG: make the agent earn its answers

Retrieval-augmented generation fetches evidence from authoritative sources before the agent reasons. Precision matters: a policy-issuance assistant needs the exact underwriting authority rules for this product and state, not general underwriting documents. Single-agent RAG suits narrow tasks; multi-agent RAG splits retrieval across sources and adds a reconciler when evidence conflicts.
