Intelligent Agentic AI Delivery and Operations Management

An agent can look healthy on every dashboard while behaving badly. Delivery and operations have to watch behaviour, cost and responsibility, not just uptime.

1

Healthy dashboards, misbehaving agents

Delivery and operations were built on the assumption that software behaves predictably once released. Agents can be technically healthy while behaving incorrectly: choosing the wrong tool, looping, drifting, overusing fallbacks. Flip between the two views of the same afternoon.

From model deployment to agent deliveryClassic AI delivery shipped a model. Agentic delivery ships a behaviour made of a model, prompts, tools, retrieval sources, memory, policies and workflows, all version-controlled and released together. Core objectives: business value, safety, reliability, resilience and recovery, cost control, and continuous improvement.
2

Operating model: who owns what

Four roles share the work: the platform team (model gateway, orchestration runtime, shared services), AI engineering (agents, prompts, evaluations), security (identity, access, threats) and the business (outcomes, rules, acceptance). At minimum four controls need a clear owner. Match them.

Governance forums that workSmaller and more operational, not endless committees. What makes a forum effective is the clarity of decision rights behind it: who approves a new tool, a higher autonomy level, a policy change, and who is paged at 2 a.m.
3

Organizational readiness and the maturity model

Readiness is a composite, not a declaration. A company may have great infrastructure and still be unready. Rate the six dimensions the chapter uses; the result maps onto the five-level maturity model.

4

Scaling: what to centralize, what to federate

Scaling needs common patterns, reference pipelines and reusable platform services, without turning governance into a bottleneck. Governance should be proportional to risk, not applied identically to everything. Sort each capability.

Incident management for agents

Not every incident is an outage. The chapter distinguishes platform, behavioural, safety and data/context incidents, each with its own runbook and a severity level. Classify the alerts, then choose an autonomy level for the platform.

6

Capacity planning and FinOps

Treat each agent workflow as a measurable pipeline and build a resource profile: inference load, retrieval load, tool-call load and concurrency. The chapter's embedding example: 10,000 retrieval queries × 5 documents × 500 tokens = 25 million embedding tokens. Change the inputs.

Assumes 3,000 tokens per model call, 500 tokens per document and an 8-hour busy period with a 3× peak factor. Common waste: sequential calls that could be parallel, re-retrieving the same documents, and caching answers that were only valid for one user's permissions or jurisdiction.

FinOps for agentsStart with showback (make cost visible per agent, team and workflow), then move to chargeback as the program matures so cost accountability sits with the people who design the workflows. Revisit reserved-capacity commitments as usage shifts.
7

Responsible AI as architecture, not a statement

AI ethics sets the values, AI governance sets the rules and decision rights, and responsible AI architecture makes them operational inside agents, pipelines and runtime. The chapter rests it on an ethical foundation (fairness, transparency, privacy, accountability) and an operational one (safety, robustness, human oversight and control). Its governance runs from enterprise direction (board mandate, strategy, risk appetite) down to controls and monitoring, and loops back as prompts, tools, models and regulations change.
