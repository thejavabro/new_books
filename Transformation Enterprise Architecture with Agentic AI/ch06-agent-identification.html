Agent Identification: A Domain-Driven Approach

Finding the right agents is a design problem, not a naming exercise. Domain-driven design and event storming show where judgment lives and where plain services are enough.

1

From functional decomposition to decision decomposition

Microservices embody a transactional worldview: wait for a request, execute deterministic logic, return. A microservice asks "what must I execute correctly within my boundary?". An agent asks "given my objective and what I know, what should happen next?". That shifts boundaries from services to decisions, and from control flow to intent flow. Compare the chapter's compliance example.

Every agent is a service; not every service is an agentDeterministic services have no goals, no memory of intent, and give the same output for the same input. Keep them. Payments, ledgers and policy records need precision and repeatability more than autonomy. Services change through deployments; agents also change through experience and learning.

4

Microservices and agents: a partnership

They are not competing styles. Services stay the accountable owners of truth; agents own judgment. They differ in three telling ways.

Design ruleDo not make an agent the owner of truth, and do not force a microservice to do fuzzy reasoning. Choose a microservice when the capability owns authoritative state (Policy, Order, Claim, Payment, Account). Choose an agent when the output is a judgment: a risk view, a confidence score, a suggested action, an escalation recommendation.

Event storming to find agents

Event storming gets people from different roles to describe the same process on one wall, and stops the organization guessing how the business works. The chapter extends the classic steps with a hunt for reasoning and decision points. Walk through a commercial-insurance underwriting workshop.

A detective and cluesThe chapter's image: in agentic DDD the agent is the detective and value objects are clues. Clues have no identity, but they carry the meaning the detective reasons over: a loss ratio, a territory code, a driving-record score.
6

Sizing agents: between god agents and nano agents

The old microservice-sizing debate returns. Cognitive load is the scaling constraint: the concepts an agent must keep consistent, the size and variability of its context, the number of tools and the number of decisions. A well-sized agent feels like an expert who never has to ask "remind me what we decided?". Adjust the load.

7

Relationships, coalitions and the agent org chart

Agents need an organization: supervisors, peers and subordinates; delegation, escalation and veto; and for short, complex problems, coalitions and temporary swarms. A digital org chart states roles and boundaries, authority and permissions, reporting lines, KPIs and owners. Click a relationship.

8

Signals of a real agent, and agent washing

Agent washing is renaming an automation, rule engine, chatbot or pipeline as an "agent". Executives then believe they run an agentic enterprise when they do not. Score a component against the chapter's positive signals and washing symptoms.

Regulated enterprises: who is legally deciding?

An agent becomes a legal decision-maker when its output is determinative, directly changing a customer outcome. Human-in-the-loop is a spectrum, not a checkbox. Slide along it.

Audit-ready means reconstructableTurn "the AI said so" into "here is what was known, what policy was applied, what was decided, and why". Delegated authority lets an agent decide within thresholds; retained authority means it may recommend but a licensed person commits.
