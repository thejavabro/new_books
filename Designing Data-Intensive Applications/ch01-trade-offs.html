Trade-Offs in Data Systems Architecture

There is rarely one right answer in data architecture, only trade-offs. This chapter lays out the big forks in the road: operational versus analytical, cloud versus self-hosted, one machine versus many, and the law that sits over all of it.

1

Two kinds of system: operational and analytical

Inside most organizations, data lives two lives. Operational systems are the backend services and databases where data is born: a user clicks "buy", a row is inserted, a balance is updated. Analytical systems hold a read-only copy of that data, reshaped so business analysts and data scientists can ask big questions of it. The access patterns are so different that they are usually kept apart.

The operational pattern got the name OLTP (online transaction processing): lots of small, predictable reads and writes, each touching a handful of records found by key. The analytical pattern is OLAP (online analytical processing): few but heavy queries that scan millions of rows to compute counts, sums and averages.

The in-between: product analytics and HTAPSome systems run analytical-style aggregates inside a user-facing product, with low latency and real-time ingestion (think of a dashboard every customer sees). Others, called HTAP, offer both workloads behind one interface, though under the hood they usually pair an OLTP engine with a separate analytical one. Neither removes the need for a central warehouse where data from many services can be combined.
2

From many operational databases to one warehouse

A large company may run hundreds of operational systems: the website, the tills, inventory, routing, payroll. Letting analysts query them directly is a bad idea. The data is scattered in silos, OLTP schemas are awkward for analysis, heavy queries would slow down customers, and the systems may sit on networks analysts cannot reach. So data is copied into a separate data warehouse through ETL: extract, transform, load.

Data scientists often want something looser: raw files they can feed to Python, Spark or a model. A data lake is a central store of files in any format, usually on cheap object storage. It can also be a staging area on the way to the warehouse, which lets each consumer shape the raw data their own way.

3

Systems of record and derived data

A second distinction follows the flow of data. A system of record (or source of truth) holds the authoritative copy: new facts are written there first, each one once. Derived data is anything computed from it: caches, search indexes, materialized views, denormalized copies, the warehouse, even a trained model. Derived data is redundant on purpose. Lose it and you can rebuild it; that is what makes it derived.

No tool is inherently one or the other. The same database product can be your source of truth in one place and a derived cache in another. What matters is that you know which is which.

Cloud versus self-hosting

Should you run your own database, or rent it as a service? It is a classic build-versus-buy decision, and the honest answer is "it depends". Self-hosting tends to win when your team already knows the software and your load is steady. The cloud tends to win when you need something you do not know how to operate, or when load swings wildly, because you can hand back resources you are not using.

5

Cloud native: storage and compute come apart

Moving old software onto cloud virtual machines is one thing. Cloud native systems are designed around cloud services from the start. The big shift is the separation of storage and compute. In a classic server, the CPU and the disk sit in the same box, and RAID protects against a failed disk. In the cloud, a local disk vanishes when its instance dies or is resized, so it is treated as a cache. Durable data goes to a storage service such as an object store, and compute nodes come and go around it.

Operations do not disappearThe cloud turns capacity planning into cost management and hands patching to the provider, but someone still owns security, monitoring, service interactions and tracking down why things got slow. Services are also often multitenant: many customers share hardware, which needs careful isolation so one tenant cannot starve another.
6

Distributed versus single-node systems

A distributed system is several machines (nodes) cooperating over a network. There are many good reasons to build one: users on different devices, fault tolerance, scaling beyond one machine, serving users near where they are, elasticity, specialized hardware like GPUs, data-residency laws, even running jobs where renewable power is cheap.

But each network call can fail or time out, and you often cannot tell whether the other side did the work. Retrying might do it twice. Debugging needs tracing across services. Data spread across services is hard to keep consistent. The advice: if a single machine will do, prefer it. A modern server is very capable, and a single-node database is often simpler and faster.

Every hop is another chance to fail

If every call must succeed and failures are independent, the request succeeds with probability an. In practice failures are often correlated, which can be better or much worse.

MicroservicesEach service owns its database and is deployed by one team. That is mostly a solution to a people problem: letting teams move without coordinating. In a small company it is often unnecessary overhead.
ServerlessFunction as a service brings metered billing to code: the provider starts and stops capacity per request. You trade control for convenience, with time limits and cold starts.

The chapter also contrasts cloud computing with supercomputing (HPC): HPC runs long batch computations on tightly coupled hardware and typically handles a fault by restarting from a checkpoint, whereas online services must stay up while individual parts fail.

7

Data systems, law and society

Architecture is not only about the business. Laws such as the EU's GDPR and California's CCPA give people rights over data about them, including the right to have it erased. That creates real engineering questions. How do you delete one person from an append-only log, or from a model trained on their data? And the cost of keeping data is not just the storage bill. It includes the risk of a leak, fines, and being compelled to hand data over.

A principle worth keeping in mind is data minimization (in German, Datensparsamkeit): collect only what you need for a stated purpose and keep it no longer than necessary. It runs against the "store everything, it might be useful" habit of big data.
