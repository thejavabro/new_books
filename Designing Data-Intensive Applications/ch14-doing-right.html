1

Why a data book ends with ethics

The book closes by stepping back from mechanisms. Every system has a purpose and consequences beyond it, intended or not. Many datasets are about people: their behaviour, interests and identity. Technology is not good or bad by itself; what matters is how it is used and whom it affects, and engineers share responsibility for that. Ethics is not a compliance checklist. It is an ongoing practice of reflection, in dialogue with the people affected, with accountability for the results.

Two separate concernsThe chapter splits into two halves. First, using data to make automated decisions about people (predictive analytics). Second, collecting data in the first place (tracking, surveillance, consent, privacy).
2

Predictive analytics and the “algorithmic prison”

Predicting the weather affects nobody's rights. Predicting whether a person will default, reoffend or make costly insurance claims does. For an organisation, rejecting a good applicant costs little, while accepting a bad one costs a lot, so when unsure the model says no. But when many institutions rely on similar scores, one person flagged as risky, rightly or wrongly, gets refused everywhere, with no proof of wrongdoing and little chance to appeal.

Bias and discrimination: removing the column isn't enough

With machine learning we are not just automating a decision rule; we let the rule be inferred from data, and the learned pattern is opaque. If past decisions were biased, the model learns that bias. Removing the protected attribute does not help when other features act as proxies: in a segregated city, a postcode predicts ethnicity well. The model below never sees the group label. Try it.

Predictive systems extrapolate from the past, so if the past discriminated, the model encodes and amplifies it. Building a better future needs moral imagination, which only people can supply. Data and models should be tools, not masters.

4

Responsibility and accountability

When a person decides wrongly, they can be held to account and the decision appealed. Who is accountable when a model decides? A traditional credit score asks how did you behave in the past? and is based on your own record, which you can correct. Many ML scores ask how did people like you behave?. That is stereotyping, using inputs that are wider and harder to inspect.

Predictions are also statistical: right on average, wrong for many individuals. A national life expectancy of 80 says little about when any one person will die. Sample some lives:

5

Self-reinforcing feedback loops

Recommendation systems that predict what you want to see can build echo chambers. Decisions about people can produce downward spirals. Take employers screening hires by credit score: a misfortune causes missed bills, the score drops, the job applications fail, so income drops further and the score falls again. Algorithms can also collude by accident: pricing bots at petrol stations learned to keep prices high. Systems thinking means modelling the people around the software too, and asking whether the system widens or narrows existing gaps.

6

Privacy, tracking and surveillance

If a system stores only what users deliberately enter, it is doing a service for them: the user is the customer. Behavioural tracking can still serve users (better search ranking, "people also bought", A/B tests). But in an ad-funded business the advertisers are the customers, profiles grow ever more detailed and are kept longer, and the relationship becomes one better described as surveillance. The book suggests a thought experiment: swap the word.

Phones, smart speakers, TVs, toys: nearly every inhabited room now holds an internet-connected microphone, which past authoritarian regimes could only dream of, and we accept it voluntarily. "Nothing to hide" works only if you are not in a marginalised group. And it stops being benign once it feeds decisions: car telematics changing insurance premiums, or smartwatch motion sensors revealing what you type.

Consent and freedom of choice

"Users agreed to the terms" has three problems. Is the tracking actually necessary, or just what pays for ads? Can consent be informed when privacy policies obscure, and derived datasets combine data that users cannot reason about, including data about non-users? And is it free, when the terms are one-sided and leaving a service everyone uses has a real social cost? The GDPR says consent must be freely given, specific, informed and unambiguous, and withdrawable without penalty. Silence and pre-ticked boxes do not count. Judge each case:

8

Privacy is a decision right, not secrecy

"Privacy is dead, look what people post!" misunderstands the word. Privacy means choosing what to reveal, to whom and when. A patient may gladly share records with researchers but not with an insurer. Surveillance does not abolish that right; it transfers it to the company, which then decides, for profit, what is revealed indirectly (for example, ad targeting by illness). Privacy settings control what other users see, not what the service itself does.

Data will sometimes be wrong, unwanted or hurtful even when it is accurate, such as a "memories" feature resurfacing a painful event. Plan for these failures and keep human judgement in the loop.

9

Data as assets and power

Calling behavioural data "exhaust" makes it sound like waste. It is closer to labour users do for advertisers, and an asset that data brokers trade and that is sold off in a bankruptcy. Because it is valuable, criminals, insiders, hostile states, future management and future governments all want it. Critics call it a toxic asset, or the new uranium. Each year you keep it is another year it can leak or be compelled.

Knowledge is power, and watching others while not being watched is a strong form of it. Installing technology that a future regime could use for oppression is, in one security expert's phrase, poor civic hygiene.

Legislation and self-regulation

GDPR asks for purpose limitation and data minimisation: collect for specific, explicit purposes, and only what is necessary. Big data culture assumes the opposite: collect everything, combine it, and explore for new uses. Enforcement has been weak. Regulation also carries real trade-offs: shared medical data threatens privacy but could save lives. The book's conclusion is a culture shift. Treat users as people, not metrics. Regulate yourselves, explain clearly, purge data when it is no longer needed, and collect less to begin with. Data you do not hold cannot leak, be stolen, or be demanded. Practise on a sign-up form:
