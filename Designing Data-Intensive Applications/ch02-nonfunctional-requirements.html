Defining Nonfunctional Requirements

Beyond what an app does, there is how well it does it. Using a social-network timeline as a running example, the chapter makes speed, reliability, scalability and maintainability concrete enough to measure.

1

Case study: a social network's home timeline

Functional requirements say what an app does. Nonfunctional requirements say how well: fast, reliable, scalable, maintainable, compliant. To make them concrete, the chapter imagines a Twitter-like service: about 500 million posts a day (roughly 5,800 per second on average, with spikes far higher), and an average user who follows and is followed by about 200 accounts.

The key read is the home timeline: recent posts from everyone you follow. You could compute it with a join every time someone looks. With 10 million users online and refreshing every five seconds, that is 2 million queries per second, each merging posts from about 200 accounts: hundreds of millions of lookups per second. The alternative is to precompute: when someone posts, deliver a copy into each follower's timeline, like dropping a letter into mailboxes. Reads become cheap; writes get expensive. This is fan-out.

Materialized timelines are derived dataThe precomputed timelines are a cache of the join. Fan-out trades work at write time for speed at read time, which pays off because timelines are read far more often than posts are written. The awkward case is the celebrity with tens of millions of followers, which is why real systems use a hybrid: fan out for ordinary accounts, merge celebrity posts in at read time.

Throughput, response time and queueing

Two families of performance metric: throughput (requests per second, bytes per day) and response time (how long one request takes). They are linked by queueing. When a server is quiet, a request is served right away. As throughput approaches capacity, each arrival is more likely to wait behind someone else, and response time shoots up.

Curve from the simplest queueing model (one server, random arrivals): mean response time is service time divided by (1 − utilization). Real systems differ in detail, not in shape.

Clients that time out and retry add load exactly when the server is struggling. That can lock a system into a metastable failure: it stays overloaded even after the original spike passes. Try a spike with and without protections.

3

Where the time goes

The book uses words carefully. Response time is what the client sees, end to end. Service time is the part where the server is actually working on the request. Latency is time spent waiting, not being processed: travelling over the network, or sitting in a queue. Because queueing happens on the server before work starts, you must measure response time from the client side to see it.

The same request repeated gives different times: a context switch, a lost packet and TCP retransmit, a garbage-collection pause, a page fault. A few slow requests also hold up the fast ones queued behind them, called head-of-line blocking.

4

Averages hide the pain; use percentiles

Response time is a distribution, not a number. The mean is easy to compute but says little about what a typical user felt. The median (p50) is the time half of requests beat. The tail, p95, p99, p999, tells you how bad the slow requests are, and those are often your most valuable customers, with the most data. Draw a sample and look.

Don't average percentilesAveraging p99s from several machines or time windows is mathematically meaningless. Merge the underlying histograms instead. Libraries such as HdrHistogram, t-digest and DDSketch keep approximate percentiles cheaply.

Percentiles also make good contracts. A service level objective might say: median under 200 ms, p99 under 1 s, 99.9% of valid requests succeed. A service level agreement adds what happens if you miss it, such as refunds.

6

Reliability: faults are not failures

Reliable roughly means "keeps working correctly even when things go wrong". A fault is one part going wrong: a disk, a machine, a dependency. A failure is the whole system no longer providing its service. A fault-tolerant system turns faults into non-events; a part whose fault always becomes a failure is a single point of failure. Tolerance is always for certain kinds and numbers of faults, which is why some teams deliberately inject faults (chaos engineering) to test it.

Hardware faults are mostly independent; redundancy works well against them. Software faults are correlated: every node runs the same code and the same bug.

Reliability matters for people, not just uptimeIn the UK Post Office Horizon scandal, bugs in accounting software made shortfalls appear in branch accounts, and hundreds of sub-postmasters were prosecuted for theft or fraud. The law presumed computers to be correct. Blameless postmortems and well-designed systems are how engineers take that responsibility seriously.
7

Scalability: coping with growth

"Is it scalable?" is the wrong question. Better: if load grows in this specific way, what are our options, and when do we hit the wall? Start by describing load with the right numbers (requests per second, read/write ratio, followers per user) and then ask how performance changes with load, or how much hardware keeps performance flat. If twice the resources handle twice the load, that is linear scalability.

PrinciplesBreak systems into parts that can work largely independently (the idea behind microservices, sharding and stream processing). And do not make it more complicated than necessary: a single machine beats a cluster if it does the job, and 5 services are simpler than 50. Rethink the architecture every time load grows by an order of magnitude or so.
8

Maintainability: operability, simplicity, evolvability

Most of the cost of software comes after it is first built: fixing bugs, keeping it running, adapting it to new platforms and requirements. Every valuable system becomes a legacy system. Three principles help the people who inherit it.

Essential or accidental complexity?

Essential complexity comes from the problem itself; accidental complexity comes from our tools. The line moves as tools improve, so treat this as a thinking exercise.
