The story so far

Plot recap · Chapters 14–15 (start of Part Three)

The Promotions effort, now joined by the Data Hub crew, needs a name that doesn't sound like Phoenix. Dwayne half-jokes "the Unicorn Project", after the nickname for high-flying tech startups, and it sticks, along with names for its platforms. At the Town Hall the CEO publicly takes responsibility for the Phoenix launch. Engineers build a fast data and API layer, a data platform and a recommendation engine against a Black Friday deadline, and learn the hard way to bring data scientists into engineering meetings early (one test run would have offered snow tires to customers in Texas).

On the Tuesday before Thanksgiving they rehearse with a promotion to 1% of customers. Orders stall at 250 when a decades-old order system overloads and clients retrying make it worse; a configuration change slowing the retries saves the day, and the response rate is the best the company has ever seen. On Black Friday the load is far higher. A shipping provider's API starts throttling them, the recommendation widget pins the web servers' CPUs, and the team keeps the site alive by switching things off and moving load elsewhere. Maxine's verdict at the celebration: impressive, but they are still a video-rental chain that has learned to do coupons.

Put a fast layer in front of slow systems

A major part of Narwhal is keeping copies of data from the big systems of record, wherever those are too slow, too hard to change or too expensive to call for every request. Teams get one consistent place to find data, and the old systems stop being hammered. Compare a product page that calls the back ends directly with one served by a copy.

4

The 1% rehearsal and the thundering herd

Sending the first campaign to just 1% of customers, in the middle of a working day, is itself a lesson: a small blast radius and everybody on hand. It pays off when completed orders flatline. The "available to ship" check runs on a heavily customized core system that can't simply be given more CPUs; as queries slow and time out, every client retries, adding even more load: a thundering herd. The fix is to get clients to back off. Compare retry policies.

// illustrative: wait longer after each failure, with randomness
delay = min(cap, base * 2 ** attempt) * random(0.5, 1.0)

They could not update the mobile apps quickly, but they could push new retry settings to every web server, and did, in under ten minutes. A few months earlier, a production change in ten minutes would have been unthinkable.

Black Friday: keep the site up

On the day, a hundred times the trial load is expected. Several things go wrong at once. Your job: choose the responses the team uses and keep orders flowing. Each one trades a little functionality for survival.

Two ideas worth keepingGraceful degradation: design features so they can be turned off or simplified under stress, like showing only ground shipping when carriers' APIs fail. Don't depend hard on things you don't control: Maxine notes that a seemingly trivial external call nearly took down the whole launch, and that this will be the new normal.
6

What the trial run returned

At the end of the 1% trial, Maggie reads out the early numbers: over a fifth of recipients viewed the recommended products and over 6% bought, conversion she says is better than anything before by a wide margin. Most promoted items were high-margin or overstocked, so each sale counted double. The funnel below uses those two figures from the story.

"We're Blockbuster with coupons"Maxine's warning in the celebration: a better promotion is not a strategy. Companies that only got better at their old business (video rental, bookstores, toy and department stores) still disappeared. They have learned to fly; they haven't won anything yet.
