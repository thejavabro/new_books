1

The story so far

Plot recap · Chapters 5–6

Kurt's "mysterious meeting" is at the Dockside, a bar popular with factory workers. Around the table are engineers from Dev, QA, Security and Ops who have been quietly helping each other get around the bureaucracy: Kurt calls them the Rebellion. They trade war stories about steering committees, requirement documents, and having to go up, over and down the org chart just to talk to a peer. Then they open a betting pool on how long the Phoenix release will take.

The release starts Friday evening and goes worse than even the most pessimistic bet. A database migration runs for hours; the one infrastructure engineer everybody needs is pulled into every problem; on Saturday the e-commerce site keeps crashing under traffic while in-store systems, back-office apps and even badge readers fail. By the next week people are sick, others have quit, and the QA director has been let go. At the Dockside, a story about consolidating network switches gives Maxine a sudden insight: the company did all of this to itself. A voice behind her agrees.

"The Square": up two, over two, down two

One complaint gets a name and a cheer. To ask a fellow engineer in another department for something, you go up two levels of management, across, and down two levels. Ops engineers joke that they also need the return trip. Compare that path with talking directly, as the Rebellion does at the bar.

Protect the constraint

Brent, the infrastructure lead, is needed for the database meltdown and for people's configuration files at the same time. Every interruption leaves his most important task waiting. Maxine organizes "goalies" who handle anything that doesn't truly need him. Run the night both ways.

A recurring characterBrent is the same engineer who was the bottleneck in the companion novel The Phoenix Project. A single expert everybody depends on is a symptom: the knowledge needs to be spread and the work needs to be automated, not just routed better.
7

How the company did it to itself

Dwayne tells of three plants that each had their own network switch, consolidated onto one to save money. When it failed, three outsourcers blamed each other for a day. Maxine sees the bigger pattern: once the three teams share a component, they can't change it without coordinating, so they need a master schedule, approvals and project managers. Add more teams to one shared thing and watch the coordination paths multiply.

The number of pairs that might need to talk grows as n(n−1)/2: three teams make three pairs, ten make forty-five. That cost doesn't appear on the savings spreadsheet, and it is exactly what Phoenix suffers from. The next chapter gives it a name.
