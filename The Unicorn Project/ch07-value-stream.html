The story so far

Plot recap · Chapters 12–13

Data Hub can now get features production-ready in hours, so the bottleneck has moved again: to deployment. Maggie Lee, who owns retail product management, explains that effective promotional bundles are the most urgent business need before the holidays, and Data Hub is in their path. The team tries the official routes. Security won't reprioritize its review queue. The architecture review board (the TEP-LARB) defers every proposal, including Maxine's. Then a memo from on high quietly lets Data Hub operate outside the usual Ops and QA processes, and the team spends days containerizing, cleaning secrets out of repositories and speeding up builds.

Maxine measures one feature's history and finds that only about 2.5% of its time from idea to customer was spent in development. Maggie fixes the most obvious wait by moving a product manager's desk next to the engineers. The first production deploys are rehearsals with blank-line changes; the first real one breaks because of a mistyped connection string, and Kurt runs a blameless review. Maxine then joins the Promotions effort and, on Maggie's invitation, spends a weekend working in a store as a new hire.

Fix one bottleneck and the next one appears

The chapter opens with a tidy summary of the story so far: first nobody could get environments, then testing was the constraint, now it is deployment. The system can never go faster than its slowest step, and improving any other step changes nothing. Play the game: improve one stage at a time and watch where the constraint goes.

Where does a feature's time go?

Maxine pulls dates from the project-management and ticketing systems for one feature, a warranty idea that bounced around for about two years before becoming a crash priority. The number she gets: only 2.5% of the time from concept to customer use was spent in development. The rest was funding approval, waiting for a product manager's answers, and waiting for QA and deployment. She calls it not a value stream but a stagnant value pond. Maggie's fix is blunt: answering engineers' questions becomes the product manager's top priority, and his desk moves next to Maxine's. Questions that took days by ticket get answered by swiveling a chair, and engineers start learning the business directly.

4

The architecture board that says "next meeting"

The TEP-LARB is a board of architects who must approve new technologies. Maxine watches an engineer named Ellen propose a mainstream, widely used open-source application server, with her own team offering to support it. The board asks for a history of its vulnerabilities and patches and invites her back next meeting. Maxine and Adam's proposal gets the same treatment. The pattern Erik warned about: decisions made far from the work by people with little basis to judge it.

5

Rehearse the deployment with nothing

Before shipping real changes, the team pushes changes that do nothing: a few blank lines at the end of a file, at the same time each evening, after internal users have gone home and before the midnight batch jobs. Even that goes wrong at first (files missing from the container images take the service down for half an hour), which is precisely the point. Step through.

6

The blameless post-incident review

The first real deployment fails: tests pass, the application doesn't start, and the health checks go red. In the review, Kurt states the ground rule, the Agile Prime Directive: everyone did the best they could with what they knew. Brent immediately blurts out that it was all his fault for missing a connection string. Kurt gently steers back to building the timeline. Practice the skill: pick the wording that helps the system learn.

No hindsight allowedKurt's one rule for the review: nobody may say "I should have done X". In a crisis nobody knows what is really going on, and the next crisis will be just as uncertain. The question is what would make it easier to see and recover next time.
7

The Fifth Ideal on the shop floor

Every director-level leader at the company works in a store twice a year, a tradition said to date back to its founding. Maxine, who never got the chance in seven years, signs up through Maggie. She learns how much time staff spend diagnosing why a car won't start, and how they help customers avoid being overcharged at service stations. And she sees the trainer tell new hires to look up parts on a paper form, because the computer system is worse. Tap the things she can't unsee.

The persona work Maggie's team does, describing real customers and what they want, gets Maxine thinking of Erik's Fifth Ideal: what matters is what customers would pay for, not what an internal silo thinks it needs.
