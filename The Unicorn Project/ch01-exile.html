1

The story so far

Plot recap · Prologue to Chapter 2

Parts Unlimited, a century-old auto parts maker and retailer, botches a payroll run. The official write-up blames "human error and a technology failure" and says the person responsible has been moved somewhere harmless. That person is Maxine Chambers, a senior developer and architect who was on vacation when it happened.

Her new post is the Phoenix Project: the company's omni-channel rescue plan, years late, around $20 million spent, and famous for misery. Told to "lie low" and write documentation, she sets herself a smaller goal: do what any new developer should be able to do and build Phoenix on her own laptop. A week later she still can't. Meanwhile, at a Town Hall, the retail SVP announces Phoenix will launch within weeks, and a new board member starts quietly pushing to break the company up.

2

"Who caused it?" is the wrong question

The book opens with a textbook case of what it will later call the opposite of psychological safety. A complex failure gets reduced to a single name, that person is moved, and the organization declares the problem solved. Nothing about the system that produced the failure has changed. The scavenger hunt: a week without a build

Maxine's to-do list grows to over a hundred items: network shares, license keys, wiki access, installers, credentials, a development environment. Each lives with a different team behind a different ticket queue, and tickets get reassigned rather than resolved. Try her first week. Then flip on a self-service platform and run it again: same work, no queues.

Each item needs a ticket. In queue mode a ticket waits 1 to 6 days and has a chance of being transferred to another team, which restarts the wait. The numbers are illustrative.

The build is a product tooMaxine's view: the systems that let developers write, build and test code safely matter more than any single feature. Here nobody owns them. If the Phoenix developer setup were a product, she thinks, it would be the worst product ever.
4

Why the tickets take so long

A ticket isn't slow because people are lazy. It's slow because the people who handle it are busy. A common rule of thumb from queueing theory: the wait in front of a resource grows with the ratio of busy time to idle time. At 50% busy you wait about one unit; at 90%, nine; at 99%, ninety-nine. Now chain several of these queues together, which is what Maxine's list really is.

5

Measuring hours instead of outcomes

At the end of the week her manager asks for a timecard: eight hours a day against a project code, plus a description of what she did. Her honest answer is "waiting for the entire universe". The system rewards looking busy, because utilization is what protects headcount, while the thing that actually matters, value delivered, is not measured at all.

6

"Safety is a precondition of work"

The CEO, Steve Masters, ends the Town Hall with a story from a plant: a worker was hurt by a stamping press, and the team replaced the machine with one that has a sensor to stop the plates closing on a hand, without waiting for budget. On the factory floor the company already knows that you fix the conditions, not the worker. The irony the book sets up is that its technology department does the opposite, as the payroll scapegoating shows. Physical safety and psychological safety turn out to be the same idea.

7

The clock in the background

Between chapters, short emails from a new board member set up the business stakes. The company has spent heavily on its digital push with nothing to show for it, and he wants options: restructure, sell non-core divisions, return cash to shareholders. The story is a race between two theories of how to save Parts Unlimited.

Squeeze for valueCut costs, split manufacturing from retail, sell what can be sold. Fast, measurable, and it treats technology as a cost center.
Grow through softwareUse technology to serve customers better and win share. Slower to prove, and impossible while hundreds of developers can't even build their code.
