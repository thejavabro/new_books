1

The story so far

Plot recap · Chapters 3–4

Maxine starts inviting herself to any meeting that might lead to the pieces she needs. In a crowded Phoenix status meeting, run by the head of the project office, Kirsten Fingle, she hears an engineer say what she already believes: developers need fast, continual feedback on their work. Afterwards a QA manager named Kurt Reznick quietly takes a photo of her list and says he can get her almost all of it.

That afternoon she helps at a middle-school coding club and fixes a contribution to her own open-source side project in fifteen minutes. It reminds her what coding should feel like. The next day the launch is moved to tomorrow. Marketing expects a huge surge of customers; testing shows Phoenix falls over at a tiny fraction of that load; firewall changes alone usually take weeks. Kurt delivers a binder of hard-won build instructions and a virtual machine cluster, and Maxine's first full build runs for three hours before failing on a missing credentials file.

Run your program all the time

Maxine's advice to two students stuck on a pile of indentation errors: run the program every time you change anything, so each mistake shows up alone and right away. The same rule scales to a team. The longer the gap between making a change and learning whether it works, the more other changes pile on top, and the link between cause and effect disappears. Drag the slider.

3

Two kinds of coding day

The chapter sets two days side by side. On her open-source project Maxine reviews a stranger's improvement, merges it, and adds the feature he wished for, all in a quarter of an hour, because the system lets one person make a change without depending on anyone. In Phoenix the same person can't get started. Compare where the hours go.

Focus, flow and joyFast feedback, independence from other teams, and seeing the results of your work: Maxine recognizes these as what great architecture makes possible. Erik will later name them the Second Ideal.
4

Anatomy of a ticket loop

To get a development environment Maxine opens ticket after ticket. Each comes back closed for a different reason. Step through a loop the chapter makes painfully familiar.

What Kurt does insteadKurt's binder is the opposite of a queue: someone who cares has already gathered the instructions, keys, videos and a cluster with admin access, so the next developer doesn't repeat the hunt. It is an early, hand-made version of the internal platform the Rebellion will go on to build.
5

Five transactions a second

In the launch meeting, Marketing's estimate is around a million visitors and a couple of hundred orders per second. The current test result is about five transactions per second before database clients start timing out (probably missing indexes). When arrivals outrun capacity, the backlog doesn't level off; it grows every second until requests time out. Push the load past the line.

6

Why the big bang almost never works

Phoenix had never been deployed to production before. The launch would change systems that had never talked to each other, needing hundreds of tickets, firewall changes and coordinated steps across Dev, QA, Ops and Security. When everything must go right at once, small risks multiply.

The way out isn't to be more careful. It is to make each change small, deploy often and practice the path to production until it is boring. That is exactly what the rest of the book builds toward.
