The story so far

Plot recap · Chapter 7 (end of Part One)

The voice belongs to the bartender, who Kirsten recognizes as Dr. Erik Reid: a figure from the company's past who helped roll out its manufacturing planning system decades ago and brought Lean practices to the plants, and whom the CEO is trying to recruit to the board. He praises Maxine's old manufacturing system as a "masterpiece of simplicity and locality", explains a word the table has never heard (complected), and calls the switch story a lesson in coupled value streams.

Pressed for more, he lists the Five Ideals, explains only the first two, and leaves the rest as one-liners before going back to serving drinks. The table also learns that IT's engagement score is deeply negative while the rest of the company is among the best in its industry. Kurt announces a bold move: instead of staying in QA, he will ask to run a development team, and everyone at the table volunteers to join him.

2

Four strands of yarn

Erik borrows an image from the programmer Rich Hickey. Four strands of yarn hanging side by side form a simple system: you can change one without touching the others. Braid the same strands together and they are complected: to change one you have to handle all four, and some changes become impossible. Both versions do the same job; one is far easier to change.

Pick an arrangement, then try to change one strand.

3

Locality: change one thing in one place

Simplicity is what makes locality possible. In code, locality means a feature can be built by changing one module, one service or one API, and a cross-cutting concern like logging, security or retries lives in one place, so changing it there changes it everywhere. In organizations, it means a team can make a decision without convening people far from the work. Erik asks: isn't it absurd that one feature needs the UI team, the front-end team, the back-end team and the database team?

4

Complexity debt comes due

Asked what technical debt is, Erik quotes Ward Cunningham: it is what you feel the next time you want to make a change. Erik widens it to complexity debt, because it covers builds that no longer give fast feedback, components that have become complected, and org structures that force small decisions upward. And it is always a choice: spend capacity on features, or on paying the debt down. Choose a policy and run three years.

Toy model: each quarter of feature work adds complexity; complexity slows all future work; paydown removes some. Shapes, not exact numbers.

PhoenixThe build that used to work was neglected until it disappeared. Responsibility for it slid from developers to QA to interns. At one point each build was outsourced at a per-build fee.
Maxine's old teamSmall teams working independently on isolated components, paying down debt as part of daily work, and still adding features faster than Phoenix.

Erik's contrast: at the large tech companies, developer productivity is the job of the most senior engineers. Here it was handed to the least experienced.

The Five Ideals

Erik gives the names all at once and the explanations piecemeal. Each one also has an opposite, which this story has already shown in action. Tap through.

6

A department that is miserable

Erik's parting question: compare the engagement of technology staff with the rest of the business. Kirsten knows the answer. The company scores among the best in its industry; IT's score is negative twenty-seven. The CEO talks about engagement at every Town Hall, yet the department running the most strategic program is unhappy, and that should worry him.

The IT figure is the one quoted in the story. The company-wide bar is drawn only to show "high", not a number from the book.

7

Testing belongs inside development

Friends urge Kurt to apply to lead QA now that its director is gone. He wants a development team instead. His reasoning is one of the book's quieter ideas: testing after the fact is the old model of QA. Quality has to be built in by the teams who ship features, so the way to change testing is to change how developers work. A QA manager running a dev team breaks a status hierarchy that the book calls a caste system, and almost nobody expects him to get the job.
