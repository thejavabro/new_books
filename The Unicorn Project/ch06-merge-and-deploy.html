The story so far

Plot recap · Chapters 10–11

Testing Day turns into merge day. Developers who have worked on private branches for weeks pile into a conference room to fold hundreds of changes into the release branch, tracked in a QA lead's spreadsheet; the room overflows into a second one. Maxine watches thousand-line conflicts, forgotten merges and a near-miss where two changes combined cleanly but silently changed what the program did. Meanwhile Brent, Tom and Adam race to get Data Hub running in its own slimmed-down environment.

A pricing bug hits the live site. The fix is ready, but only one release engineer, Jared, is allowed to push it, and he can't be found. When Maxine asks why developers can't deploy their own fixes, the room answers with acronyms; she answers that the real reason is that nobody trusts them. Following Jared downstairs, she finds Ops in an identical war room, with identical pizza, waiting for identical approvals. She goes home with a fever. When she comes back, Data Hub runs in its own environment and under continuous integration, and the team has a Plan B: if nobody else will deploy and run Data Hub, they will.

Merging: fifty screenwriters, one script

Maxine's analogy: fifty screenwriters each write their part of a film in isolation for weeks, then meet the day before the deadline to merge it into one story. The longer branches live and the more people work in parallel, the more their changes overlap. Compare long-lived branches with merging into trunk daily.

Illustrative model: each developer touches ~40 lines a day in a 200,000-line codebase spread over hot spots; any two branches conflict when they touch the same area.

Merging daily doesn't remove conflicts; it keeps each one small, recent and obvious. Practices the Rebellion goes on to adopt, continuous integration and small batches, are this idea applied every day.

3

The conflict that compiles

The scariest thing Maxine sees is a semantic merge conflict: two changes that merge automatically, compile, and quietly change behavior. Someone noticed only because a result "didn't look quite right". Here is a small invented example.

Branch A: store prices in cents

price = item.priceCents  // was dollars
total = price * qty

Branch B: add a $5 discount

price = item.price
total = price * qty - 5

Auto-merged result

price = item.priceCents
total = price * qty - 5
// 5 cents off, not $5

No textual conflict, no compiler error. Only a test that checks the result, run soon after the merge, catches it.

A backlog with no "why"

Home sick, Maxine browses the Data Hub ticket backlog for the first time: hundreds of items, many over a year old, long on what and how but silent on why. A recurring theme stands out: promotions. A whole batch for a summer bundle campaign was never started, and it is now autumn. Sort the backlog by whether a ticket says why it matters.
