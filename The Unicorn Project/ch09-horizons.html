The story so far

Plot recap · Chapters 16–17

A week after Black Friday, Maxine is in the executive boardroom while Maggie briefs the CEO, CFO and others on the results: record promotions, higher sales in pilot stores after the in-store tablets were simplified, and a mobile app people actually use. Steve sums up his belief that employee engagement and customer satisfaction, plus careful cash management, are the only things that matter. Maggie then pitches bigger ideas, and Erik, present at the table, introduces Geoffrey Moore's Three Horizons. The retail SVP, Sarah, argues instead for splitting and selling the company, as the chairman and the new board member prefer. Steve and Dick find $5 million for an innovation program that Kurt, Maggie and Maxine will run under Bill Palmer.

A week later the ground shifts. The engineers loaned to Unicorn are being reassigned for the new fiscal year; the scavenged CI hardware keeps failing and teams threaten to leave it. Then Steve announces that the board wants a 3% cut in company costs, about 150 jobs, and the innovation money has to come from internal savings too. Erik reframes the exercise: separate Core from Context. Bill, Wes, Chris and Maxine work through what the company should stop running itself, and Steve commits to retraining the people affected instead of letting them go.

2

Three Horizons

Erik's framing: Horizon 1 is today's cash-cow business, where customers and operations are well understood (for Parts Unlimited, manufacturing and retail, each over a billion dollars a year and under attack). Horizon 2 is a new business growing fast enough to matter soon. Horizon 3 is the place for many small bets on ideas that might become Horizon 2. Each horizon needs to be run differently. Click one.

Erik's diagnosis is blunt: everything around the boardroom was built on Horizon 1, and the company has nothing in Horizons 2 or 3.

3

Why Horizon 3 must be fast

In Horizon 3, Erik says, speed matters most. He cites Steven Spear's observation from the pharmaceutical industry: the first product to market in a category tends to capture about half of all the revenue that category will ever produce, the second about a quarter, the third about 15%, and later entrants little. Choose your arrival order.

4

Fund bets, then decide every quarter

The plan Steve summarizes: each innovation initiative has clear business outcome measures (customer acquisition, repeat use, satisfaction). Every quarter the council decides for each one: continue, kill and move the team to the next idea, double down, or graduate it to Horizon 2. Run a portfolio of six hypothetical bets through four quarters.

Each bet has a hidden potential; each quarter reveals a noisy measurement of it.

The structure matters as much as the money: Kurt and Maggie run it "two in a box", sharing one position and responsibility for both the business and the technical outcome.

5

Squeeze for value, or grow

Steve lays out the two paths to the room. Run for value and you cut costs, squeeze margins and sell assets, which Steve compares to selling the furniture to pay the mortgage. Run for growth and you invest in offerings customers want and take share; investors reward growth with higher multiples. Toggle the strategies (illustrative curves, not company figures).

6

Sort it: Core or Context?

Moore's other idea, as Erik explains it: Core is what creates competitive advantage, what customers pay for and investors reward. Context is everything else a company must do, often mission-critical (payroll, email, HR), but no customer pays you for great payroll. Companies smothered by Context can't invest in Core. His analogy: factories once had a "chief power officer" running their own electricity; today power is bought from a utility. Sort these items.

Core or Context?

Erik adds a line attributed to Clayton Christensen: keep in-house what is not yet good enough, and buy what is more than good enough. And he warns that outsourcing isn't automatically the problem; it is losing sight of what customers pay for.

7

From Context to Core, in numbers

In the working session, the group identifies eighteen positions doing work that software services could replace for about $500K a year. Bill adds a column: fully funded at $5 million, the innovation effort could create about thirty-three technology positions in Core. The point isn't the layoffs; it is that the same people could be moved to more valuable work. Maxine even volunteers parts of her old manufacturing system (keeping the scheduling module, which is a real advantage as the plants move from build-to-forecast to build-to-order).

Meanwhile, back in the code

A smaller scene in the same chapter: Maxine and Cranky Dave open a piece of order code nobody has dared touch in years: about two thousand lines of near-duplicate getters. They first write tests to pin down what it actually does, then pull common operations into one place and rewrite the rest in a functional style with map, filter and reduce. By noon it is about five hundred lines. Step through the method.
