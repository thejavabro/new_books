The story so far

Plot recap · Chapters 8–9 (start of Part Two)

Kurt gets the development job, partly because nobody else wanted it: he takes over Data Hub, the unglamorous middleware being blamed for the launch disaster, and brings Maxine and Cranky Dave with him. At the same time the leadership declares a one-month feature freeze, which the Rebellion learns to call Project Inversion: stop building features, fix what is broken and pay down technical debt.

On her first day Maxine and a veteran developer, Tom, corner a race condition that has haunted the team, and fix it by rewriting the logic as pure functions. Then they discover the fix can't be tested until "Testing Day" the following week. At the Dockside, Kirsten reports that features now need over four teams on average, and Erik explains the Third and Fourth Ideals with stories from Toyota and Alcoa. On Testing Day Maxine shows up at QA with doughnuts, and finds a whole building of people who have been waiting on everyone else.

2

From Octopus to Data Hub

Data Hub moves data between business systems. When Tom joined it was called Octopus, because it connected eight applications. Now it connects more than a hundred, built in a mix of technologies accumulated over decades. Maxine refuses to sneer at it: software in production for twenty years is a city, always under renovation. But every new spoke makes the hub harder to change, and it was folded into Phoenix even though it shares almost no code with it.

3

Fixing a race condition with pure functions

The bug: under load, two threads update the same customer object at once and occasionally produce corrupt data. Maxine pushes input, output and side effects to the edges, gives each thread its own copy, and rewrites the object's methods as pure functions, whose output depends only on their inputs. Then she writes a unit test that reproduces the old bug every time, and passes every time with the fix. Run each version a few hundred times.

Why features got ten times slower

  
Two numbers frame the problem. Kirsten's project managers sampled features and found they needed 4.2 teams on average, many more than eight. Cranky Dave remembers when a significant feature took two to four weeks; now similar ones take twenty to forty. Every extra team is another backlog where your request waits its turn, and the feature isn't done until the slowest team is. Simulate it.

  

    
    

    

    
Toy model: each team needs one week of work but first finishes its own queue, a random 0–8 weeks; the feature also needs a coordination step per extra team.

  

  
The extended-warranty feature Maxine and Tom pick up is a live example: the whiteboard keeps growing until they expect to need six other teams. That's the opposite of the "two-pizza team" ideal; this feature would need a truckload of pizzas.

The Third Ideal and the Andon cord

  
Erik's explanation: in the age of mass production, managers designed the work and workers executed it, with little power to improve it. But learning happens at the front line. At Toyota, anyone who hits a problem pulls the Andon cord and is thanked for it, even if it stops the line; the problem is swarmed, solved, and the lesson spread. Its opposite is TWWADI, "the way we've always done it": a growing pile of rules, approvals and stage gates. Run a line both ways.

The Fourth Ideal, and a lesson from Alcoa

  
Psychological safety: nobody experiments or reports bad news in a culture of fear, and when something breaks a fearful organization asks "who?", then adds rules and removes the bad apple. Erik's evidence is industrial. Paul O'Neill made worker safety Alcoa's first goal; the story recounts that about 2% of a 90,000-person workforce was injured each year, a roughly 40% chance of being hurt over a career. Move the sliders to see how a small annual rate turns into a large lifetime one.

Steve Masters made the same commitment when he ran the plants, reportedly telling the CEO of the day that if workers can't count on not getting hurt, why believe any of management's quality or profit goals? Safety is a precondition of work. The open question in the book is whether he can extend that to the technology organization.

Testing Day: everybody is waiting on somebody

  
Maxine arrives at QA with five boxes of doughnuts, the first time anyone can remember Dev throwing QA a party. She hears the same frustrations she has: waiting for environments, environments left dirty by others, defects bouncing between Dev and QA. QA runs test plans from hundreds of Word documents and a decade-old tool that needs an ancient browser in a virtual machine. Tap a node to see who it waits on.

Project InversionA month to stop feature work and pay down debt, if teams actually take it.

    
Pure functionsNo shared mutable state, side effects at the edges: bugs become reproducible and fixes provable.

    
Teams per featureLead time follows the slowest of many queues, plus coordination.

    
Andon cordStop and fix causes, and the problem rate falls. Work around them and it doesn't.
