What is Uber?

Uber lets customers book a driver for a taxi ride from their phone. Drivers use their own cars to drive customers around. Both sides talk to the service, and to each other, only through the Uber app on their smartphones.

If you have not used the Uber app before, open it and watch what it does. It shows the cars near you, and the cars move on the map. When you request a ride, one nearby driver accepts, and you watch that car come to you. This lesson designs the service behind that screen.

This design builds on Designing Yelp or Nearby Friends. Yelp taught us how to find things near a point with a QuadTree. A QuadTree is a tree that splits the map into grids. When a grid holds too many points, it splits into four smaller grids. We reuse that index here.

The new part is that drivers move. Yelp's places stay still, so its index almost never changes. Uber's index would change every few seconds. Handling that change is the main design decision this lesson adds.

Step 1: Clarify the Requirements

💡 Always clarify requirements at the beginning of the interview. Ask questions until you know the exact scope of the system the interviewer has in mind.

Start with a simpler version of Uber. There are two kinds of users in the system: drivers and customers.

Functional requirements

  1. Drivers regularly tell the service their current location, and whether they are free to pick up a passenger.
  2. Customers can see all the available drivers near them.
  3. A customer can request a ride. Nearby drivers are told that a customer is waiting to be picked up.
  4. Once a driver accepts a ride, the driver and the customer can see each other's current location until the trip ends.
  5. When the car reaches the destination, the driver marks the journey complete. The driver then becomes available for the next ride.

Non-functional requirements

  1. Real time. A ride request must reach nearby drivers within seconds. The cars a customer sees on the map must be where they really are, within a few seconds.
  2. High availability. A customer who cannot open the app cannot get home. A driver who cannot report a location cannot earn.
  3. A heavy update load. Every active driver reports a location every three seconds. The design must absorb that stream without slowing down searches.

Some things are out of scope: payments, fare pricing, and map routing. We assume every driver already has a rating. How that rating is computed is out of scope too. Say this list out loud, so the interviewer can add anything back.

Step 2: Estimate the Scale

Ask the interviewer for the user counts. Assume the following:

  • 300 million customers and 1 million drivers in total.
  • 1 million daily active customers and 500 thousand daily active drivers.
  • 1 million rides a day.
  • Every active driver reports their location every three seconds.

Load. The rides are not the load. One million rides a day is only about 12 new rides per second. The location reports are the load.

500K active drivers / 3 seconds  = ~167K location updates per second

Bandwidth in. Each update carries a DriverID and a location. A DriverID takes 3 bytes, for reasons step 4 explains. A location is a latitude and a longitude of 8 bytes each, so 16 bytes. That is 19 bytes per update.

500K x 19 bytes                  = 9.5 MB per three seconds
9.5 MB / 3 seconds               = ~3.2 MB per second in

That is a small stream of bytes. What matters is the number of updates, not their size. About 167 thousand small writes a second is heavy for any index. Remember that number in step 6.

The outgoing bandwidth depends on how we send driver positions to customers, so we estimate it in step 6.

Step 3: Define the API

Five calls cover the core flow. The first one carries almost all the load.

PUT  /drivers/{driver_id}/location
  body: { lat, lng, available }        sent every three seconds
  200:  no content

GET  /drivers/nearby?lat=&lng=&radius=
  200:  [ { driver_id, lat, lng, rating }, ... ]

POST /rides
  body: { customer_id, pickup, destination }
  201:  { ride_id, status: "searching" }

POST /rides/{ride_id}/accept          (driver)
  200:  { ride_id, status: "accepted", customer_location }

POST /rides/{ride_id}/complete        (driver)
  200:  { ride_id, status: "completed" }

The location call runs about 167 thousand times a second. The nearby call draws the map for the customer. The three ride calls follow a ride from request to completion. Every call is authenticated. A driver may update only their own location, and no faster than once every few seconds, so a broken app cannot flood the service.

Step 4: Define the Data Model

There are two kinds of data here, and they change at very different rates.

Slow-changing data. A Drivers table (DriverID, name, car, rating, status) and a Customers table. And a Rides table (RideID, CustomerID, DriverID, pickup, destination, start time, end time, status). These are ordinary rows in a database. They change about 12 times a second, when rides are created and completed. The rating column is used in step 6 to rank drivers.

Fast-changing data. The current location of every active driver. This data changes 167 thousand times a second, so it lives in memory, in two structures:

  1. The QuadTree from the Yelp lesson. It answers "which drivers are near this point?" quickly.
  2. A hash table with the latest position reported by every driver. Call it DriverLocationHT.

Why both? The QuadTree is expensive to update, and the hash table is cheap. Step 6 explains the split. For now, define the hash table record. It holds the driver's ID, their previous location, and their newest location.

DriverID                          3 bytes
Old latitude                      8 bytes
Old longitude                     8 bytes
New latitude                      8 bytes
New longitude                     8 bytes
Total                            35 bytes

1M drivers x 35 bytes            = 35 MB

Why 3 bytes for DriverID? Two bytes can name only 65,536 drivers. We have one million. Three bytes can name about 16.7 million, so three is the smallest whole number of bytes that works. Why keep the old location? It tells us which grid the driver was in, so we can move them in the QuadTree.

The whole table is about 35 MB, ignoring hash table overhead. That fits in the memory of one server. Even so, step 6 spreads it across several.

One record is still missing. Customers watch drivers move: on the map, and during a ride. Step 6 builds a notification service that pushes a driver's new position to the customers watching that driver. That service needs to know who watches whom. Store this as a third memory structure, a subscription list: a hash table keyed by DriverID. The value is the list of CustomerIDs subscribed to that driver. The DriverID key takes 3 bytes, as before. Each CustomerID takes 8 bytes. Step 6 sizes this table and computes the bandwidth its broadcasts need.

That completes the model: three database tables, two location structures, and the subscription list. The schema below shows all six records and where each one lives:

Start with the memory band at the top. DriverLocationHT takes the full write stream, 167 thousand updates a second. It holds one record per driver, keyed by DriverID. The record stores exactly two positions: the previous one and the newest one. The old position exists only to find the driver's old grid. The new position is what the notification service broadcasts.

The QuadTree grid serves the searches. Each grid covers one area of the map and holds the drivers inside it. A full grid splits into four, as in the Yelp design. Each driver sits in exactly one grid, placed by the last position the tree received. The grid keeps each driver's rating with their position. Step 6 uses that copy to rank nearby drivers without a database read.

It also keeps the driver's availability, one byte, and that field is not optional. Requirement 2 asks for the available drivers near a customer, and requirement 1 has every driver reporting whether they are free along with their location. Without the flag in the index, a nearby search returns drivers who are mid-ride, and the Aggregator has to read the database for each one to find out. The available value arrives on the same update as the position, so keep it in both the hash table and the grid.

The subscription list belongs to the notification service. It maps one driver to the customers watching that driver. During a ride, the list holds the one customer in the car. Step 6 sizes it at about 21 MB and shows when customers subscribe.

The bottom band is the database, and it is ordinary. Drivers holds one row per driver, one million in total. Its rating column is a single aggregated number, for example stars out of five. Computing that number is out of scope, so no table here stores individual reviews. Customers holds one row per account, 300 million in total.

Rides connects the other two tables. Each ride names one customer and one driver. Its status column moves through the values the API returns: searching, accepted, and completed. One million rides a day is only about 12 new rows per second. The location stream never touches these tables.

Note how small the fast data is. The location table is about 35 MB, and the subscriptions are about 21 MB. The load from step 2 was never about size: it was 167 thousand writes a second. Step 6 spreads these small structures across several servers for exactly that reason.

Step 5: Draw the High-Level Design

At a high level there are two paths through the system, and they must not slow each other down.

The update path carries driver locations. A driver's app sends its position every three seconds. A load balancer spreads these updates over a group of Driver Location servers, each holding a part of DriverLocationHT. From there, a Notification Service sends the new position to any customer watching that driver. And QuadTree servers refresh the driver's place in the index, but only every 15 seconds.

The search path carries customer requests. When a customer opens the map or requests a ride, an Aggregator server takes the request. It asks the QuadTree servers for nearby drivers, merges their answers, and returns the result. For a ride request, it also contacts drivers through the Notification Service. A database holds the drivers, customers, and rides.

The point of the split is simple. A stream of 167 thousand writes a second must never go into the index that searches read from. Step 6 shows how the pieces do that, and ends with the full diagram.

Step 6: Go Deep

Why the Yelp QuadTree is not enough

Recall the dynamic grid from the Yelp lesson. Each grid holds up to a fixed number of points, and a full grid splits into four. That index was built for many reads and almost no writes. Uber has the opposite mix, which creates two problems.

  1. Updates are expensive. To move a driver, we find their current grid from their previous location. If the new position is outside that grid, we remove the driver and reinsert them into the correct grid. If the new grid then reaches its limit, we repartition it. Doing that for every driver every three seconds takes too much time and too many resources.
  2. We need a fast way to send driver positions out. Every active customer must see the current positions of nearby drivers. During a ride, both the driver and the passenger must see where the car is.

So the QuadTree finds nearby drivers quickly, but a fast update is not guaranteed.

Do we need to modify the QuadTree every time a driver reports a location? If we do not, the tree holds slightly old data. But remember why the tree exists: to find nearby drivers efficiently. Every active driver reports every three seconds, so there are far more updates than searches. So keep the latest position of every driver in DriverLocationHT, and update the QuadTree less often. Guarantee that a driver's newest position reaches the QuadTree within 15 seconds.

Be careful what that staleness is allowed to touch. Step 1 promised the cars on the customer's map are where they really are within a few seconds, and a 15-second-old index cannot deliver that on its own. The way out is to use each structure for what it is good at. The QuadTree answers which drivers are nearby, and 15 seconds of lag there is harmless, because a car does not leave the neighborhood in 15 seconds. The coordinates we send the customer come from DriverLocationHT, which is never more than three seconds old. So a nearby search returns a list of DriverIDs from the tree, and the Aggregator reads their newest positions from the hash table before answering. Say that out loud, because it is the difference between a stale map and a fresh one.

Spreading DriverLocationHT across servers

Do we need to distribute DriverLocationHT? The memory (35 MB) and bandwidth (about 3.2 MB per second) do not require it. One server could hold everything. But for scalability, performance, and fault tolerance, we spread it across several servers anyway.

Distribute by DriverID, not by location. Hashing on DriverID spreads the drivers over the servers at random. So a crowded area, like a city center at rush hour, does not put all of its drivers on one server. Distributing by location would do exactly that. Call the machines that hold DriverLocationHT the Driver Location servers. Besides storing locations, each server does two things:

  1. As soon as it receives an update for a driver, it broadcasts the new position to every customer interested in that driver.
  2. It tells the QuadTree server that holds the driver to refresh the driver's location. As agreed above, that can happen every 15 seconds.

Say how the QuadTree itself is partitioned, because the rest of this step assumes it. We hash on DriverID, as the Yelp lesson hashes on LocationID. Every server then holds a tree covering the whole map, over its own share of the drivers. Two consequences follow. A driver stays on the same server for their whole shift, so an update is always one server's work and never a handoff. And a nearby search has to ask every server and merge the answers, which is what the Aggregator does. Partitioning by region would avoid the fan-out, and it would put every driver in a busy city centre on one server, which is the hotspot we are avoiding.

Sending driver positions to customers: the push model

How can we broadcast a driver's location to customers efficiently? One answer is a push model. The server sends positions to the users who need them. A dedicated Notification Service does the sending. We build it on a publisher/subscriber model. In that model, a publisher sends messages on a topic, and every subscriber to that topic receives them. Here each driver is a topic, and the customers watching that driver are the subscribers.

When a customer opens the app, it asks the server for nearby drivers. Before the server returns that list, it subscribes the customer to updates from those drivers. The Notification Service keeps a list of subscribers for each driver. Whenever DriverLocationHT gets an update for a driver, it broadcasts the new position to all of that driver's subscribers. So the customer always sees the driver's current position.

How much memory do the subscriptions take? We have 1 million daily active customers and 500 thousand daily active drivers. Assume five customers subscribe to one driver on average. Store the subscriptions in a hash table keyed by driver, so updates are cheap. Each entry needs a 3-byte DriverID and its list of 8-byte CustomerIDs.

(500K x 3 bytes) + (500K x 5 x 8 bytes)   = ~21 MB

How much bandwidth does the broadcast need? Five subscribers per active driver gives the total number of subscriptions.

5 x 500K                         = 2.5M subscriptions

Suppose we send every subscriber a DriverID (3 bytes) and a location (16 bytes) every second. That is an upper bound, since a driver reports only every three seconds.

2.5M x 19 bytes                  = 47.5 MB per second out

How do we deliver the pushes? Either HTTP long polling or push notifications. The long polling lesson compares the options.

What is the drawback? New drivers. The customer was subscribed to the drivers who were nearby when the app opened. What happens when a new driver enters the area the customer is looking at? To add and remove subscriptions as drivers come and go, we would have to track the area each customer is watching. Keeping every customer's subscription list current makes the solution complicated.

The simpler answer: let clients pull

What if clients pull the information instead? The client sends its current location to the server. The server finds all the nearby drivers in the QuadTree and returns them. The client redraws its screen with the current positions. The server pushes nothing; the client asks. If the client asks every five seconds, the number of round trips stays small.

This is simpler than the push model for the map of nearby drivers. The push model still fits one case well: a ride in progress. There, one customer watches one driver, and the subscription never changes.

That choice makes the subscription numbers above much smaller, so redo them. Only rides in progress hold a subscription. A million rides a day at twenty minutes each gives the number of concurrent rides:

1M rides x 20 mins / 1,440 mins  = ~14K rides in progress
14K x 19 bytes                   = ~266 KB per second out

So the push path carries about 266 KB a second over 14 thousand subscriptions, not 47.5 MB a second over 2.5 million. The 21 MB subscription table shrinks to well under a megabyte.

The pull path takes the traffic instead, and step 2 left this figure for step 6. Assume 100 thousand customers have the map open at once, each pulling every five seconds, and each answer carries twenty nearby drivers:

100K / 5 seconds                 = 20K map requests per second
20K x 20 drivers x 19 bytes      = ~7.6 MB per second out

That is the real egress of the design, and it is the number to quote. It is larger than the in-ride push but far smaller than pushing every nearby driver to every customer. The push vs pull lesson covers the general trade.

Splitting and merging grids

Do we need to repartition a grid as soon as it reaches its limit? No. Give each grid a cushion. Let a grid grow or shrink about 10 percent past its limit before we split or merge it. Without the cushion, a busy grid near the limit would split and merge over and over. With it, the load from splitting and merging in high-traffic grids goes down.

The diagram below shows all of these parts together. The Driver Location servers receive the update stream and refresh the QuadTree servers. The Notification Service pushes positions out, and the Aggregator servers answer customers.

Read the diagram along the two paths from step 5. Driver updates come in through the load balancer to the Driver Location servers. From there they go out to the Notification Service and, every 15 seconds, to the QuadTree servers. Customer requests come in to the Aggregator servers, which query the QuadTree servers and reach drivers through the Notification Service.

Requesting a ride

How does the "Request Ride" use case work?

  1. The customer requests a ride.
  2. One of the Aggregator servers takes the request and asks the QuadTree servers for nearby drivers.
  3. The Aggregator server collects all the results and sorts them by how long each driver would take to reach the customer, using rating to break ties.
  4. The Aggregator server sends the request to the top few drivers at the same time, say the top three. Whichever driver accepts first is assigned the ride. The other two receive a cancellation request. If none of the three respond, the Aggregator sends the request to the next three drivers on the list.
  5. Once a driver accepts, the customer is notified.

Why three drivers, and not every nearby driver at once? Only one driver can get the ride. Every other driver who was asked must then be sent a cancellation request. Asking three at a time keeps that waste small, while still finding a driver quickly.

Ranking drivers

How can we rank the drivers within a given radius? Distance alone is not the whole answer, and neither is rating. Keep each driver's overall rating in the database and in the QuadTree, as one aggregated number, for example stars out of five.

Then be careful which number you sort on. Ranking a dispatch purely by rating sends the request to the best-rated driver in the radius, who may be the farthest one in it. The customer waits longer and the driver drives farther for the same fare. What a rider actually wants is the car that arrives soonest, so sort by estimated time to pickup and use rating to break ties. Distance along the road is a reasonable stand-in for that time when routing is out of scope, as it is here.

The merge does not change. Ask each QuadTree partition for its own best 10 by that score, and the Aggregator picks the best 10 of everything returned. That is the same merge it already does for a nearby search. Only the number being sorted on is different.

Fault tolerance and replication

What if a Driver Location server or Notification server dies? Keep replicas of these servers. If the primary dies, the secondary takes over.

Resist the urge to write the location stream to disk as well. It would mean 167 thousand durable writes a second, and it would buy very little, because this data repairs itself. Every active driver reports again within three seconds, so a server that comes up empty is complete again almost immediately. The one thing worth persisting is the ride state, which lives in the Rides table already. Compare that with the Booking rows in Designing Ticketmaster, where nothing regenerates a lost reservation and durability is the whole point. The replication lesson covers the mechanism.

Step 7: Bottlenecks and Failure Points

The interviewer does not expect this design to be limitless. They expect you to know where it ends.

  • The update stream is the first thing that breaks. About 167 thousand location writes a second would overwhelm the QuadTree if they went straight into it. DriverLocationHT and the 15-second refresh keep the index usable. Say that out loud, because it is the strongest thing about the design.
  • A dense city center makes hot grids. Many drivers in a small area means frequent splits and merges. The 10 percent cushion reduces that load, but it does not remove it.
  • Pushing positions to everyone is expensive. Pushing every nearby driver to every customer would need 47.5 MB per second and subscription lists that change constantly. Pulling the map every five seconds costs about 7.6 MB per second instead, and leaves push for rides in progress, where it is about 266 KB per second.
  • A Driver Location server can die. Its part of DriverLocationHT is lost unless a replica or persistent storage holds a copy.

Three advanced issues are worth naming, even if you do not solve them:

  1. How will we handle clients on slow and disconnecting networks?
  2. What if a client gets disconnected in the middle of a ride? How will we handle billing in that case?
  3. What if clients pull all the information, instead of servers always pushing it?

Where AI Fits in This Design

After the main design, interviewers often ask one more question: where would AI fit in this system?

The dispatch loop is where AI belongs. The Aggregator already ranks nearby drivers by rating before asking the top few. Rating alone is a weak predictor of a good match. A model can score each candidate instead: how long this driver would really take to reach the rider, and how likely they are to accept. The Aggregator's merge does not change; the number it sorts by gets smarter. ETA prediction, estimating how long a driver needs to arrive, uses live traffic and past trips. It is the deepest of these signals and a system of its own.

Demand forecasting runs offline. It predicts where riders will appear before they do, so drivers can be guided toward the next busy area. It feeds planning decisions, never the request path.

Fraud detection is the third fit, and it carries the interview point about errors. A model can flag fake rides and stolen accounts. Name the asymmetry: a wrong ETA costs a few annoyed minutes, but a wrong fraud flag blocks a real person from getting home. The safeguards should match the cost: the ETA needs no human review, and the fraud flag does.

DriverLocationHT, the QuadTree refresh, and the update stream stay exactly as designed.

💡 In the interview: spend your time on the update problem, and move quickly through everything else. Say early that the nearby search comes from the Yelp QuadTree, and that the new problem is drivers who move every three seconds. The moment the interviewer is listening for is when you separate the two jobs. A hash table takes the writes, and the QuadTree serves the reads, refreshed every 15 seconds. Then explain push versus pull for the map, and say which one you would choose and why. If you are asked what breaks first, name the update stream reaching the QuadTree directly.

Key takeaway: Uber is Yelp with moving points. Reuse the QuadTree for the nearby search, but never send 167 thousand writes a second into it. Keep the newest position of every driver in DriverLocationHT, spread across Driver Location servers by DriverID, and refresh the QuadTree every 15 seconds. Let the client pull nearby cars every five seconds, and use publish/subscribe push only for a ride in progress, where the subscription never changes. Route ride requests through an Aggregator that sorts nearby drivers by time to pickup, breaking ties on rating, and asks the top three at a time. Answer a nearby search with ids from the tree and coordinates from the hash table, so the map is seconds old rather than fifteen. Give grids a 10 percent cushion, and keep replicas rather than persisting the stream, because every driver reports again within three seconds.
