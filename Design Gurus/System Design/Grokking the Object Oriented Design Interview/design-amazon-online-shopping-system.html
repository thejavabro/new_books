Let's design an online retail store.

Amazon (amazon.com) is the world's largest online retailer. The company began as a bookseller. It now sells a wide range of consumer goods and digital media. For this problem, we focus on the online retail business, where users can buy and sell products.

Requirements and Goals of the System

We will design a system with the following requirements:

  1. Users should be able to add new products to sell.
  2. Users should be able to search for products by name or category.
  3. Users can search and view all products. They must become a registered member to buy a product.
  4. Users should be able to add, remove, and modify items in their shopping cart.
  5. Users can check out and buy the items in the shopping cart.
  6. Users can rate a product and add a review.
  7. Users should be able to give a shipping address where the order will be delivered.
  8. Users can cancel an order if it has not shipped.
  9. Users should get a notification whenever the order or shipping status changes.
  10. Users should be able to pay by credit card or electronic bank transfer.
  11. Users should be able to track their shipment and see the current state of the order.

Use case Diagram

We have four main actors in our system:

  • Admin: Manages accounts and adds or modifies product categories.
  • Guest: Can search the catalog, add or remove items in the shopping cart, and become a registered member.
  • Member: Can do everything a guest can. Members can also place orders and add new products to sell.
  • System: Sends notifications for order and shipping updates.

Here are the top use cases of the Online Shopping System:

  1. Add or update products. Whenever a product is added or modified, the catalog is updated.
  2. Search for products by name or category.
  3. Add or remove items in the shopping cart.
  4. Check out to buy the items in the shopping cart.
  5. Make a payment to place an order.
  6. Add a new product category.
  7. Send notifications to members with shipment updates.

Stage 1: products

Start with the thing being sold. A product belongs to a category. It carries a price and a stock count, and it collects reviews.

One decision here matters later. A review records its reviewer by id. It does not hold the member object. If a product held members and members held products, neither class could be understood on its own.

ProductCategory, Product, and ProductReview: Here are the classes related to a product:

Requirements 1 and 6 produce every class in this stage. Requirement 2 is the reason a product carries a category at all.

The filled diamond is composition: the whole owns its parts, and the parts die with it. Here it says Product owns its reviews. Delete the product and the reviews go with it.

The arrow to ProductCategory is a plain association instead. An association is a link between two classes where neither owns the other. A category exists whether or not a product is filed under it.

Notice what is not on the diagram. A review records its reviewer by id, so no arrow runs from ProductReview to a person. The legend after the diagram shows how to read every arrow used in this lesson.

Stage 2: finding a product

Requirement 2 says users search by name or by category. So the catalog keeps one index for each. Both indexes are updated in a single method. That is what stops them drifting apart when a product is added.

Search interface and Catalog: Catalog implements Search so that products can be searched:

Requirement 2 lists two ways to search. Those two methods become the one interface here. An interface is a list of methods a class promises to provide. Catalog implements Search, which is the dashed line with the hollow head. It keeps one index per search, so a lookup is a read rather than a scan of every product. The catalog indexes products, it does not own them. So that arrow is a plain association. Deleting the catalog would not delete a single product.

Stage 3: taking money and moving parcels

Requirement 10 asks for two ways to pay, and they behave differently. A card clears at once. A bank transfer is still pending when the call returns. That difference in behavior is why they are two classes rather than one class with a type field.

Shipment keeps a log rather than a single status field. So requirement 11 is answered by reading the last entry, and the whole history is still there.

Payment, Shipment, ShipmentLog, and Notification: After an order is placed, a shipment record is created:

Requirements 9, 10 and 11 produce this stage. An abstract class cannot be created on its own; only its subclasses can. Payment is abstract with one subclass per way of paying. Notification is abstract with one subclass per way of delivering. Both pass the substitution test, which asks whether a subclass can stand in for its parent. Either subclass really can be asked to take money or to send a message. The filled diamond at Shipment says it owns its log entries. That is what makes requirement 11 a matter of reading the last entry, with every earlier state still on record.

Stage 4: the cart and the order

A cart is a working area that can change. An order is a record of what was bought, and it must not change. So it takes a copy of the items rather than pointing at the cart.

Requirement 8 says an order can be cancelled until it ships. That rule lives in one method, canCancel, so nothing else has to remember it.

ShoppingCart, Item, Order, and OrderLog: Users add items to the shopping cart, then place an order to buy everything in the cart.

Requirements 4, 5, 7 and 8 produce the four classes here. The cart owns its items, the filled diamond, because clearing the cart destroys them. The order points at items with a plain arrow instead. It holds the list of what was bought, and that record must not change when the cart does. Order owns its log entries and its shipment, because it creates both. It only refers to the payment, which is handed to it from outside.

Stage 5: who is allowed to buy

Requirement 3 is the interesting one. Anyone can browse and fill a cart, but only a registered member can place an order.

That single sentence decides the class shape. Everything a guest may do sits on Customer. Placing an order does not, because a guest is a Customer and would inherit it. Ordering sits on Member, which is the only class with an account. Registering turns a guest into a member and carries the cart across.

This is the substitution test from the method chapter, applied to a requirement rather than to a piece of code.

Account, Customer, Guest, Member, and Admin: These classes represent the different people who interact with our system:

Requirement 3 decides the shape of this stage. Guest and Member both extend Customer. That is inheritance: each subclass gets everything the parent has. So anything on Customer is something a guest may do, and that is why placing an order is not there. Customer owns the shopping cart, the filled diamond. The cart is created with the customer and destroyed with them. Member and Admin each refer to an Account with a plain arrow. Member also places orders. That is the arrow that finally connects a person to the order built in stage 4.

The finished class diagram

Now that every class exists, here is the whole model in one picture. It is the same picture the five stages have been filling in, with every class in full color at last. Nothing on it appears here for the first time.

Here are the descriptions of the different classes of our Online Shopping System:

  • Account: There are two types of registered accounts. An Admin adds new product categories and blocks or unblocks members. A Member can buy and sell products.

  • Guest: Guests can search for and view products, and add them to the shopping cart. To place an order they must become a registered member.

  • Catalog: Users can search for products by name or category. This class keeps an index of all products for faster search.

  • ProductCategory: The different categories of products, like books and electronics.

  • Product: The thing that users buy and sell. Each Product belongs to a ProductCategory.

  • ProductReview: Any registered member can add a review about a product.

  • ShoppingCart: Users add the product items they intend to buy to the shopping cart.

  • Item: One product item that a user buys or places in the shopping cart. For example, a pen could be a product. If there are 10 pens in the inventory, each of the 10 pens is a product item.

  • Order: A buying order for everything in the shopping cart.

  • OrderLog: Keeps track of the status of orders, like unshipped, pending, complete, and canceled.

  • ShipmentLog: Keeps track of the status of shipments, like pending, shipped, and delivered.

  • Notification: Sends notifications to customers.

  • Payment: The payment for an order. Members can pay by credit card or electronic bank transfer.

What we left out, and what to say about it

This design covers the eleven requirements. Wish lists, seller payouts, returns after delivery, recommendations, and inventory across warehouses are all absent. That was a decision, not an oversight.

Two places where a reviewer will question the design, and the honest answer to each:

  • Stock is taken when the order is placed, not when it is paid. That keeps two buyers from taking the last copy. It also means an abandoned order holds stock until it is cancelled. A real system releases it on a timer.
  • Order copies the cart items. Sharing them would be cheaper. But then editing the cart would silently change a completed order.

💡 In the interview: the requirement worth talking about out loud is the one that separates a guest from a member. Say that you put ordering on Member rather than Customer. Otherwise a guest would inherit a method it must refuse. Name it as a Liskov substitution problem. Expect two follow ups. The first is "what if we add a new payment type". That is why Payment is an abstract class rather than an enum. The second is "how do you stop two people buying the last item". That is the reservation step in placeOrder.
