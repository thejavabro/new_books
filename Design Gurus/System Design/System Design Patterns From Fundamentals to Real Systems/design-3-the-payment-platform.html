Design 3 - The Payment Platform

On This Page

  1. The Architecture, Annotated

"Design a payment platform for a marketplace: accept card payments from buyers, hold balances, pay out sellers, and handle refunds: correctly, auditably, at scale."

1. Requirements and Numbers

Pause. Clarify and compute; notice which requirement outranks all the others.

Scale? About 2,000 payments per second at peak, and 5M sellers holding balances.

Latency budget? Authorization in about 2 seconds; the card networks dominate it anyway.

What is the correctness bar? Every cent must be accounted for, and every movement must remain permanently auditable for regulatory review.

The arithmetic shows that 2,000 payments per second is a moderate workload for Module 6. Correctness requirements, rather than raw throughput, determine this architecture.

Previous capstones permitted bounded staleness or approximation, but a payment ledger requires complete financial correctness; omitting a social post may be tolerable, while omitting a cent creates accounting and regulatory failures.

The design therefore uses the course's strongest mechanisms: Module 7, exactly-once, and event sourcing. Financial decisions fail closed, meaning the platform rejects an uncertain operation instead of estimating its outcome. This applies Module 5's strictest rule.

2. The Five Questions Pass

Pause. Run the procedure; watch how differently it answers when money is involved.

Flow: a payment starts as request-response; then it becomes a multi-step workflow with an external party: authorize at the card network, then capture, then record, then schedule the payout. That is a saga: a workflow of steps that each commit on their own. The network's confirmations arrive as at-least-once webhooks: callbacks that may arrive more than once.

Storage: the domain requires a ledger, so use event sourcing to store every change as an appended event. Financial accounting has used append-only ledger principles for centuries.

Speed: frequently displayed balances are derived projections, or read models computed from ledger entries. Any operation that authorizes spending checks the authoritative ledger, following the caching module's zero-staleness rule.

Failure: idempotency, where a retried request has the same effect as the first, is required on every write path. The $998 lesson was this domain's founding incident; degradation fails closed: no payment is better than a wrong payment.

Growth: shard by account: split the data across machines by account; that raises the cross-account transfer question, this design's deepest deep dive.

The skeleton: use an append-only double-entry ledger as the authoritative record and derive balances as projections. Coordinate payments across the external network with sagas, require end-to-end idempotency, and operate reconciliation as a dedicated subsystem.

3. The Architecture, Annotated

The gateway. The gateway requires an idempotency key for every mutating request. This client-selected ID identifies all retries of one operation; the gateway rejects requests without a key, following the discipline popularized by Stripe.

The intent. A payment becomes an intent: a state machine (created → authorized → captured → recorded → …); its transitions are the saga's steps; each step is idempotent, and each is made durable before its side effect. That is write-ahead thinking: the intent log is the workflow's WAL, a write-ahead log, recorded before the action it describes.

The card network. Calls to this external party use the full reliability kit, including a circuit breaker that pauses calls to a failing dependency. Confirmations arrive through webhooks and follow that lesson's five disciplines. However, the platform never treats a webhook as sufficient evidence by itself; daily settlement files provide an independent record, and reconciliation compares the internal ledger, webhook stream, and settlement files.

The ledger. This is the center; every money movement is an appended, immutable pair of entries: debit one account and credit another, summing to zero. That is double-entry, the 500-year-old invariant that makes errors visible; money can never appear or vanish. It can only move.

Mistakes are corrected by new compensating entries; that is the accountant's rule. The error and its correction both stay visible forever; that is exactly what auditors mean by "auditable."

Balances. Balances are projections: derived, rebuildable, and cached. Balance snapshots serve as checkpoints, so computing an account's balance replays days of entries, not years.

4. Deep Dives

Pause. The interviewer will pick from three: the ledger's write path, the transfer question, and reconciliation.

Deep dive 1: the account write path, which is ownership again. Suppose two 100 balance. At most one can succeed, so the design must prevent a financial check-then-write race. Apply ownership from the ride-sharing capstone: one writer appends entries for each account, and the account's shard processes them sequentially. Each append conditionally verifies that the projected balance covers the debit; this atomic ledger claim converts account contention into a queue without distributed locks.

The marketplace's own fee account is touched by every transaction; it is a hot key: one key that gets far more traffic than the rest. It gets the hot-key treatment: entry batching and a dedicated shard; skew's seventh appearance.

Deep dive 2: transfers, and the 2PC question everyone expects. A buyer paying a seller moves value between accounts that may reside on different shards. This does not require the gift-card 2PC approach across two databases because both accounts belong to one logical ledger.

A transfer is two entries in the same logical journal; the journal's append is the atomic unit. Within a shard, that is trivial; across shards, the entry pair is written as a single ledger event: both legs in one record, projected to two accounts' balances. The atomicity lives in the append, not in coordinating two databases.

Module 7's rule applies: avoid distributed transactions by defining one atomic operation when possible. Here, the money movement is that atomic operation, while balances are projections of it.

External moves cannot use this trick; a payout to a real bank is a saga with idempotent bank instructions and reconciliation, because the bank's ledger is not ours.

Deep dive 3: reconciliation, the subsystem that assumes we are wrong. Three records exist: the internal ledger, network webhooks, and network settlement files. Bank statements provide a fourth record for payouts; automated daily reconciliation compares them, creating a work item with trace-carrying evidence for every discrepancy.

This applies log-and-verify to financial records; reconciliation recognizes that external systems can fail, retry, and duplicate operations even when internal code is correct. Agreement with the bank is verifiable only through comparison; this expands the webhook lesson's reconciliation loop into a dedicated operational function.

5. The Failure Walk

Pause. Money's failure walk is the strictest in the course; walk every arrow.

Crash mid-capture. The durable intent remains authorized, while the capture outcome is unknown; this is financial timeout ambiguity. Retry capture with the same key so the network returns the original outcome, or query the network directly before advancing state. Durable intent state makes recovery deterministic.

A webhook never arrives. The settlement file catches it within a day. The intent's timeout policy catches it within hours: an authorized intent past its window triggers an active query. Silence is a signal, and it is covered twice.

Double refund click. The idempotency key dedupes the request; the state machine rejects the replay as well, because refunded is terminal. That is two independent guards; refunds are where retries and impatience meet.

A projection drifts. If the balance cache disagrees with the ledger, treat the ledger as authoritative and rebuild the projection from events. The architecture defines which value is correct, applying the event sourcing lesson's Q3 to financial data.

A bug wrote wrong amounts for two hours. Immutable ledger entries remain unchanged, so append correcting entries that reverse the erroneous movements. The event stream identifies every affected payment; the audit history preserves both the error and correction, satisfying compliance requirements.

The card network browns out. When the network slows and partially fails, the breaker opens. The platform saves the intent and fails closed: "we couldn't process your payment, nothing was charged, please retry." The Module 5 capstone's deferred-capture option applies only to preauthorized flows with an approved financial risk budget. Uncertain payment movement requires the fail-closed behavior.

6. The Follow-Up Questions

"Why not just a balances table with UPDATEs? Simpler."

Three reasons justify the ledger; Audit: an UPDATE destroys history required for disputes and regulation, recreating the event sourcing incident. Correctness: double-entry's sum-to-zero invariant exposes errors that a mutable balance can hide; Concurrency: conditional append operations serialize at one writer, while concurrent UPDATE statements require explicit locking. A balance table still exists as a disposable projection.

This question tests whether you understand that event sourcing is usually optional but is particularly appropriate for financial ledgers.

"Where's your 2PC?"

Internal transfers are single atomic ledger events, while balances are projections, so one journal does not require a distributed transaction. At external card-network and bank boundaries, no shared journal exists; use sagas with idempotent instructions and reconciliation instead. Two-phase commit cannot extend across independent companies, following the 2PC lesson's trust-boundary rule.

The trap: proposing 2PC to the bank; the interviewer is checking that you know where the pattern cannot go.

"How fresh are balance reads?"

Projections lag by milliseconds; that is fine for display; any read that authorizes movement is different. The payout check and the negative-balance guard are the conditional append itself. The account's single writer evaluates it against the ledger, atomically. Zero-staleness reads route to the source, and here the source is the append's own precondition. Display and decision, split again.

"GDPR deletion versus an immutable ledger?"

Split the data; personal fields are crypto-shredded: each user's data is encrypted with a per-user key, and the key is destroyed on request. Financial records are retained; regulation requires money records to survive deletion requests, and the exemption is explicit. Immutable structure, erasable identity, retained obligation.

"What breaks first at 10x scale?"

Fee accounts and popular sellers become hot keys before average traffic exceeds capacity; batching and dedicated shards address this skew. The next limit is reconciliation's daily batch window, which can move to streaming. Ledger append throughput otherwise scales by account shard.

7. The Pattern Manifest

PatternWhere it served
Event SourcingThe ledger: the pattern's ancestral home, mandatory for once
CQRSBalances as rebuildable projections; display vs. decision reads
SagaPayment lifecycle; payouts; everything crossing a trust boundary
Two-Phase Commit (avoided)The transfer insight: one journal, no distributed transaction
IdempotencyEnd-to-end: gateway-required keys → intents → appends → bank instructions
WebhooksNetwork confirmations: five disciplines + never-trust-alone
Write-Ahead LogIntent states durable before side effects; snapshots as checkpoints
Sharding + ownershipAccounts with one writer each; hot-account treatment
Circuit Breaker + fail-closed degradationThe network boundary; "nothing was charged" as the only honest fallback
Reconciliation / log-and-verifyThe three-way match: assuming we're wrong, provably
Exactly-OnceFee metering and financial aggregates ride the audited machinery
Distributed TracingEvery discrepancy a clickable story

This payment platform combines twelve lessons around Module 7's consistency mechanisms; internal journal design removes the need for 2PC, while external parties require sagas and reconciliation. The interview summary is: "money is an append-only history, balances are projections of that history, and reconciliation verifies agreement among independent records."
