Two-Phase Commit

  1. TL;DR

1. The Incident

The store adds gift-card transfers between customers. Customer A, stored on shard 2, sends $50 to customer B on shard 5. The sharding lesson's warning now applies because one logical operation changes two databases without one transaction covering both.

The first implementation subtracts 50 to shard 5. Thousands of transfers complete successfully through these two sequential writes.

At 2:37 AM, the application server stops after committing the subtraction but before sending the addition. No retry occurs because the process holding the transfer intent no longer exists, and no visible error identifies the missing work.

A nightly reconciliation job compares shards and finds that 50 twice. Finance now has both destroyed and created money from the same underlying failure.

Each shard executed its local write correctly, but the overall operation was never atomic. All-or-nothing behavior across machines must be implemented explicitly.

2. The Obvious Fixes, and Why They Fail

"Write A, then B, and retry B until it works." Idempotency keys make repeated writes safe by applying one key only once, but two gaps remain. If shard 5 is unavailable for an hour, users see missing money for an hour. If the application stops between writes, its in-memory retry loop also disappears. The transfer intent must be durable enough to survive its caller, which becomes important in the saga lesson.

"Write both in parallel." Either side can still fail independently, which creates two possible partial states instead of one. Faster writes do not provide atomicity.

"Let reconciliation clean it up." A nightly process can detect and repair mismatches, which is acceptable when the business permits hours of inconsistency. However, reconciliation detects a failure after it becomes visible rather than preventing the failure. Gift cards might tolerate that delay, but operations that must never expose partial state need atomic execution.

3. The Pattern

A coordinator runs the operation in two phases. Phase 1, prepare: it asks every participant "can you commit this: yes or no?" Each participant does all the work, durably records its readiness, and votes. Phase 2, commit or abort: if every vote was yes, the coordinator orders everyone to commit. Any no (or silence), and everyone aborts. Nobody commits until everybody can.

A yes vote carries a binding promise: the participant completed the work, stored it in its write-ahead log, and can commit after a crash. It must also retain its locks until the coordinator sends a decision. This promise creates both atomicity and the pattern's largest risk:

The participant must store PREPARED before replying yes, while the coordinator must persist its final decision before announcing it. Those ordering rules ensure that recovery never invents a result from memory. A restarted participant reads its log and asks the coordinator for a verdict rather than rerunning business logic.

  1. A participant that voted yes is in doubt. It cannot commit on its own, because someone else may have voted no. It cannot abort on its own, because the coordinator may already have decided commit. It must wait, holding locks on the rows involved, until the coordinator speaks.
  2. So the coordinator's silence freezes everyone. If the coordinator crashes after collecting yes votes, every participant sits in doubt with locks held. Every other transaction that touches those rows is blocked, for as long as the coordinator takes to recover.
  3. The decision itself lives in the coordinator's log. One entry, "commit" or "abort," is the transaction's fate. 2PC gains atomicity by putting the decision in one place. It inherits that one place's fragility.

4. Walkthrough with Numbers

Now apply 2PC to the $50 transfer.

The happy path's price. Prepare and commit require two network round trips, plus a durable log write, or fsync, at each participant and the coordinator. The transfer takes about 4x the latency of one local-shard write, which is noticeable but may be acceptable.

The hidden price is lock time. Each shard holds gift-card-row locks from prepare through commit, increasing the duration from about 1 ms to 10-20 ms. The arithmetic lesson's relationship, rate x duration = concurrency, means 10x longer locks create 10x more collisions on hot rows. A 2PC transaction therefore slows nearby work as well as itself.

The failure drill. The coordinator stops 5 ms after both shards vote yes. Both participants remain in doubt with locks held until a replacement replays the coordinator log and sends commit 40 seconds later. During that period, every transaction touching either customer's rows waits or fails.

Coordinator replication reduces recovery time by keeping the verdict available after one machine fails. However, replication does not remove prepare locks, network round trips, or dependency on every participant. It mitigates the most severe single-process failure without changing the protocol's fundamental cost.

The availability math. As the foundations lesson explains, the coordinator and both shards must be available together. Three parts that are each 99.9% available produce about 99.7% combined availability. Every additional participant reduces that result.

5. Trade-offs

You gain:

  • Real atomicity across machines. The vanished-$50 class of bug is structurally gone, with no reconciliation required.
  • Strong consistency. No observer ever sees the half-done state. The world where money left A but has not reached B is invisible to everyone.
  • Transparency to application code. With driver support (the XA standard, a common interface for 2PC), it looks like an ordinary transaction.

You pay:

  • Blocking, the signature cost. In-doubt participants retain locks while a coordinator may be unavailable. The coordinator becomes a single point of failure for progress because no participant can finish alone. Stuck prepared transactions are a genuine operational emergency for database administrators.
  • Latency and throughput. Two round trips, several fsyncs, and longer lock duration increase latency and contention on frequently accessed data.
  • Availability multiplies down. Every participant must be available before any of them can commit.
  • It does not cross trust boundaries. A trust boundary separates your systems from another company. External organizations will not allow your coordinator to retain locks in their databases, so 2PC remains internal.

6. When Not to Use It

Do not use 2PC between microservices. Locks held across service boundaries tie every participant's latency and availability to the slowest service. A checkout can take seconds or minutes, making its lock duration unacceptable, while the coordinator becomes both a technical and organizational bottleneck. The saga pattern addresses this exact problem.

Also avoid 2PC for hot rows, cross-company operations, and workflows where reconciliation by morning satisfies the business requirement. In those cases, its atomicity costs more than the inconsistency it prevents.

The pattern remains useful for short, internal, infrequent, high-value operations, especially inside distributed databases. Systems such as Spanner run 2PC between shard groups, but each participant and coordinator is a quorum-replicated group rather than one machine. A majority can continue after individual failures, which removes most coordinator-blocking risk. 2PC is therefore neither obsolete nor appropriate everywhere; its strongest use is inside a database with replicated participants.

Operational safeguards still matter in that environment. Set strict transaction deadlines, expose prepared-transaction age, and alert before retained locks affect unrelated workloads. Recovery tooling must show the coordinator decision and every participant state, because manual guessing can create the inconsistency that 2PC was intended to prevent.

7. 2PC vs. Saga (a Preview)

The next lesson develops this comparison fully:

Two-Phase CommitSaga
AtomicityReal: all-or-nothing, enforced by locksSimulated: steps commit one by one, failures are undone by compensations
Intermediate statesInvisible to everyoneVisible: the world sees "payment taken, shipping pending"
BlockingYes: in-doubt participants hold locksNo: each step is a normal local transaction
Coordinator diesParticipants freezeWorkflow resumes from its durable log
FitsShort, internal, infrequent (or intra-database)Business workflows across services

In one sentence, 2PC hides intermediate state by retaining locks, while sagas expose and manage that state. The remaining differences follow from this choice.

8. Real-World Examples

The XA standard and Java's JTA provide 2PC infrastructure for committing databases and message brokers together. Banks and airlines still operate these systems.

PostgreSQL's PREPARE TRANSACTION directly exposes the participant role. Its documentation warns against casual use because an abandoned prepared transaction can retain locks indefinitely until an operator resolves it.

Google Spanner uses 2PC across shard groups whose participants are Paxos-replicated. Paxos is a consensus protocol that lets multiple copies agree on one value. Replication mitigates individual failures, although it introduces consensus costs.

Kafka's transactions use a 2PC-shaped protocol for atomic consume-process-produce work, with a broker coordinator making the decision.

For data engineers: exactly-once writes to external systems commonly use this pattern. Flink transactional sinks treat checkpoints as prepare, meaning every operator records durable readiness. Checkpoint completion then commits work into Kafka or a database. The exactly-once semantics lesson returns to this protocol.

9. In the Interview

When asked how two services can update databases atomically, explain the full decision rather than naming 2PC alone. Describe its true atomicity, blocking failure, and modern use inside distributed databases, then explain why service workflows usually use sagas. Saying that 2PC belongs everywhere or nowhere misses its actual operating limits.

The 30-second answer: "Two-phase commit gets true atomicity across machines. A coordinator collects binding yes votes: each participant does the work, logs it durably, and holds locks. Then the coordinator orders everyone to commit. The price is blocking. A participant that voted yes can neither commit nor abort alone, so if the coordinator dies at the wrong moment, everyone freezes holding locks. Add two round trips of latency, and availability that multiplies down with every participant. So between microservices I use sagas instead: local transactions plus compensating undo steps. And I respect 2PC where it actually lives today: inside distributed databases like Spanner. There every party is quorum-replicated, so no single crash can block the protocol."

Likely follow-ups:

  1. "Coordinator crashes right after both participants vote yes: walk me through it." Both participants are in doubt, committed to following the decision but unable to decide independently, so they retain locks until the coordinator recovers and replays its log. Weak tooling may require a human to resolve the prepared transactions. The essential property is blocking.
  2. "Why is this fine inside Spanner but wrong between your services?" A database uses replicated participant groups, millisecond transactions, one trust boundary, and one operational domain. Microservices often have individual participants, multi-second business workflows, and independent teams, which maximize every 2PC weakness without its mitigations.

A mistake that fails candidates: proposing 2PC for a multi-minute checkout, omitting coordinator failure behavior, or claiming the pattern is obsolete without recognizing its database implementations.

10. Check Yourself

Q1 (recall). Name the two phases. State exactly what a yes vote promises. Explain how that promise creates the blocking problem.

Q2 (trade-off). Your team debates using 2PC for two flows. One is the gift-card transfer: two shards, same database cluster, about 50 ms end to end. The other is the checkout workflow: inventory, payment, and shipping services, 2-30 seconds. Rule on each.

Q3 (scenario). 3 AM page: queries against the orders table have been hanging for four minutes. The DBA finds several transactions in PREPARED state, holding locks, hours old. Explain what happened, what to do now, and what design question the postmortem must ask.

Answers

A1. During Phase 1, prepare, participants perform the work, store a durable PREPARED record, retain locks, and vote. During Phase 2, unanimous yes produces commit everywhere, while any other result produces abort everywhere. A yes vote promises durable ability to commit and continued retention of state and locks until the coordinator decides. The participant can neither commit when another may have voted no nor abort when commit may already be decided, so coordinator silence blocks it indefinitely.

A2. 2PC is defensible for the gift-card transfer because it is internal, infrequent, within one trust domain, and about 50 ms long. First ask whether both balances can share one shard, applying Fact 2. The checkout workflow should use a saga because seconds or minutes of cross-service locks would sharply reduce throughput and couple three teams' availability.

A3. These participants voted yes and entered PREPARED, but the coordinator never delivered its decision because it stopped, lost its log, or failed between phases. PostgreSQL retains their locks until someone finds the coordinator verdict and runs COMMIT PREPARED or ROLLBACK PREPARED. If no verdict survives, the business must decide manually. The postmortem should ask whether the flow needs 2PC and, if it does, why the coordinator remains unreplicated. The repair is either a saga or a replicated coordinator.

11. Related Patterns

  • Write-Ahead Log: where PREPARED records and the coordinator's verdict live. The promise is only as durable as the log under it.
  • Saga: the successor for service-to-service workflows. Next lesson.
  • Sharding: the pattern that created this need. Its key-choice discipline remains the cheapest way to avoid it.
  • Quorum: how modern systems make participants and coordinators crash-proof. The Spanner approach.

12. TL;DR

ProblemOne logical operation spanning two machines has a gap where a crash strands half-committed state: money vanished, or doubled: because cross-machine all-or-nothing must be built, not assumed.
MechanismPrepare: every participant does the work, durably logs readiness, holds locks, and votes: a binding promise. Commit: unanimous yes triggers commit everywhere; anything else aborts everywhere. The coordinator's log holds the verdict.
CostsBlocking (in-doubt participants freeze with locks held when the coordinator is silent), two round trips plus fsyncs, contention from long lock holds, and availability that multiplies down per participant.
Skip it whenBetween microservices, across organizations, on hot rows, or for long flows: that is saga territory. Its honest home: short internal transactions, and inside quorum-replicated databases where no single crash blocks the protocol.
30-second answerUnanimous binding yes votes, then a simultaneous commit: true atomicity, paid for in locks and the risk that everyone freezes if the coordinator dies mid-protocol: so services use sagas, and 2PC lives inside replicated databases.
