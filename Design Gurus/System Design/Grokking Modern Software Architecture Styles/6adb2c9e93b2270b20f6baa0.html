Key Takeaways

Know the Vocabulary

This lesson uses the terms below repeatedly. Read them once now and return here when a later section needs one.

  • User-visible behavior: What a user can do and observe in the product, stated without naming any part of the design.
  • Measurable requirement: A requirement stated with a number that decides pass or fail and the conditions under which that number applies.
  • Trace: A record of one request that lists each step it took, such as a database query or a call to an external service, with the time each step took.
  • Load test: A test that sends a planned rate of requests to a copy of the system and measures its latency and errors at that rate.
  • Spike: A short experiment with throwaway code, limited to a few days, that answers one technical question before a decision.
  • Little's law: The rule that the average number of requests in progress equals the arrival rate multiplied by the average time each request takes.
  • Idempotency: The property that processing the same message twice produces the same result as processing it once. An idempotency key, such as the event ID stored in the database, lets a consumer detect and skip a repeat.
  • Webhook: An HTTP call that an external service makes to your server to report an event, such as a completed charge.
  • Change scenario: One specific future change, used to estimate the work each design would need.
  • Failure scenario: One specific fault, used to find out what each design does when it happens.
  • Revisit condition: A measurable condition, written in the decision record, that reopens the decision when it becomes true.

It is a Friday evening, and the payment provider that the food-delivery app uses has slowed down. For 38 minutes, most of its charges take about 8 seconds, and some take more than 10. Customers who tap Place Order see a long spinner, often followed by an error, and many tap again. By Monday, 240 customers have reported a charge for an order that never reached a restaurant.

At the Monday meeting, three engineers bring three proposals: make payments asynchronous, raise the payment timeout from 10 to 30 seconds, or move Payments into its own service. Each proposal starts from a design. None starts from what the customer needed on Friday evening.

The Constraints and Trade-offs lesson taught a five-step method to compare options and record the result. That method is only as good as its inputs. This lesson covers the inputs and the comparison: behavior, measurable requirements, evidence, scenario walks, and assumptions that someone can check.

Part 1: Start from Behavior, Not from a Design

A proposal that names a design has skipped a step. "Make payments asynchronous" does not say which problem it solves. Nobody can tell whether a smaller change would solve the problem too, or whether this one solves it at all.

The first step is to write down what each kind of user needs to do and observe, without naming any part of the system. With support and the product owner, the team writes five statements for checkout.

  1. Customer: when I tap Place Order, the app answers at once and tells me what is happening.
  2. Customer: I find out quickly whether my order went through.
  3. Customer: I am never charged twice, and never charged for an order the restaurant does not receive.
  4. Restaurant: I only cook orders that have been paid for.
  5. Every user: when payment has a problem, the rest of the app keeps working.

None of the statements mentions a queue, a service or a timeout. Several designs can meet each one, so all stay open until the evidence chooses.

Behavior statements also expose disagreements early. The product owner reads "quickly" in statement 2 as "before the customer gives up". One engineer assumed it meant "in the same response". Only a number settles that.

Part 2: Measurable Requirements and Constraints

From Behavior to Measurable Requirements

The Quality Attributes lesson showed how to write a quality requirement as a scenario with a response measure. A decision needs one per behavior statement, and the most important part of each is its environment: the conditions under which the number must hold.

Every design is fast on a normal evening, so a requirement that ignores a slow provider cannot separate the designs. The table below turns the five statements into requirements. All figures in this lesson are illustrative. Three months have passed since the Dispatch decision in the Constraints and Trade-offs lesson. The second city is live, and the dinner peak has doubled to about 2 orders per second.

RequirementTargetToday
R1: Place Order responsep99 under 500 ms at the peak, also while the provider is slow380 ms normally; 10-second timeouts when slow
R2: Final result reaches the customer99 percent within 3 seconds normally, within 30 seconds when slow0.4 seconds normally; an error when slow
R3: Payment mismatches repairedEach found and repaired within 1 hour840 in 90 days, found after 2 days on average
R4: Only paid orders reach restaurantsZero unpaid orders sentMet
R5: Menu and tracking responsep99 under 300 ms while the provider is slow or downMenu p99 of 9 seconds when slow

Every target value needs a source. R1 reuses the 500 ms target that the Dispatch decision accepted. R2's 3 seconds comes from checkout analytics, which show abandonment rising sharply once the spinner passes 3 seconds. R3's 1 hour comes from support, which can refund a customer within that time. A target that nobody can explain is a guess, and should be labeled as one.

The Constraints

The constraints come next, because they remove designs before any comparison starts.

  • Provider: The contract runs for 21 more months. The provider accepts an idempotency key on each charge, keeps it for 24 hours, and sends a webhook when a charge completes.
  • Team: The team that owns Payments and Accounts has 5 engineers, and nobody in the company has operated a message broker in production.
  • Installed apps: Every Customer app version in use understands only two order results, "confirmed" and "failed". Old versions stay installed for weeks: after the last two releases, they took 8 and 11 weeks to fall below 5 percent of active customers.
  • Budget: Infrastructure, including the Dispatch worker machines, costs about 10,000 limit.

The installed apps are the easiest constraint to miss: any design that adds a new order state must still give old versions an answer they understand.

Part 3: Gather Evidence Instead of Opinions

Every proposal in the meeting rested on a belief, such as the belief that a longer timeout would let slow charges finish. Arguments between beliefs tend to favor the most confident speaker. The team writes down the question behind each belief and answers it from evidence.

Five Sources of Evidence

Production metrics show how much and how often. Over 90 days, the provider's p99 was 250 ms on normal evenings. In three slow periods, of 15, 22 and 38 minutes, its p50 was about 8 seconds and its p99 above 10 seconds.

A trace shows why. The diagram below shows one failed order from the 22-minute period, next to the provider's record of its charge.

The order insert took 40 ms, and the request then waited on the provider. At 10 seconds, the timeout fired, the transaction rolled back and the customer saw an error. The provider finished the charge at 11.3 seconds. The 150 double charges have a related cause: with no idempotency key, the provider treated a second tap as a new payment.

Incident history shows what the failures cost. Across the three slow periods, 690 customers were charged without an order and 150 were charged twice. About 4,500 customers, half of those who tried to order in those periods, gave up without placing an order. In the longest period, menu pages also slowed to a p99 of 9 seconds, although menus never call the provider.

A load test shows whether an explanation is right. The 6 web servers run 10 worker threads each, 60 in total. Each thread handles one request at a time. By Little's law, the busy threads equal the request rate times the average time a request holds a thread. At the dinner peak, the servers receive about 1,500 requests per second in total, mostly courier location updates, order tracking and menu pages. These take about 20 ms each, so they keep 1,500 x 0.02 = 30 threads busy.

Place Order is a small share of that traffic: about 2 requests per second at 0.2 seconds each on average, or 0.4 threads. In the slow periods, many customers tapped again, so Place Order requests rose to about 5 per second. At about 8 seconds each, they needed 5 x 8 = 40 threads, while the other requests still needed 30. That is 70 threads, and only 60 exist. The diagram below compares a normal evening with a slow period.

The team then ran a load test on a staging copy (a copy of the system used only for testing). A stub (a stand-in for the provider) answered after 8 seconds. With the normal peak traffic plus 5 order requests per second, all 60 threads were busy within 8 seconds. Menu p99 rose from 180 ms to 9.2 seconds, so the cause is known rather than suspected.

A spike answers a question that no existing data covers. Two engineers spend 3 days testing the provider's keys and webhooks in its test mode. A repeated key returned the original result in 200 of 200 tries. Webhooks arrived with a p99 delay of 4 seconds, and 3 of 600 arrived twice. A throwaway worker delivered the result to a test app in 1.6 seconds at p99.

Evidence Removes Options and Creates Them

The evidence settles two of the three proposals before any comparison. The threads run out while most charges take 8 seconds, so a 30-second timeout cannot free any of them. It only lets the slowest charges hold a thread three times longer. Rerun with that timeout, the load test left menu p99 above 9 seconds. A Payments service would not help either. The module uses about 3 percent of web server CPU, and a service would still wait on the same provider. The problem is waiting, not computing.

The evidence also produces an option that nobody proposed. The trace shows two gaps behind the wrong charges: no idempotency key, and a rollback after a timeout. The load test shows a third: no limit on how many requests may wait on the provider. Closing the three gaps inside the current design becomes Design A.

Part 4: Two Designs and Seven Dimensions

The Two Designs

Design A: harden the synchronous charge. Place Order still calls the provider and waits, with three changes. First, every charge carries the cart ID and an attempt number as its idempotency key. The server creates the cart before checkout, so a second tap finds the existing order and resends the same key. The provider returns the first result. A retry after a declined card uses the next attempt number, so the provider treats it as a new charge. Second, the timeout drops to 3 seconds. Each server allows at most 3 provider calls at a time, 18 in total, so other requests always keep at least 7 threads on every server. A request over the limit is told at once to try again.

Third, an order whose charge times out is saved as payment_unknown instead of being rolled back. The app receives "failed" with the message "Payment not confirmed yet; we will notify you", which every app version can show. Every 5 minutes, a job compares our payment records with the provider's and repairs each mismatch (a reconciliation job). It looks up each unknown charge by its key, confirms or cancels the order, and a push message tells the customer.

Design B: charge in a background payment worker. Place Order saves the order as payment_pending and answers in about 150 ms without calling the provider. Worker processes from the same codebase, on two new machines, charge pending orders oldest first, up to 40 at a time. The provider's response or webhook moves each order to confirmed, which sends it to the restaurant, or to payment_failed. If a worker stops in the middle of a charge, the next attempt sends the same key, so the provider never charges twice.

The Customer app shows a "Confirming payment" screen and checks the order status every second; after a declined card, the customer returns to checkout. If the provider keeps returning errors, the worker stops retrying after 20 seconds and marks the order payment_failed. Orders still pending after 15 minutes are canceled, and a charge that completes on a canceled order is refunded. B uses the orders table as its list of work, so it needs no message broker.

Both designs need the idempotency key and reconciliation. The diagram below shows the request path of each design.

Seven Dimensions for the Comparison

The team compares the designs on seven dimensions. Four restate quality attributes: change effort is changeability, latency is performance, failures cover availability, and cost is cost. The other three are added because decisions often fail on them.

DimensionMeasured as
Change effortEngineer-days and modules touched per change scenario
CorrectnessScenarios that end with our records not matching the provider's
Latencyp99 of each wait users see, normally and while the provider is slow
FailuresWhat users see in each failure scenario, and what else stops
ReleasesDeployables and app releases needed, and their order
OwnershipTeams involved, and new alerts and on-call duties
CostEngineer-weeks to build and dollars per month to run

A design can be fast and available while still charging the wrong customer. Releases and ownership decide whether a change waits for another team or an app store review.

Part 5: Walk Scenarios Through Each Design

A dimension becomes concrete only when it is applied to one specific event. "B is more resilient" is an opinion. "When most charges take 8 seconds, B confirms each order about 9 seconds later, while A leaves most orders unconfirmed for minutes" is a finding. Walking a scenario means following one event through a design and writing down what each part does, what the user sees and what it costs.

Change Scenarios

The table below walks three changes from the product roadmap through both designs.

Change scenarioDesign ADesign B
Add a digital wallet as a payment methodPayments and the Customer app; about 4 daysThe same; about 4 days
Add a tip after deliveryReuses the timeout, the limit and the unknown state for a second kind of charge; about 6 daysA new job type for the worker; about 3 days
Charge only when the restaurant acceptsThe restaurant's accept request waits on the provider; about 7 daysThe accept request creates a worker job; about 4 days

The wallet costs the same in both designs. The other two are charges that no customer waits for, and only B already has a place for that work.

Failure Scenarios

What the Walk Found

The walk produced six findings that neither design's description stated.

  1. A is not as small as it looked. In F1, its unknown state and later result form an asynchronous path.
  2. B adds a failure that A lacks. F4 needs an alert and an owner, and its customers wait until the worker returns.
  3. Both need a rule for late charges. In F5, the customer sees a charge and then a refund.
  4. B is slower on a normal evening: 1.6 seconds at p99 in the spike, against 0.4 seconds today. Both meet R2.
  5. B reports a provider outage later: after about 20 seconds, against at most 3 seconds in A.
  6. B needs A's path for old app versions, which cannot show the confirming screen.

The Decision

Design A fails R1 and R2, but only while the provider is slow. Design B meets all five for about 5 more engineer-weeks, an app release and a new failure to watch. The decision therefore depends on what a slow period costs. The product owner supplies the number: about 4,500 lost orders in 90 days at about 27,000 per quarter.

At an illustrative 20,000. Design A would keep some of those orders, but nobody knows how many customers would wait 5 minutes for a push message. Even if A kept half, the other half would cost about $13,500 per quarter, so B's extra cost is repaid within two quarters.

The team chooses B, built in two stages. Stage 1 is Design A in full, takes about 3 engineer-weeks, and fixes the wrong charges and the slow menus for every app version. Stage 2 adds the worker and the confirming screen. If Stage 2 is ever stopped, Stage 1 still meets R3, R4 and R5.

Part 6: Record Assumptions and Revisit Conditions

Assumptions That Can Be Checked

Every decision rests on facts that nobody has proven yet. Design B assumes that customers will wait on the confirming screen. An assumption is useful only if it also says how the team will learn that it is false.

  • Unchecked: Customers will accept the confirming screen.
  • Checkable: Fewer than 2 percent of customers leave the confirming screen before the result, as app analytics will show in the first 2 weeks.

Revisit Conditions Someone Can Check

A revisit condition is checkable when it has five parts: the metric, the threshold, the time window, the data source and the owner who checks it. The diagram below shows the five parts in one condition from this decision.

The table below rewrites three weak conditions as checkable ones.

Weak conditionCheckable condition
Revisit if the worker causes problemsOldest pending order over 60 seconds for more than 5 minutes, more than twice in 30 days (pending-age alert; Payments team)
Revisit if customers dislike the screenOver 2 percent leave the confirming screen in any week (app analytics; product owner)
Revisit when the old apps are goneOld versions above 5 percent of customers 12 weeks after release (app version dashboard; Ordering team)

A checkable condition can become a dashboard alert, so its owner knows exactly when to reopen the decision.

The Decision Record

This chapter's ADRs add three fields to the form from the Constraints and Trade-offs lesson: Options Considered, Assumptions and Revisit When.

ADR 0011: Charge customers in a background payment worker

Status: Accepted.

Context: In 90 days, the provider had three slow periods in which most charges took about 8 seconds. Place Order waited up to 10 seconds, so 840 customers were charged wrongly, about 4,500 orders were lost and menus slowed to 9 seconds. At the dinner peak of about 2 orders per second, even while the provider is slow, we need Place Order p99 under 500 ms. We also need a result within 30 seconds and normal menus. Constraints: no broker experience, and old app versions that stay in use for 8 to 11 weeks. The load test ruled out a longer timeout, and CPU data ruled out a Payments service.

Options Considered: (A) Harden the synchronous charge: about 3 engineer-weeks, but orders stay unconfirmed for up to 5 minutes while the provider is slow. (B) A background payment worker: orders are confirmed during slow periods, but it needs about 8 engineer-weeks, an app release and an alert for a stopped worker.

Decision: Stage 1, option A in full (cart ID and attempt number as the key, 3-second timeout, at most 18 provider calls, reconciliation), ships to all app versions within 3 weeks. In Stage 2, Place Order saves each order as payment_pending, and workers on two machines charge it, at most 40 at a time. An order fails after 20 seconds of provider errors, and is canceled after 15 minutes pending, with any later charge refunded. Old app versions stay on Stage 1. The Payments team owns the worker and its alert.

Consequences: Place Order p99 falls to about 150 ms, and orders are confirmed during slow periods. A normal result takes 1.6 seconds instead of 0.4, and an outage is reported after 20 seconds instead of within 3. Two payment paths exist for about 12 weeks. Payments gains an on-call duty. The cost is about 8 engineer-weeks and $300 per month.

Assumptions: Fewer than 2 percent of customers leave the confirming screen (app analytics, first 2 weeks). Webhooks arrive within 60 seconds for 99.9 percent of production charges (webhook delay metric, first 30 days). Old versions fall below 5 percent within 12 weeks (app version dashboard). The provider accepts 40 calls at a time from us, below its documented limit of 100 (confirmed in writing with the provider before Stage 2).

Revisit When: The oldest pending order is over 60 seconds for more than 5 minutes, more than twice in 30 days (pending-age alert; Payments team). Over 2 percent of customers leave the confirming screen in any week (app analytics; product owner). Any mismatch is older than 1 hour when repaired (daily reconciliation report; Payments team). Old versions are above 5 percent of customers 12 weeks after release (app version dashboard; Ordering team).

FAQs

Q: How much evidence is enough before deciding?

Enough to answer the questions on which the options disagree. Match the effort to the cost of undoing the decision, as the Constraints and Trade-offs lesson explains. Stage 1 is easy to undo, so it could start on the metrics and the trace alone. Stage 2 changes what the apps display, so it waited for the spike.

Q: What if there is no production data, such as for a new feature?

Use the closest evidence: a spike, a load test against a prototype and metrics from a similar feature. Label each target as an estimate, and add revisit conditions that test it soon after launch.

Q: How many scenarios should a comparison walk?

Usually three to five change scenarios and four to six failure scenarios. Take changes from the roadmap and failures from the incident history, then add the failures each design introduces, such as F4.

Q: How do scenario walks relate to the weighted scoring table?

They supply its reasons. The Constraints and Trade-offs lesson asks for a reason beside every score. A scenario result, such as "B confirms orders in about 9 seconds while the provider is slow", is that reason.

Key Takeaways

  • Start from user-visible behavior, written without naming any design, so every design stays open until the evidence chooses.
  • Make each requirement measurable, with a number and its conditions, such as "while the provider is slow".
  • List the constraints before comparing. Installed app versions and contracts remove some designs and reshape others.
  • Replace opinions with evidence from metrics, traces, incident history, load tests and spikes. Evidence can remove proposals and produce better ones.
  • Compare designs on seven dimensions: change effort, correctness, latency, failures, releases, ownership and cost.
  • Walk concrete change and failure scenarios through each design to find costs that descriptions hide, such as A's unknown state and B's stopped worker.
  • Write assumptions with the way to check them, and revisit conditions with a metric, a threshold, a time window, a data source and an owner.
