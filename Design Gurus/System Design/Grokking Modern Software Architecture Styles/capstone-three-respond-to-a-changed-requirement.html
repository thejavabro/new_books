Key Takeaways

Know the Vocabulary

This capstone relies on the terms below, each defined in an earlier lesson and repeated here with the same wording.

  • Modular monolith: One deployable application whose code is split into modules by business capability, each with its own interface and its own data.
  • Main requirement: The one measurable target a review is run to protect, such as a checkout p95 under 800 ms at the dinner peak.
  • Revisit condition: A measurable condition, written in the decision record, that reopens the decision when it becomes true.
  • Change coupling: The tendency of two files or modules to be changed in the same commits again and again.
  • Release contention: Changes from different teams waiting for one another because they ship, and roll back, in the same release.
  • Read replica: A copy of the database that streams every change from the primary and answers read queries.
  • Feature flag: A runtime switch that turns a code path on or off for some users without a new deployment.
  • Saga pattern: A sequence of local transactions in separate services, where each step has a compensating action that undoes it on failure.
  • Expand-migrate-contract: A way to change a schema without downtime: add the new structure, move code and data to it over several deployments, and remove the old structure last.
  • Idempotency: The property that processing the same message twice produces the same result as processing it once. An idempotency key, such as the event ID stored in the database, lets a consumer detect and skip a repeat.

Nine months after launch, the learning platform from the Capstone Two lesson in this chapter serves about 38,000 learners. In one week, four change requests arrive with evidence from production, and two of them seem to need a new service.

You answer one of them with the methods of this chapter. You classify it, compare retaining the design with a targeted change against a structural change, and plan each stage so that it can be undone or repaired. You produce a decision record, revised diagrams, migration steps and a rollback or repair plan.

The Scenario

Capstone Two's design runs as written. Nine modules share one Python codebase, which runs from one image as api and worker processes. One PostgreSQL database holds one schema per module. Reporting reads other modules' data through published views, and the worker claims jobs from platform.jobs in two lanes, default and reports.

Growth has followed the first-year forecast. The table below compares launch with today. All figures in this lesson are illustrative.

After a scoring defect reached production in month 3, the team adopted a release train: a fixed schedule of releases, each shipping everything merged since the last one. Releases go out on Tuesday and Thursday after a 3-hour manual check in staging. The team also holds a release freeze, a period in which no change may ship, for 3 working days before each large course launch.

The driving requirement is to change a running system without breaking it. Capstone Two's targets apply during every stage, and the team has about 10 engineer-weeks for architecture work next quarter.

What You Are Given

You are given Capstone Two's reference design as written, including ADRs 0041 to 0043, and the four change requests below with their evidence.

Request 1: Slow Certificates

The support lead writes: "Learners who pass on Monday morning wait up to an hour for their certificate." The Capstone Two target is a PDF ready and emailed within 5 minutes for 95 percent of learners, which the team measures per day.

  • Metrics, last 4 weeks: pass-to-email p95 is 2.1 minutes from Tuesday to Sunday and 44 minutes on Mondays. The oldest waiting certificate job passed 5 minutes on 4 days last month, all Mondays.
  • One trace, Monday 09:12: the render job was added at 09:12:04 and claimed at 09:51:38. Rendering took 3.2 seconds, the same p95 as at launch. The email was sent at 09:53:05, 41 minutes after the pass.
  • Jobs table, Monday 09:01: 28,400 jobs waited in the default lane. Of these, 28,000 were digest emails added at 09:00 by notifications.send_weekly_digest, a job scheduled since month 6.
  • Worker and email service: the worker grew from 1 to 3 tasks at 09:06, but sends stayed at about 10 per second. From 09:00 to 09:50, 41 percent of send attempts got HTTP 429 (too many requests). The account's plan allows 10 sends per second.

Request 2: Separate Billing Releases

The two Billing engineers write: "Billing needs to ship on its own schedule." The evidence covers the last 90 days.

  • Release log: 25 train releases and 6 hotfix releases. A hotfix release ships the last released commit plus the fix, after the full staging check. Each took about 4 engineer-hours, and 5 of the 6 were Billing fixes.
  • Billing changes: 34 Billing pull requests, with a median of 2.6 days from merge to production. Eleven waited more than 4 days: 7 behind freezes and 4 behind rollbacks.
  • Rollbacks: 4 train releases were rolled back. Three of them, caused by Learning and Catalog defects, also removed 4 working Billing changes.
  • Coming dates: the payment provider retires the platform's API version in 10 weeks and changes the new version every two weeks. A subscription-cancellation rule in one market takes effect in 8 weeks, inside a planned freeze.
  • Coupling: 30 of the 34 Billing pull requests changed only Billing. At the peak, Billing uses about 3 percent of api CPU and 4 percent of database CPU, and no module calls Billing.

Request 3: Reporting Pressure

The on-call engineer and the finance team write: "Reports slow down the site every hour." The evidence covers the last 30 days.

  • Latency: at the evening peak, from 18:00 to 22:00 UTC, the API p95 is 240 ms. In the first 10 minutes of each hour it reaches 610 ms, against a 300 ms target.
  • Database: CPU is 48 percent at the peak and 81 percent in those 10 minutes.
  • Query statistics: pg_stat_statements, a PostgreSQL extension that records the total time each query uses, shows reporting.refresh_course_stats taking 52 percent of database time in those minutes. The job recomputes 30 days of figures from v_enrollments and v_course_progress every hour. It runs 9 minutes at the peak and 3 minutes at 04:00.
  • Finance export: a 12-month revenue export from v_payments ran at 19:20 on the 1st for 14 minutes. Checkout start p95 rose to 3.1 seconds, against its 2-second target.
  • Instructor dashboards: get_course_report has a p95 of 35 ms, because it reads stored report tables.

Request 4: Frequent Cross-Feature Changes

The product manager writes: "Every learning feature now touches three modules and takes twice as long to review." The evidence covers the last 90 days.

  • Commit history: 58 of 214 merged pull requests (27 percent) changed three or more modules.
  • Change coupling: 41 of the 58 changed Learning, Assessments and Certificates, for features such as course versions and an 80 percent completion threshold for short lessons. Eleven built team accounts across Accounts, Enrollment and Notifications, and 6 changed other modules.
  • Review time: a median of 2.4 days for a three-module pull request and 0.8 days for a one-module one.
  • Code: the course completion rule exists in three places. Learning computes progress, and Assessments checks for 100 percent before unlocking the final assessment. Since month 2, certificates.render_pdf checks progress and the passed attempt again.
  • Import graph: Certificates now calls Learning and Assessments, two calls that Capstone Two's boundary table lacks. The second forms a cycle with Assessments.
  • Defects: after the 80 percent change in month 7, the render job's copy still required 90 percent. Twenty-three certificate jobs failed 8 times and were marked dead.

Your Task

Pick one request and work it in full. First classify all four briefly, because a request that looks structural can turn out to be a traffic issue. Keep Capstone Two's names, and state every change as a change to its design.

Deliverables

Write the decision record in the chapter's ADR template, numbered from 0051.

  1. Classification: for each request, traffic or structural issue, the deciding measurement, and the Capstone Two revisit condition it makes true.
  2. Main requirement: one measurable target, plus the Capstone Two targets that the change must not break.
  3. Evidence analysis: the cause the evidence shows, and any measurement you would still collect.
  4. Options: the retained design with a targeted change against at least one structural change, on the seven dimensions of the decision process lesson.
  5. Decision record: an ADR, stating which Capstone Two ADR it supersedes or amends, if any.
  6. Revised diagrams: each Capstone Two view that changes, plus a runtime trace if a request path changes.
  7. Migration steps: stages from today's design to the new one, each with its exit check.
  8. Rollback or repair plan: per stage, how to go back, how long it takes, the data at risk, and the point after which only repair works.

Complete the task before you read the reference solution, and write each deliverable down. Expect about 30 minutes for the classification and two to three hours for the full request.

Reference Solution

Step 1 classifies all four requests. Steps 2 to 8 work Request 2 in full, because it needs the largest change, and Step 9 condenses the other three.

Step 1: Classify the Four Requests

Ask whether a problem changes with load before you ask how to fix it. The Incremental Evolution and Strangler Fig lesson in this chapter separates a traffic issue, a shortage of capacity in a sound design, from a structural issue in how code, data or teams are divided. The table below applies its questions.

Request 1 is a traffic issue, although more worker tasks did not help. The limits are the email service's 10 sends per second and the single first-in, first-out lane in front of it, so extra tasks made more attempts, not more sends.

A true revisit condition reopens a decision; it does not choose the answer. Three conditions of ADR 0041 are true, yet only one leads to a new deployable. Request 3 also exposes a weak condition: Reporting used 52 percent of database time in the refresh minutes, but about 13 percent averaged over the peak hour. "Over half at peak" was true or false depending on the window, which is why the decision process lesson in this chapter asks for one.

Step 2: Write the Main Requirement for Billing

State what Billing's releases must achieve, then the targets the change must keep. The main requirement, in the sense of the Architecture Reviews and Common Failure Patterns lesson in this chapter, reads as follows.

A merged Billing change reaches production within 1 working day, even during a freeze of the rest of the platform. No rollback of another module's change reverts a Billing change.

Three Capstone Two targets act as guards. No confirmed payment is ever left without access, 99 percent of payments grant access within 1 minute, and checkout start keeps its p95 of 2 seconds.

Step 3: Read the Evidence

The waits come from three mechanisms, and none depends on load. The train makes every change wait up to 3 working days, freezes held 7 changes, and other modules' rollbacks removed 4. This is release contention, and Billing's 3 percent of api CPU shows that capacity plays no part.

The evidence also shows that a split is feasible, and where it costs. Thirty of the 34 Billing pull requests changed only Billing, and no module calls Billing. In the other direction, Billing calls get_user, has_access, grant_access, revoke_access and queue_email, and the HTTP layer calls authenticate before any request reaches Billing. Most important, the webhook runs grant_access, Enrollment's own code, in the transaction that marks the payment paid. That transaction guarantees that no confirmed payment is left without access.

The team collected two missing measurements. An internal HTTP call between two tasks takes about 5 ms at p99, and 2 of the roadmap's 30 Billing changes for the next quarter need a change in another module.

Step 4: Compare Retaining the Design with Separating Billing

Option A retains the design with targeted changes. Releases go out daily, each Billing change sits behind a feature flag, and a Billing fix during a freeze goes out on a release branch.

Option B separates Billing as its own deployable on the shared PostgreSQL server. Billing gets its own image, pipeline and tasks from the same repository, keeps its schema, and calls Accounts and Enrollment through internal HTTP endpoints.

Option C extracts Billing with its own database and a message broker, the full extraction that the Incremental Evolution and Strangler Fig lesson plans, with data movement and a rollback limit at the first write.

The table below compares the options on the seven dimensions of the decision process lesson in this chapter. Its Releases row walks two scenarios: a webhook change during a freeze and a rollback forced by a Learning defect.

DimensionA: retain, with daily releases, flags and release branchesB: Billing deployable, shared PostgreSQLC: Billing service, own database and broker

CorrectnessPayment and access in one transactionPayment and grant job in one transactionOutbox to a broker; reports need copied data
LatencyUnchangedCheckout start adds about 15 ms; access 1 to 3 seconds laterAs B, plus the broker's delay
FailuresUnchangedWith the api down, checkout fails but webhooks succeed; the database is still sharedAdds the broker as a failure point

CostAbout 1.5 engineer-weeks, plus 9 engineer-hours per week of staging checksAbout 6 engineer-weeks and $80 per month, staging includedAbout 14 engineer-weeks and $200 per month

Option A fails the main requirement. A daily train runs the 3-hour staging check three more times a week, and freezes and rollbacks still hold Billing. A flag also cannot cover a schema migration or a new webhook format, the two kinds of change the provider deadline brings.

Option C meets the requirement but not the constraints. It costs 14 engineer-weeks against a budget of 10, nobody on the team has run a broker, and the requirement never asked to move payment data.

Option B keeps the payment guarantee, but not through the old transaction. Enrollment's grant_access now runs in another process and cannot join Billing's transaction. Instead, the webhook's transaction marks the payment paid and adds an enrollment.grant_access job through the job runner's enqueue function. The platform.jobs table serves as the outbox and the api worker as the relay, as in Event-driven Architecture.

The flow becomes a two-step saga in the sense of Microservices Architecture: Billing's local transaction, then Enrollment's. The grant step retries until it succeeds, which is safe because grant_access is idempotent by the payment ID. Its compensating action, a refund, is needed only for a grant that can never succeed.

Option B gives release independence for code, not for data or failures. Four couplings remain.

  • Schema: v_payments remains a contract with Reporting. Only Billing's pipeline migrates the billing schema, by expand-migrate-contract, so neither image meets a schema it cannot read.
  • Connections: both images share one connection limit, so each Billing task's pool is capped.
  • Failures: a database outage stops Billing and the api together, so the split adds no availability.
  • Calls: checkout start calls the api three times over HTTP, so it fails while the api is down.

Shared data and synchronous calls are the two causes of a distributed monolith, a system split into services that must still be changed and deployed together. Billing avoids it while few changes cross the boundary: 4 of 34 last quarter and 2 of 30 on the roadmap. ADR 0051's first revisit condition watches that share.

Step 5: Record the Decision

ADR 0051 amends ADR 0041 instead of superseding it. Most of ADR 0041 still holds: one repository, one database, one schema per module, the import check and the published views. Superseding it would retire those rules for the other eight modules too. ADR 0041 therefore stays Accepted and unedited, and the ADR index lists ADR 0051 beside it. ADR 0042 also stays Accepted.

ADR 0051: Release Billing as a separate service that shares the PostgreSQL server

Status: Accepted.

Context: This record amends ADR 0041 for Billing only and adds an access lane to ADR 0043's worker. Billing waited for the shared release 3 or 4 times in each of the last three months, against ADR 0041's limit of 2: 7 changes behind freezes and 4 reverted by other modules' rollbacks. The provider retires our API version in 10 weeks, and a cancellation rule takes effect inside a freeze in 8 weeks. Thirty of 34 Billing pull requests changed only Billing. A confirmed payment must grant access within 1 minute for 99 percent of payments and must never be lost.

Options Considered: (A) Daily releases, flags and release branches: about 1.5 engineer-weeks, but freezes and rollbacks still hold Billing. (B) A Billing deployable on the shared PostgreSQL server: Billing ships alone, but access follows payment by seconds and the database stays shared. (C) Its own database and a broker: full isolation, but about 14 engineer-weeks and a broker nobody has run.

Decision: Billing gets its own image and pipeline from the same repository. It runs as billing-api, 2 to 4 tasks of 0.5 vCPU and 1 GB across two zones, and billing-worker, 1 task of the same size that claims only the billing lane. The load balancer sends /checkout, /prices and /webhooks/payments to billing-api. Its own database user writes only the billing schema and platform.jobs, with at most 10 connections per task, and only Billing's pipeline migrates that schema. Billing calls Accounts and Enrollment through internal endpoints with a 1-second timeout. In the payment's transaction, the webhook adds enrollment.grant_access, enrollment.revoke_access and notifications.queue_email jobs, which the api worker runs in a new access lane of 2 slots. Freezes of the api image do not apply to Billing. A build check fails on any import between Billing and the other eight modules.

Consequences: Billing ships on any working day in about 45 minutes, after its automated payment tests pass in staging, and other modules' rollbacks no longer revert it. Access arrives 1 to 3 seconds after the webhook. Checkout start fails while the api is down, and a database outage still stops both. A new receipt template needs an api release before Billing uses it, and the job row format becomes a contract between two images. The change costs about 6 engineer-weeks and $80 per month, staging included.

Assumptions: Fewer than 1 Billing change in 5 needs an api change, as 4 of 34 did. The access lane grants 99 percent of payments within 10 seconds. Internal calls stay under 50 ms at p99.

Revisit When: More than 1 Billing change in 5 needs an api change in a quarter (pull request labels; Billing engineers). The access delay p99 passes 30 seconds in any week (access-delay metric; on-call engineer). A billing schema change breaks v_payments or an api release twice in a quarter (incident log; Billing engineers). A regulation requires payment data in a separate database.

Redraw only the views that change. The context view stays the same, because users and external services see the same system. The container view and the enroll-and-pay trace change, and the deployment view gains billing-api with 2 to 4 tasks across both zones and billing-worker with 1 task.

It records that time even when a revoke found nothing to revoke, so a late grant is still skipped.

The internal endpoints answer only on the private network. Billing copies the learner's name and email into the checkout session at checkout start, so the webhook needs no call to Accounts.

Billing now reaches authenticate, get_user and the access check over HTTP, and the webhook's transaction adds a grant job instead of granting access. The api worker claims that job about 1 second later, and the polling return page reports "ready" usually within 3 seconds.

Step 7: Plan the Migration

Billing moves in five stages, and each starts only after the previous one passes its exit check. The plan applies the strangler fig pattern as the Incremental Evolution and Strangler Fig lesson extends it. The table below lists the stages, which take about 7 weeks.

StageWeeksWorkExit check
Stage 1: refactor inside the monolith1 to 3Job handlers first; then grants by job behind one flag and HTTP calls behind another; Billing's database user; architecture tests7 days with access p99 under 10 seconds and no paid payment without access
Stage 2: deploy without traffic4Billing image, pipeline and tasksThe payment flows pass in staging against billing-api
Stage 3: route by path4 to 5Weighted routing of /prices, then /checkout, then /webhooks/payments, at 10, 50 and 100 percentEach step through one evening peak, with no payment that reconcile_checkouts has to grant
Stage 4: move the billing lane5Billing's scheduled jobs move to the billing laneReconciliation runs on schedule for 3 days
Stage 5: remove Billing from the api image7Delete Billing code and routes from the api image2 weeks at 100 percent with no rollback trigger

Release the consumer of a job before its producer. The api worker learns to run enrollment.grant_access one release before any code adds such a job. Otherwise a rollback of that release would leave queued grant jobs that the old worker cannot run, retrying for about 4 hours.

A percentage of requests is a safe routing key here, unlike for Dispatch matching. Dispatch matching moved by city, because two matchers with separate courier data could assign one courier twice. Here both implementations run the same code against the same tables and unique keys. A webhook delivered to both is stored once, and two events for one payment meet the same row checks as on two api tasks today.

Both implementations must run the same Billing code from stage 2 to stage 5, so every Billing change deploys through both pipelines from one commit. The team plans only urgent Billing fixes for weeks 4 to 7.

Step 8: Plan Rollback and Repair

Rollback means going back, and repair means fixing forward. A rollback returns the system to the previous version's code and behavior, by changing a flag or a route or by redeploying the previous image. A repair keeps the new design and ships a change that corrects the defect and any damaged data.

A rollback is possible only while three conditions hold. The previous version must still be deployable and still read and write the data in its current form, and nothing outside the system may depend on the new version. Otherwise only repair remains. The table below gives the plan for each stage.

The provider switch in week 8 is the planned end of the rollback window. Once the provider sends webhooks only in the new format, no image from before the switch can verify them, so a defect in the switch is repaired forward, and each fix ships in about 45 minutes. The first contract step in the billing schema ends the window in the same way.

Some effects are never rolled back. A completed charge, a sent email and an access grant that a learner has used are repaired with a compensating action, such as a refund, a correction email or a revoked enrollment.

Four repair tools are in place before routing starts. The billing.reconcile_checkouts job shares the webhook's code, so it adds the grant job for a paid session whose webhook was lost. A new check every 15 minutes re-adds grant jobs for payments paid over 5 minutes ago with no access. Dead jobs can be replayed after a fix, and the provider retries a failed webhook for 3 days.

Roll back when the cause is unknown and a rollback is still possible, because it usually takes seconds and needs no diagnosis. Repair when a rollback is no longer possible, or when the cause is known and going back would undo something that depends on the new version.

Step 9: The Other Three Requests, Condensed

Request 1: Retain the Design and Add Worker Lanes

The design is sound, but its lanes no longer fit the load. The traced certificate waited 39.6 of its 41 minutes in a first-in, first-out lane behind 28,000 digest emails. Two lanes were right at launch, with 1,500 emails per day and no digest.

ADR 0052 keeps the worker and splits its lanes. It supersedes ADR 0043 and restates its unchanged parts: the jobs table, the lease, 8 attempts with backoff and idempotent jobs. Each task runs six lanes: default with 3 slots, access with 2 (ADR 0051), certificates with 2, email with 3, bulk with 1 and reports with 1. The claim query limits the bulk lane to 8 sends per second across all tasks, which leaves 2 for receipts and certificates. The digest then takes about 58 minutes instead of 47, which marketing accepts. The change takes about 3 engineer-days and adds no component, and a new revisit condition reopens it if the digest takes over 2 hours.

Two other answers were rejected. A Certificates service or serverless rendering would cost 4 to 6 engineer-weeks, and its email would still wait behind the digest. A plan of 50 sends per second would clear the digest in about 9 minutes, which still breaks the 5-minute target for a learner who passes at 09:01.

The diagram below compares the lanes on a Monday at 09:12, before and after the change.

A lane change also needs a way back. The new lanes are claimed one release before a flag assigns jobs to them, and turning the flag off returns new jobs to the default lane within seconds. Rolling back the worker image needs a repair first, because the old worker ignores the new lanes: one UPDATE moves their waiting jobs back.

Request 3: Retain the Design and Move Report Reads

This is a traffic issue, and the instructor dashboards, which read stored figures in 35 ms, are not its cause. The load comes from building the figures: an hourly job that recomputes 30 days, and staff exports that scan 12 months.

Two targeted changes keep the design. First, a read replica serves the reports lane's reads and the staff exports, while the small writes to the report tables stay on the primary. The replica includes the views, and its lag of about 1 second does not matter for hourly figures. Second, the refresh recomputes only the days whose rows changed, which cut a run from 9 minutes to under 1 on a copy of production. Together they cost about 6 engineer-days and 1,430 per month including staging, under the $1,500 budget.

A Reporting service with its own analytical database, fed by change data capture, was rejected at about 8 engineer-weeks and a pipeline nobody on the team runs. ADR 0053 records the replica and gives the weak condition a window: Reporting queries above 30 percent of primary CPU over any 10 minutes of the evening peak, on more than 3 days a month. Going back points report reads at the primary again, which takes seconds.

Request 4: Refactor Inside the Codebase

This is a structural issue, and its cause is one rule in three places, not the number of modules. The 11 team-account pull requests changed three modules because one feature needed three different jobs done. The 41 learning pull requests edited the same rule three times, and two copies disagreed in month 7.

A targeted change would not remove the copies. A shared test would catch disagreement, but each change would still edit three modules, so the structural change is a refactor that gives the rule one owner. Learning gains get_completion_status, which returns whether a course is complete, its version and the requirements still open. Assessments calls it before unlocking the final assessment, and Certificates stops checking, because its only caller already has.

Two larger answers were rejected. Splitting the three modules into services would turn each of the 41 changes into a coordinated release of three services, a distributed monolith. Merging them would mix scoring with PDF layout, which Capstone Two rejected.

The refactor costs about 2 engineer-weeks, and about 30 of the 41 pull requests would have changed Learning alone. Removing Certificates' two calls ends the cycle, and an architecture test fails the build on any cycle. Each caller moves in its own release, so a redeploy can go back until the old copies are deleted. ADR 0054 records the new owner.

Common Mistakes

  • Treating every true revisit condition as an order to extract. Three conditions of ADR 0041 became true, and one led to a new deployable.
  • Adding instances against a limit outside the system. Three worker tasks still met an email limit of 10 sends per second.
  • Calling across the split after the payment commits. That call is lost if the process stops in between; a job added in the same transaction is not.
  • Routing writes by percentage without checking where the state lives. It is safe for Billing only because both implementations write one database.
  • Assuming that a rollback is always available. A contract step, a provider switch or a sent email ends it.
  • Counting modules instead of copies. One rule copied into three modules caused the defects, not the module count.

Key Takeaways

  • Classify a request before you design an answer. A problem that changes with load is a traffic issue, and one that appears at every hour is structural.
  • A true revisit condition reopens a decision without choosing the answer, and it needs a time window to be checkable.
  • Test the retained design first, then choose the smallest structural change. Two requests needed only a targeted change, and Billing needed its own releases, not its own database.
  • A separate deployable on a shared database gives release independence only. Keep the payment guarantee with a job added in the payment's transaction, and manage the shared schema, connections and failures.
  • Plan rollback and repair for every stage. A rollback needs a deployable previous version, compatible data and no outside dependency on the new version.
  • Amend or supersede ADRs with a new record instead of editing them, and record a retained design as a decision too.
