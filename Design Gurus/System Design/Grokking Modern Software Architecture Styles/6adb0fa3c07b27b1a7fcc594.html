Key Takeaways

Know the Vocabulary

This lesson uses the terms below repeatedly. Read them once now and return here when a later section needs one.

  • Constraint: A limit on how a system may be built that is set outside the design team, such as a budget, a deadline, a law or an existing system that must keep working.
  • Quality attribute: A measurable property of how well a system does its job, such as how fast it responds or how often it is down, as opposed to what the system does.
  • Trade-off: A choice in which improving one quality attribute makes at least one other quality attribute worse, such as faster responses at a higher cost per request.
  • Percentile (p50, p95, p99): The latency that a given share of requests stay at or under; a p99 of 800 ms means 99 percent of requests finish in 800 ms or less.
  • PCI DSS (Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard): An industry security standard, enforced mainly by the card brands through contracts rather than by law, for every business that stores, processes or transmits payment card data.
  • Tokenization: Replacing a card number with a token, a substitute value that has no use outside the payment provider and that only the provider can map back to the real number.
  • Data residency: A requirement that certain data be stored, and sometimes processed, inside a named country or region.
  • Conway's law: The observation that a system's structure tends to copy the communication structure of the organization that builds it.
  • Weighted scoring table: A table that scores each option on each quality attribute and multiplies every score by that attribute's weight, its agreed share of the total importance.
  • Architecture decision record (ADR): A short document that records one architecture decision with its title, status, context, decision and consequences.
  • Reversible decision: A decision that can be undone in days at a small cost, such as moving a background job onto its own machines.
  • Irreversible decision: A decision that would take months or a large migration to undo, such as splitting one database into two.
  • Last responsible moment: The latest point at which a decision can be made before further waiting removes a good option.

It is a weekday evening, and the food-delivery app has slowed down. Between 6 PM and 9 PM, the slowest Place Order requests take more than a second. The Fulfillment team reports that the courier matching code in Dispatch is the cause.

One engineer proposes a separate Dispatch service, another proposes more servers, and a third proposes a smaller change. Each proposal is reasonable, and each would make something else worse.

This lesson explains how such a decision gets made: inside fixed limits, by comparing trade-offs, and with a written record. Every lesson in the Architecture Styles chapter describes its style in the same terms, so this method is how you will compare the styles that follow.

Part 1: Constraints, the Limits You Do Not Choose

Constraints, Requirements and Quality Attributes

Three kinds of input shape every design. A requirement states what the system must do, such as "a customer can follow the courier on a map". A quality attribute states how well it must do it, such as "order placement has a p99 latency of 500 ms or less". A constraint limits how the system may be built, such as "the team has 14 engineers".

The difference is who controls each one. Requirements and quality targets can often be discussed with the product owner. A constraint is set outside the design team, by a budget holder, a regulator, a contract or the existing system. The food-delivery app has the constraints below; the figures are illustrative.

Team Size and Skills

The app is built by 14 engineers in three teams. Ordering (5 engineers) owns Orders, Menu, the Customer app and the Restaurant app. Fulfillment (4 engineers) owns Dispatch, Notifications and the Courier app, and the Payments team (5 engineers) owns Payments and Accounts.

Every engineer knows Python and PostgreSQL well. Nobody has operated a message broker (a server, such as RabbitMQ or Kafka, that receives messages from producers and delivers them to consumers) or several separately deployed services in production. A design that needs a broker is possible, but the team would learn to operate it while customers depend on it.

Budget

The company spends about 10,000. A second region for failover might add $6,000 per month, which exceeds the limit however much availability it adds. Budget also covers people: a new service with its own on-call rotation takes engineering hours away from features.

Deadline

The company has promised restaurants in a second city that ordering opens there in 8 weeks, and the launch will double the dinner-peak load. A design that takes 14 weeks fails this constraint, however good it is. Some dates are targets rather than commitments, so ask who set the date and what would happen if it moved.

Regulation and Industry Standards

The first kind is payment card rules. In most places, PCI DSS is an industry standard rather than a law, but the card brands require it, through contracts, of every business that stores, processes or transmits card data. Its assessment covers each system that handles card numbers and each system that can affect their security. The app uses the payment provider's hosted payment form and tokenization, so card numbers never reach the company's servers, including the Payments module. This reduces the part of the system the assessment covers, but it does not remove the company's obligations.

The second is data residency. Some countries' laws, and some contracts, require certain personal data to stay inside a named country. If the company expands to such a country, the single PostgreSQL database may not be allowed to hold those customers' addresses. Which data a law covers is a legal question, so the architect gets a written statement of the requirement from the legal team.

Existing Systems and Contracts

Every design must work with the systems that already exist. Restaurants rarely update the Restaurant app on their tablets, and about 30 percent run a version more than six months old. The Orders API (application programming interface, the requests a program accepts from other programs) must therefore keep its current endpoints working. Similarly, the payment provider contract runs for 24 more months, so switching providers must wait.

The Organization's Structure and Conway's Law

Melvin Conway observed that organizations design systems whose structure copies their own communication structure, an observation now called Conway's law. In the food-delivery app, three teams will tend to produce three groups of modules, because each team communicates most often within itself. A module owned by both Ordering and Fulfillment conflicts with that structure, because every change needs a meeting between two teams. The team structure is therefore a constraint on module boundaries, and some companies change their teams first so that the architecture they want is the one their teams naturally build.

Test each constraint before accepting it. Ask who set it, what evidence supports it and what would change it. "We must use Java" may be a habit, while "card numbers never reach our servers" is a deliberate rule that keeps the compliance scope small.

Part 2: Trade-offs Between Quality Attributes

The Quality Attributes lesson in this chapter defines the five attributes this course measures: changeability, performance, availability, security and cost. An architecture decision almost never improves all five. Usually, the mechanism that helps one attribute is the same mechanism that hurts another.

A cache is the simplest example. The Menu module reads each menu from PostgreSQL on every request, and a customer makes about 30 menu requests for every order placed. Keeping each menu in memory for 60 seconds removes most of those reads, which improves performance and lowers the cost per request. The same 60 seconds is also the cost. When a restaurant marks a dish as sold out, customers can still order it for up to a minute.

The table below lists four decisions in the app and what each one improves and costs.

Decision in the appImprovesMakes worse
Cache menus for 60 secondsPerformance, cost per requestMenus can be 60 seconds out of date
Send notifications through a queueOrder latency; ordering survives a notification outageNotifications can arrive seconds late; one more component to run
Encrypt and log every read of courier locationsSecurityLatency per read; extra work in every feature that uses the data
Split Dispatch into its own serviceChangeability for Fulfillment; independent scalingNetwork calls between Orders and Dispatch; operations cost

Two rules follow. First, an option that seems to improve everything usually has a cost nobody has found yet, most often in operations.

Second, whether a trade-off is good depends on priorities. A menu that is 60 seconds old is acceptable, but a payment status that is 60 seconds old is not. That is why the method below ranks the attributes before it compares any options.

Part 3: A Step-by-Step Way to Make a Decision

The Five Steps

The method has five steps, and the order matters. The team fixes the attributes and their weights before anyone scores an option. This prevents the most common failure: choosing a favorite first and then picking weights that make it win.

  1. State the problem and the quality attributes that matter most, with numbers.
  2. List two or three real options that the team could build and operate.
  3. Score each option in a weighted scoring table.
  4. Check the highest-scoring option against the constraints; if it fails one, check the next.
  5. Decide, and record the decision.

The diagram below shows the five steps, with the return path from step 4 when the leading option fails a constraint.

Step 1: State the Problem

Between 6 PM and 9 PM, the app takes about 1 order per second, and Dispatch runs its matching loop every 5 seconds, comparing about 300 waiting orders with about 500 available couriers. The loop runs inside the processes that serve web requests and uses about 60 percent of their CPU at the peak. As a result, order placement p99 latency rises from 350 ms to 1,200 ms, against a target of 500 ms.

The second-city launch in 8 weeks will double the peak load. Separately, Fulfillment wants to release Dispatch daily, but the shared deployment runs twice a week.

Four quality attributes matter here: performance, changeability, availability and cost. Security is left out because the three options change it very little, and an attribute on which every option scores the same adds nothing to the comparison.

Step 2: List Two or Three Real Options

One option is not a decision, and ten options cannot all be examined carefully. The team lists three.

  • Option A, scale the monolith: Grow from 6 to 14 application servers during the peak. Every server still runs every module, including the matching loop.
  • Option B, a separate worker pool: Keep Dispatch in the same codebase and deployable, but run the matching loop as its own process on 3 dedicated worker machines. The web servers no longer run it, and it still uses the shared database.
  • Option C, a Dispatch service: Move Dispatch into its own codebase with its own pipeline and database, reached by Orders over the network. The Microservices Architecture lesson describes what running such services involves.

Step 3: Score the Options in a Weighted Table

The team gives each attribute a weight, with the weights adding up to 100. It scores every option from 1 (poor) to 5 (excellent) on each attribute, multiplies each score by the weight, and adds the products, so the maximum is 500.

Quality attributeWeightA: scale the monolithB: separate worker poolC: Dispatch service
Performance (order p99 at peak)352 x 35 = 704 x 35 = 1405 x 35 = 175
Changeability (Fulfillment releases)252 x 25 = 503 x 25 = 755 x 25 = 125
Availability (ordering survives a matching fault)202 x 20 = 404 x 20 = 804 x 20 = 80
Cost (infrastructure and engineering time)202 x 20 = 404 x 20 = 802 x 20 = 40
Total (out of 500)100200375420

The team writes a reason beside every score.

  • Performance: A scores 2 because every server still runs the loop. B scores 4 because the loop leaves the web servers but shares the database. C scores 5 because matching leaves both.
  • Changeability: A scores 2 because nothing changes. B scores 3 because matching gets one clear entry point but keeps the shared releases. C scores 5 because Fulfillment can release daily.
  • Availability: A scores 2 because a matching bug that uses all the CPU still slows every order. B and C score 4: both isolate matching from the web servers, but B shares a database and C adds a network call that can fail.
  • Cost: A adds about 700 (score 4). C adds about $1,200 plus a pipeline, monitoring and an on-call rotation for four people (score 2).

The scores are judgments, not measurements. The table is useful because every judgment is visible and can be challenged. Have two or three engineers score independently, then discuss any score where they differ by 2 or more.

Step 4: Check the Winner Against the Constraints

Option C leads with 420, so the team checks it first.

  • Deadline: Extracting Dispatch is estimated at 14 weeks, and the launch is in 8. C fails.
  • Team skills: Nobody has operated a second production service, and the four Fulfillment engineers would carry its on-call rotation. C fails.
  • Budget and existing systems: The extra 3,000, and the APIs that the Restaurant and Courier apps call stay the same. C passes.

C fails two constraints, so the team checks B. Option B needs about 2 weeks, uses existing skills, adds 2,600 per month and no new skills, would also pass, but it scores lowest. The diagram below shows the three totals and the result of the check.

Scoring before checking constraints shows what the constraints cost. Here the team gives up 45 points (420 minus 375) because of the deadline and its current skills. That number is worth recording, because both constraints are likely to change.

Step 5: Decide

The team chooses Option B now. It also states the conditions that would reopen Option C: Dispatch queries using more than 30 percent of database CPU at the peak, or Fulfillment releases waiting for the shared schedule more than twice in one month. Written conditions replace a vague "maybe later" with a test anyone can run.

Part 4: Architecture Decision Records

What an ADR Is

An architecture decision record is a short document, usually one or two pages, that records one decision. The most common format, introduced by Michael Nygard, has a numbered title and four sections: status, context, decision and consequences. Teams usually store ADRs as numbered Markdown files next to the code, such as docs/adr/0007-dispatch-worker-pool.md, so they are reviewed and versioned like code.

The status is Proposed while the decision is under discussion and Accepted once the team agrees. It becomes Deprecated when the decision no longer applies, or Superseded when a later ADR replaces it. Apart from its status, an accepted ADR is not edited; a changed decision gets a new ADR. The diagram below shows the parts of an ADR and what each holds.

Why ADRs Matter

Code shows what a system does, but not why. A year later, a new engineer will see the worker pool and ask why Dispatch is not a separate service. Without a record, the only answer is the memory of whoever is still on the team.

An ADR answers that question in five minutes, and it stops the same debate from restarting. Writing it also forces clarity: a team that cannot describe the problem in one paragraph with numbers is not ready to decide.

A Complete ADR for the Dispatch Decision

ADR 0007: Run Dispatch matching in a separate worker pool

Status: Accepted.

Context: Between 6 PM and 9 PM, the Dispatch matching loop runs every 5 seconds inside the web processes and uses about 60 percent of their CPU. Order placement p99 latency rises from 350 ms to 1,200 ms against a target of 500 ms.

The second-city launch in 8 weeks will double the peak load. The weighted attributes are performance (35), changeability (25), availability (20) and cost (20). We scored three options out of 500: scaling the monolith (200), a separate worker pool (375) and a Dispatch service (420). The Dispatch service needs about 14 weeks against an 8-week deadline, and no one on the team has operated a second production service.

Decision: We will run the matching loop as its own process on three dedicated worker machines, from the same codebase and deployable as the rest of the application. Web servers will no longer run the loop. Dispatch keeps its tables in the shared PostgreSQL database, and the Courier app API does not change.

Consequences: Order placement p99 latency should fall below 500 ms at double the load, which a load test will confirm before the launch. Infrastructure cost rises by about $700 per month. Dispatch releases still follow the shared schedule, and matching still competes with ordering for the database. We will reopen the Dispatch service option if Dispatch queries exceed 30 percent of database CPU at the peak, or if Fulfillment releases wait for the shared schedule more than twice in a month. Removing the worker pool takes about one day.

An ADR that records only benefits hides the trade-off, which is the part the next engineer most needs.

Part 5: Reversible and Irreversible Decisions

The What Software Architecture Is lesson in this chapter names irreversibility as one sign that a decision is architectural. This part answers a practical question: how much analysis does each kind of decision deserve?

The Dispatch case shows both kinds. The worker pool is reversible, because removing it takes about one day. Moving Dispatch's data into its own database is close to irreversible: once two databases hold different data and other modules call Dispatch's API, merging them back takes months.

AspectReversible decisionIrreversible decision
Example in the appRun matching in a worker poolMove Dispatch data to its own database
Cost to undoAbout one dayMonths of migration
Analysis it deservesHours, and a short ADRDays to weeks, a load test or prototype, and review by affected teams
When to decideAs soon as the evidence is good enoughAt the last responsible moment
Main riskSpending too long on analysisDeciding early on too little evidence

Reversible decisions deserve speed. A week of analysis for a choice that can be undone in a day is wasted, because trying the option gives better evidence.

Irreversible decisions deserve evidence and timing. Decide at the last responsible moment, when more evidence has arrived but before waiting removes a good option. In the Dispatch case, the launch forced a decision now, and Option B was chosen partly because it keeps Option C available.

An irreversible decision can often be split into reversible steps. To extract Dispatch later, the team can first move its tables into their own schema (a named group of tables inside the shared database), with all access through Dispatch's interface. Next, it can run Dispatch as a separate service against that schema, and only then move the schema to its own database. Each step can be undone on its own. The strangler fig migration in the Monolith Architecture lesson applies the same step-by-step idea to code.

Part 6: How the Style Lessons Use This Chapter

An architecture style is a reusable set of decisions, as the What Software Architecture Is lesson explains. Every lesson in the Architecture Styles chapter therefore answers the same three questions about its style:

  1. What does the style give you? The quality attributes it improves.
  2. What does it cost? The attributes it makes worse, including operations cost.
  3. Which constraints does it fit? The team sizes, skills, budgets and deadlines under which it works well.

The first answer supplies the high scores in a weighted table, the second explains the low scores, and the third is the constraint check. The table below previews the answers for four styles.

StyleWhat it gives youWhat it costsConstraints it fits
MonolithSimple deployment, fast in-process calls, one transaction across modulesWhole-application scaling and shared releases as teams growSmall team, short deadline, a domain still being learned
MicroservicesIndependent releases and scaling per serviceNetwork latency, consistency work, high operations costMany teams with strong operations skills
Event-drivenProducers and consumers that do not call each other directlyEventual consistency, harder debuggingTeams able to operate a message broker
ServerlessNo servers to manage, cost that follows trafficCold-start latency, execution time limitsUneven traffic, small operations team

Each row is taught in full in its own lesson. The Architecture Styles chapter begins with Monolith Architecture. Read it with the 14-engineer team from this lesson in mind, and ask which conditions in ADR 0007 would make the next style worth considering.

FAQs

Q: How many quality attributes belong in a weighted scoring table?

Usually three to five: the ones the team cares about and the options affect differently. With more, each weight becomes small, and the few attributes that matter most lose their influence on the result.

Q: Who chooses the weights in a weighted scoring table?

The owning team agrees on them, usually with the product owner. The weights must be fixed before anyone scores an option, or it becomes too easy to adjust them until a favorite wins.

Q: What if two options score within a few points of each other?

A small difference means the table cannot separate them, so gather more evidence, such as a load test or a closer cost estimate. If the options are still close, prefer the more reversible one.

Q: What happens to an ADR when the decision changes?

The team writes a new ADR with the new context and decision, and the old ADR's text stays as it was. Only the old record's status changes, to Superseded with the new ADR's number, so the history shows what changed and why.

Key Takeaways

  • A constraint is a limit set outside the design team: team size and skills, budget, deadline, regulation, existing systems and the team structure, which by Conway's law tends to shape module boundaries. Test each one before accepting it.
  • Almost every architecture decision is a trade-off between quality attributes. An option that seems to improve everything usually has a hidden cost.
  • Decide in five steps: state the problem, list two or three options, score them, check the winner against the constraints, and decide.
  • Fix the weights before scoring and give a reason for every score, so each judgment can be challenged.
  • Record each decision in an ADR, including its costs and the conditions that would reopen it.
  • Match the analysis to the cost of undoing the decision: hours for a reversible one, evidence and the last responsible moment for an irreversible one.
  • Every style lesson that follows answers three questions: what the style gives you, what it costs and which constraints it fits.
