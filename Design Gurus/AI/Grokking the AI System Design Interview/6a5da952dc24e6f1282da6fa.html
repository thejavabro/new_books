Key Takeaways

Check Your Understanding

Know the Vocabulary

This lesson uses the terms below many times. Read them once now, and come back here when a later section needs one.

  • Ingestion: The background pipeline that loads documents into the retrieval system and keeps them current.
  • Connector: A small service that reads documents from one source, such as the help center or the company wiki.
  • Parsing: Turning a file, such as a web page or a PDF, into clean text. Good parsing keeps the headings, lists, and tables intact.
  • Boilerplate: The menus, footers, and banners wrapped around a page's real content.
  • Token: A short word or a piece of a longer word. A chunk of 400 tokens holds about 300 English words.
  • Chunk: A focused passage cut from a document. It is the unit that retrieval finds and returns.
  • Breadcrumb: The trail of headings above a chunk, such as "Expense Policy > Team Events > Meals."
  • Metadata: Facts stored with each chunk, such as its source, its date, and who may read it.
  • Document store: The ingestion system's own record of every document, with its parsed text and its chunks. It sits outside the vector database.
  • Vector index: The searchable copy of every chunk's embedding, held in the vector database.
  • Golden set: A fixed set of real questions, each paired with the passage that answers it. It is used to test retrieval after every change.
  • Recall: Here, the share of golden-set questions whose answering passage appears in the top search results.
  • Idempotent: A job is idempotent when running it twice leaves the system in the same end state as running it once.
  • Tombstone: A marker that records a document as deleted, so that no later sync brings it back.
  • Freshness lag: The time between a change in a source document and the moment that change is searchable.
  • SLO: A service level objective. It is a measured target that a team commits to, such as "99% of edits are searchable within 15 minutes."

Part 1: Retrieval Can Only Find What Ingestion Stored

Embeddings and Vector Databases built the search for the food-delivery company's retrieval service. That lesson assumed the chunks already existed. This lesson builds the part that makes them and keeps them current.

The service holds help articles for customers, policy pages for support agents, and internal pages, such as HR documents, for employees. Together they come to about 10 million chunks, an example assumption carried over from that lesson. Now look at two failures from that service.

Failure 1: The missing answer. An employee asks how much they can spend per person on a team dinner. The answer sits in the expense policy, which is a PDF. The parser turned the policy's table of limits into scattered numbers, so no chunk puts "team dinner" next to its limit. The search works correctly and still finds nothing useful.

Failure 2: The retired answer. Last week, the refund window changed from 30 days to 14 days. The new text was indexed, but the old chunks were never removed. A support agent asks about refunds, and the assistant cites the retired 30-day policy.

Notice that neither failure happened in the search engine or in the language model. Both happened before any question arrived.

That is the main idea of this lesson. In a production RAG system, much of the quality is decided in ingestion. A better model cannot answer from a chunk that is missing, broken, or out of date.

What Interviewers Expect

Many candidates say that they split the documents into chunks and embed them, and then they move on. Interviewers then ask the questions this lesson answers. How do you split a table? How large is a chunk, and why? What happens when a document is edited, and how fast does a deletion take effect?

Part 2: The Ingestion Pipeline

The Definition

Ingestion is the pipeline that loads documents into the system and keeps them current. It runs in the background, apart from the request path. It has six stages, always in this order.

Step 1: Connectors fetch the documents. Each source gets its own connector. The connector learns which documents are new, changed, or deleted, and fetches them.

Step 2: Parse and clean. The parser turns each file into clean text. It keeps the headings, lists, and tables, and it strips the boilerplate.

Step 3: Chunk. The chunker splits the clean text into focused passages. It follows the document's own structure.

Step 4: Enrich metadata. Each chunk gets its breadcrumb, its source link, its dates, its owning team, and who may read it.

Step 5: Embed. The embedding model turns each chunk into a vector. It must be the same model version that embeds the questions.

Step 6: Index. The vectors and their metadata are written into the vector database, where search can find them.

The Document Store

One more component sits beside the six stages. The source systems, such as the wiki and the help center, own the documents. Inside the retrieval system, the document store is the source of truth. It holds the latest version of each document, with its parsed text and its chunks.

The document store lives outside the vector database, for example in object storage and an ordinary database. The vector index is derived from it, which makes the index rebuildable. If the index is lost, or a new embedding model arrives, the team rebuilds it from the document store. Nobody has to fetch every document from every source again.

What Ingestion Is Not

Ingestion is not a one-time load. Documents change every day, so the pipeline runs every day too.

Ingestion is not only embedding. Embedding is one stage of six, and it is often the easiest one.

Ingestion is not the vector database's job. The database stores what it is given, and it never notices on its own that a document was edited or deleted.

Part 3: Parsing Is the Hardest Stage

Each file format breaks in its own way. The table shows the common cases in the company's sources.

FormatWhat goes wrongWhat the parser must do
Web pages, such as help articlesThe same menus and footers repeat on every pageStrip the boilerplate and keep the main content
PDFs, such as the expense policyTables come out as scattered numbers, and two columns merge line by lineUse a layout-aware parser that keeps each table row together
Scanned documentsThere is no text, only a picture of textRun OCR, software that reads text out of images
Wiki pagesUsually clean, but embedded content can vanishCheck what each source's export really contains

Boilerplate matters more than it seems. If every help article starts with the same menu, thousands of chunks share that text. Their vectors move closer to each other, and the signal from the real content gets weaker.

Parsing Quality Is a Monitored Metric

A broken parser does not raise an error. It produces text, only the wrong text. Every later stage runs normally on that text, and every dashboard looks healthy. Format damage, such as a scattered PDF table, is caught only if someone measures it.

So treat parsing quality as a metric you monitor, not something you assume. Three checks do most of the work.

  1. Sample the outputs. Each week, pick a few parsed documents from each source for review.

  2. Score the extraction. A person or a model compares each parsed text with the original. Did the headings, tables, and lists survive?

  3. Count empty or garbled chunks. These are chunks with almost no text, or with mostly symbols. Track the count per source, and alert on a sudden jump, such as one after a parser upgrade.

Images, Diagrams, and Code

Some content is not plain prose. The rule is to acknowledge it, route it, and scope it, rather than ignore it.

  • Acknowledge that it exists. A text pipeline cannot read a picture, and a diagram may hold the only copy of an answer.
  • Route each kind to a handler built for it. Code goes to a code-aware splitter that keeps each function whole. A screenshot of an error message goes to OCR. A diagram can get a written description from a model that reads images.
  • Scope the first version. Decide which kinds are included, and say out loud which ones are out of scope.

Ignoring these content types silently is the real mistake. Questions whose answers sit in a diagram then fail, and nobody knows why.

Part 4: Choosing the Chunk Size

Why Not One Embedding per Document?

Embedding a whole document as one vector seems simpler, but it fails for two reasons.

  1. Mixed topics blur together. An employee handbook covers vacation, expenses, laptops, and security. One vector for all of it is an average of four topics. It sits close to none of them, so a question about any one topic matches it weakly.

  2. Passages need a token budget. The language model that writes the answer is often called the generator. It receives a limited number of tokens of evidence. A 30-page handbook would use the whole budget, and most of it would not relate to the question. The RAG Pipeline explains how that budget is set.

There is also a practical limit. Many embedding models accept only a limited number of input tokens, and they cut off the rest of a long document.

So documents are cut into chunks, and the size of those chunks is a trade-off.

Small Chunks

A small chunk holds one to three sentences, often under 100 tokens. Small chunks embed crisply. Each one covers a single idea, so its vector points clearly at that idea and gives sharp similarity for that topic.

The risk is lost context. Take this paragraph from the expense policy, under the heading "Meals."

"Team dinners need a manager's approval in advance. Book the restaurant through the company travel tool. The limit is $50. Alcohol is not covered."

A small chunker might put "The limit is $50" in a chunk of its own, and retrieval may return that chunk alone. No sentence in it says which limit it is, so it could be for a meal, a hotel, or a gift. The language model cannot answer at all, or it guesses and sounds confident.

Big Chunks

A big chunk holds a thousand tokens or more, such as several pages of a policy. Big chunks keep their context. But they blur the embedding, because several topics get mixed into one vector. They also use more of the token budget for every chunk that is retrieved.

Small chunksBig chunks
Example sizeUnder 100 tokens1,000 tokens or more
EmbeddingCrisp, one topic per vectorBlurred, several topics per vector
ContextOften lostKept
Typical failure"The limit is $50," with no subjectThe right page matches too weakly to be found

A common starting point is 300 to 500 tokens per chunk. That is an example starting point, not a rule.

Pick the Size by Measuring

The right size depends on your documents and your questions, so pick it empirically with a golden set.

Step 1: Build the golden set. Collect a few hundred real questions. For each one, mark the passage in the source document that answers it.

Step 2: Chunk the corpus at several sizes. For example, build one version at 200 tokens, one at 400, and one at 800. Trying a range of values like this is called a sweep.

Step 3: Run the golden questions against each version. A question counts as a hit if one of its top results, for example the top 5, contains the answering passage.

Step 4: Read off the recall. Compare the sizes, and include the cost. Smaller chunks mean more vectors, and more vectors need more memory. Bigger chunks put more tokens into every request to the language model.

Notice that Step 1 marks a passage, not a chunk. Chunk boundaries move with every size, but the answering passage stays the same.

Part 5: Structure-Aware Chunking

Fixed-Size Splitting

The simplest chunker cuts the text every 400 tokens and ignores the document's structure. So it cuts through sentences, lists, and tables. A table row can end up in one chunk while its header row sits in another.

The Production Default

Structure-aware chunking is the production default. It splits along headings and paragraphs, and it never splits in the middle of a table or a list. The rules below are a typical version.

  • Start a new chunk at each heading.
  • Fill the chunk with whole paragraphs until it nears the target size.
  • Keep each table whole. If it is too large, split it by rows, and repeat the header row in every piece.
  • Keep each list whole, together with the sentence that introduces it.
  • When a long section must be split, add overlap. Repeat the last sentence or two of one chunk at the start of the next.

Overlap helps when an idea crosses a chunk boundary. If the idea spans only a sentence or two, the repeated sentences keep it whole in at least one chunk. Overlap also has a cost: the repeated text is stored and embedded twice.

Contextual Enrichment

Structure-aware chunking still leaves part of the "50" on its own.

Contextual enrichment fixes this. Before the chunk is embedded, the pipeline prepends the chunk's breadcrumb, the trail of headings above it. The small chunk now reads as follows.

"Expense Policy > Team Events > Meals. The limit is $50."

Now the vector carries the fact that this limit is about team meals in the expense policy. So does the language model when the chunk reaches it.

Small-to-Big Retrieval

Breadcrumbs add a little context. Sometimes the answer needs much more, such as a whole section of a procedure. Small-to-big retrieval handles that case.

Small-to-big retrieval matches on small chunks but hands the language model the full section. It works in three steps.

Step 1: Index small chunks. Each small chunk stores the ID of the section it came from.

Step 2: Search the small chunks. Their crisp vectors find the best match for the question.

Step 3: Swap in the section. The system looks up the parent section in the document store and puts the full section into the context.

So small-to-big retrieval resolves the size trade-off. Matching gets the sharpness of small chunks, and answering gets the complete context of the full section. The cost is a larger context, so cap the section size. If several matches share one parent section, include that section only once.

Metadata is not decoration. Each field powers a distinct part of the system, which is why each one is worth naming in an interview.

Access-control identifiers record who may read the document, as a list of users or groups. The vector database applies them as a filter during the search, as Embeddings and Vector Databases showed. Permissions change too, and Capstone: Enterprise Document Q&A syncs those changes on their own feed.

Part 7: The Sync Contract

Documents change every day. The sync contract is the set of promises that keeps the index matching the sources, and it has five parts.

1. Incremental Sync From Each Source

Incremental sync processes only the documents that changed. Re-fetching the whole corpus on every cycle is too slow and too costly at 10 million chunks. Keep a full re-crawl for rare cases, such as connecting a new source.

The preferred way to detect changes is a webhook or a change feed from the source system.

  • A webhook is a call that the source makes to your service when a document changes.
  • A change feed is an ordered list of changes that the source keeps. The connector reads the new entries since its last position.

Some sources offer neither. For those, a scheduled diff compares last-modified times or content fingerprints on a schedule. Events can also get lost, so a periodic check that compares each source with the document store catches what the events missed.

2. Idempotent Re-Indexing

A change event starts a re-indexing job for one document. Webhooks are sometimes delivered twice, and failed jobs are retried, so the same job can run more than once.

The job must therefore be idempotent. Running it twice must leave the system in the same end state as running it once. If the job appended new chunks on every run, each repeat would add another copy of the document.

So the job is keyed by the document ID, and it replaces the document's whole chunk set instead of appending to it. Each chunk ID is built from the document ID, the version, and the chunk's position, such as "refund-policy, version 8, chunk 2." A rerun of the same version writes the same IDs again.

3. Atomic Replacement

An updated document's chunk set is replaced with a delete, then an insert, under one document ID, atomically. Atomic means that the two actions happen as one. Readers see the old set or the new set, never a mix.

Look at what happens without it.

  • Insert first, then crash. The new chunks are in, and the old ones were never deleted, so old and new answers both appear.
  • Delete first, then crash. The old chunks are gone, and the new ones never arrived, so the document disappears from search.

Some vector databases cannot run both actions in one transaction. A common way to get the same effect uses the version number. First, write the new chunks as version 8, next to version 7. Then change the document's current version in the document store from 7 to 8, in one write. Search drops any chunk whose version is not current, and version 7 is removed afterward.

The first case above is the classic silent corruption case, a half-updated document. Dashboards look healthy, because there are no errors, latency is normal, and search returns results. But old and new chunks both answer. The assistant says 30 days to one agent and 14 days to the next, and usually a user notices it before any alert does.

4. Deletes Propagate Fast, in Order

A deletion follows a fixed order.

Step 1: Tombstone the document in the document store. This comes first, so that no later sync or rebuild brings the document back. A rebuild reads the document store, and the tombstone tells it to skip the document.

Step 2: Remove its chunks from the index. After this step, retrieval can no longer find them.

Step 3: Clear the caches. Remove any cached answers that quoted the document. If the caches were cleared before the index, a new request could refill them from the index.

Then search for the document's content, confirm that nothing returns, and record the check.

5. Freshness Lag Is an SLO

Freshness lag is the time between a change in the source and that change being searchable. Treat it as an SLO, a target that you measure and alert on. An example target is that 99% of edits are searchable within 15 minutes, and deletions within 5 minutes. These are illustrative numbers, not a guarantee.

This SLO exists to prevent one incident, the assistant citing a policy that was already retired.

To measure it, each change event carries the time of the source change. When the index write finishes, the pipeline records the difference. Some teams also edit a test page on a schedule and time how long search takes to reflect the edit.

Part 8: Follow One Policy Change

The steps below follow the refund policy change through the sync contract. The times are illustrative.

Step 1: The source changes. At 9:00 AM, the policy team edits the refund policy page and changes the window from 30 days to 14 days. The wiki sends a webhook.

Step 2: The connector fetches the page. It saves the page in the document store as version 8. Its content fingerprint differs from version 7, so the job continues.

Step 3: The page is parsed and chunked. The chunker splits it along its headings into four chunks. Each chunk gets its own breadcrumb, such as "Refund Policy > Time Limits."

Step 4: The chunks are enriched and embedded. Each chunk gets its metadata, including version 8 and the support-agents group. The same embedding model version as before turns each chunk into a vector.

Step 5: The chunk set is replaced atomically. At 9:03 AM, the five chunks of version 7 are removed and the four chunks of version 8 are inserted, as one step. Cached answers that quoted version 7 are cleared.

Step 6: The lag is recorded. The change took 3 minutes to become searchable, which is within the 15-minute example target.

At 9:05 AM, a support agent asks about the refund window. Only the 14-day chunks can be found, and the answer cites the page link and the edit time.

Notice that no part of the request path was touched. Notice also what happens if the webhook arrives twice. The second run finds that the fingerprint matches version 8, so it stops. Even if it ran in full, it would write the same chunks under the same IDs, so the end state would not change.

Part 9: Using This in an Interview

The 30-second answer (to "how do the documents get into the system?"): "Ingestion runs in the background in six stages: sync, parse, chunk, enrich, embed, and index. The document store is the source of truth, and the vector index is derived from it, so I can rebuild it. I chunk along headings and paragraphs, never mid-table, and I prepend each chunk's breadcrumb. I use small-to-big retrieval when answers need the full section. I pick the chunk size by testing several sizes on a golden set. Each source syncs incrementally through webhooks or change feeds. Re-indexing is idempotent and replaces a document's chunks atomically. Deletes go to the document store, then the index, then the caches, and freshness lag is an SLO."

Likely follow-ups:

  1. "After an edit, users sometimes see the old answer and the new answer together. Why?" A re-indexing job inserted the new chunks but never removed the old ones. Make per-document re-indexing atomic: delete, then insert, under one document ID.
  2. "How big should a chunk be?" Start around 300 to 500 tokens, then measure. Chunk the corpus at several sizes, run the golden set, and compare recall and memory cost.
  3. "A document was deleted for legal reasons. How fast does it disappear?" As fast as the deletion SLO says, for example within 5 minutes. Tombstone it in the document store, remove it from the index, clear the caches, then search for it and record the result. For a legal deletion, also remove the document's text and chunks from the document store, and keep only the tombstone.

Common Mistakes

Mistake 1: Treating Ingestion as a One-Time Script

Documents change every day, so a pipeline that loaded them once will soon serve retired policies.

Mistake 2: Assuming the Parser Works

A broken parser produces wrong text without an error. Sample its outputs, score them, and count empty or garbled chunks for each source.

Mistake 3: Splitting at a Fixed Size

A fixed-size split cuts through tables and lists. Split along headings and paragraphs instead, and keep every table and list whole.

Mistake 4: Appending Chunks on Every Update

Appended chunks leave old and new versions side by side. Replace each document's whole chunk set atomically, under its document ID.

Mistake 5: Making the Vector Database the Only Copy

An index with no source of truth behind it cannot be rebuilt. Keep the documents and their chunks in a document store outside the vector database.

FAQs

Q: Should every source use the same chunk size?

No. Short help articles and long policy PDFs often work best at different sizes. Test each large source on its own part of the golden set.

Q: Does changing the chunk size mean re-embedding?

Yes. New chunk boundaries mean new chunk texts, and every new text needs a new vector. Plan it like a model change, with a second index that you compare before you switch.

Q: Can I re-crawl everything every night instead of syncing incrementally?

Yes, for a small corpus that changes slowly. At 10 million chunks, a full re-crawl is slow and costly, and every edit waits until the next night. Incremental sync keeps the freshness lag short.

Q: Do breadcrumbs and small-to-big retrieval do the same job?

No. A breadcrumb adds a few words of context to every chunk, which helps matching. Small-to-big retrieval gives the language model the whole section, which helps answering. Many systems use both of them.

Key Takeaways

  • Much of a RAG system's quality is decided in ingestion. Retrieval can find only what ingestion stored, and a better model cannot fix a missing or stale chunk.
  • Ingestion runs six stages in order. Connectors, parse and clean, chunk, enrich metadata, embed, and index.
  • Parsing quality is a monitored metric. A broken parser raises no error, so sample, score, and count empty or garbled chunks.
  • Chunk size is a trade-off, picked by measuring. Small chunks embed crisply but lose context. Big chunks keep context but blur the vector.
  • Structure-aware chunking is the default. Split along headings and paragraphs, prepend the breadcrumb, and use small-to-big retrieval when answers need the full section.
  • Every metadata field powers a subsystem. Source links power citations, and access-control identifiers power the permissions filter.
  • The sync contract keeps the index honest. Sync incrementally, re-index idempotently and atomically, delete in order, and treat freshness lag as an SLO.
  • In a system design interview, walk through the six stages and name the document store as the source of truth. Then explain what happens when a document is edited or deleted.

Check Your Understanding

1. A chunk reads only "The limit is $50." Why is it a poor search result, and what are two fixes?

Show answer

It has lost the context that says which limit it is. The language model cannot tell whether the limit is for meals, hotels, or gifts. Prepending the breadcrumb, such as "Expense Policy > Team Events > Meals," fixes the chunk itself. Small-to-big retrieval fixes the context by handing the language model the full section.

2. Why does the vector index need a document store behind it?

Show answer

So that the index stays derived and rebuildable. The document store is the source of truth. If the index is lost, or a new embedding model arrives, the team rebuilds the index from the document store. It does not have to fetch every source again.

3. A re-indexing job runs twice because a webhook was delivered twice. What should the end state be, and why?

Show answer

The same as after one run. The job is idempotent: it is keyed by the document ID and replaces the whole chunk set. It never appends, so a second run writes the same chunks again.

4. A document is deleted. In what order should the deletion move through the system?

Show answer

The document store first, then the index, then the caches. The tombstone in the document store stops any later sync or rebuild from bringing the document back. Removing the chunks from the index stops retrieval from finding them. Clearing the caches last stops old answers from being served, and the index can no longer refill them.
