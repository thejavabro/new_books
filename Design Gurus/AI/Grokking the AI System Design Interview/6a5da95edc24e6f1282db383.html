Key Takeaways

Check Your Understanding

Know the Vocabulary

This lesson uses the terms below many times. Read them once now, and come back here when a later section needs one.

  • Automatic speech recognition (ASR): A model that turns spoken audio into written text. A streaming ASR model writes each word a second or two after it is spoken.
  • Transcript: The written text of a meeting, with a time and a speaker for every line.
  • Diarization: Working out which speaker said which words. It splits the audio by voice, so each part of the transcript gets a speaker.
  • Word error rate (WER): The share of words the transcript gets wrong. It counts three kinds of error: inserted words, deleted words, and swapped words.
  • Speaker attribution accuracy: The share of transcript words that are assigned to the right speaker.
  • Crosstalk: Two or more people speaking at the same time.
  • Confidence score: A number from the ASR model that says how sure it is about a word. A span with low scores may be wrong.
  • Action item: A task that someone agreed to do in the meeting. It has an owner, and sometimes a due date.
  • Data spine: The one shared record of each meeting. Every part of the system reads from it and writes to it.
  • Map-reduce summarization: A way to summarize a long text. Each chunk is summarized on its own, and then those summaries are summarized together.
  • Workflow: A series of steps whose order a developer fixed in code. A model may do the work inside a step, but it never chooses the next step.
  • Access control list (ACL): The list of people allowed to see something. In this design, every meeting has one.
  • Edit-rate: The share of delivered summaries that users change.
  • Reassignment-rate: The share of action items whose owner a user changes to someone else.
  • Tombstone: A marker that records that something was deleted, so that no later job brings it back.

The Question

The interviewer reads the question below. The numbers in it are illustrative assumptions.

"Our video-conferencing platform serves businesses, and it hosts about 5 million meetings a week. Design an AI meeting assistant. It joins a call and shows live captions. After the call, it sends each attendee a summary with action items. Users can also ask questions across their past meetings."

Stop here. Set a 45-minute timer, and run the design yourself, out loud, before you read on. Draw as you would on a whiteboard. Say each exit criterion before you move to the next step. How to Run a Case Study explains the clock and the exit criteria.

The walkthrough below follows the course's 45-minute clock. Each Part shows what the candidate says and draws, and why.

Part 1: The Recognition Move (minutes 0 to 2)

Three Clocks in One Question

Read the question again, and look at when each output is needed. Live captions must appear while people speak, within a second or two. The summary can arrive a few minutes after the call ends. An answer about past meetings comes when a user asks, perhaps weeks later.

Each of those is a different latency regime. A latency regime is the time limit that one part of the system must meet. One pipeline cannot serve a one-second limit, a ten-minute limit, and a question that arrives weeks later. So the recognition sentence names three subsystems, not one.

"This product is three subsystems with different latency regimes, sharing one data spine. A real-time pipeline transcribes the audio live. An async generation pipeline writes the summary and action items after the call. A RAG system over the meeting archive answers questions about past meetings."

Two terms in that sentence need a plain definition. "Async" is short for asynchronous, which means the work runs in the background and nobody waits on an open request. RAG stands for retrieval-augmented generation, in which the system first finds relevant text and then a model writes an answer from it.

Each subsystem is a known system from this course. The table shows what each one starts from, and what changes.

SubsystemKnown systemLatency regimeWhat changes
Real-time pipelineOnline inference, with a streaming modelAbout 1 to 2 seconds per captionThe input is live audio, not a request
Async generation pipelineThe job queue from the text-to-image designWithin 10 minutes of the call's endJobs arrive in sharp spikes when meetings end
RAG over the archiveEnterprise document Q&AA few seconds per answerThe documents are transcripts, and access comes from attendance

Design a Text-to-Image Service built the job queue. Capstone: Enterprise Document Q&A built RAG with permissions.

The Data Spine

The three subsystems share one data spine: the meeting record. It holds the meeting's ID, its organization, and the list of people who attended. It also holds any explicit shares, the consent state, and the retention policy. A retention policy says how long each artifact is kept.

The record also points to every artifact of the meeting. The artifacts are the audio, the transcript, the summary, the action items, and the search index entries.

The spine is what turns three systems into one product. The real-time lane writes the audio and the live transcript to it. The async lane reads them, writes the summary and the action items, and indexes the transcript for search. The archive lane answers questions from that index. Access, retention, and deletion all start from the same record.

The diagram below shows the three lanes and the spine they share.

Notice that no lane calls another lane directly. Each one reads from the spine and writes to it. So a change inside one lane, such as a new caption model, does not move the other two.

Part 2: Frame the Problem (minutes 2 to 3)

Recognition is the first part of Step 1. The scope is the second part, and it takes about one more minute.

What Is In and What Is Parked

Three features are in scope: live captions, the post-call summary with action items, and questions over past meetings. The design works from the audio and the participant list. Shared screens and video are out of scope for this version.

The candidate names one group of features and parks it: real-time in-meeting features, such as live translation and live coaching. Live coaching means tips shown to a speaker during the call, such as "You have spoken for ten minutes." Both would add models to the real-time lane. Either one could take the rest of the interview, so both go on the parked list.

Consent Is in Scope

Consent machinery is stated explicitly in scope, as a hard product gate. Consent machinery is the set of checks that make recording lawful and expected. It starts with a recording notice that every participant sees and hears when the assistant joins.

It also includes rules per jurisdiction, meaning per country or state. In some places, every participant must agree before a call is recorded. The legal team supplies these rules, and the system enforces them on every call.

A hard gate means no consent, no recording. If the rules for a call are not met, the assistant does not record or transcribe it. So consent is not a feature for after launch. It decides whether any audio enters the system at all.

The Signature Embarrassment

Every product has one error that users notice most and forgive least. For this product, the signature embarrassment is an action item assigned to the wrong person.

A summary with one clumsy sentence annoys a reader for a moment. But suppose the summary says "Ben will send the contract to the client," and Chen agreed to it. Ben gets a task he never accepted, and his manager may read it. Meanwhile Chen's real task gets lost, and after that nobody trusts the list.

Name this error in minute 3, because it shapes the metrics, the model, and the checks that follow.

Exit criterion: "This is three subsystems with different time limits, sharing one meeting record. Live translation and live coaching are parked. Consent is a hard gate, and it is in scope. The error to avoid most is an action item with the wrong owner. Does that match what you want?"

Part 3: Define Success (minutes 3 to 8)

Each lane does a different job, so each lane gets its own metrics. The targets below are examples, not guarantees.

Transcription: Word Error Rate and Speaker Attribution

Word error rate is the main quality number for ASR. It is the share of words the transcript gets wrong, counted against a reference transcript that a person wrote. Three kinds of error count: insertions, deletions, and swaps.

Here is an example with one error of each kind. A person said "we ship the new model on Friday," which is seven words. The transcript reads "we chip the model on a Friday." One word is swapped, because "ship" became "chip." One word is deleted, because "new" is missing. One word is inserted, because "a" was never said. That is 3 errors in 7 words, a WER of about 43 percent.

WER says nothing about who spoke the words. So the second number is speaker attribution accuracy: the share of words assigned to the right speaker. This number matters most here, because a wrong speaker can become a wrong owner.

Example targets: WER under 10 percent on clear audio, and speaker attribution above 95 percent. Report both for slices too, such as strong accents, noisy rooms, and crosstalk. An average can hide a slice that fails.

Summaries and Action Items: A Judge, a Human Anchor, and Edits

A summary has no single right answer, so no simple formula grades it. Offline, it is graded by a judge plus a human anchor.

The judge is a language model that scores each summary against a written rubric. A rubric is a list of questions that a grader answers about each output. A rubric item might ask whether every decision is present, or whether any claim lacks support in the transcript. The human anchor is a small sample that trained people also grade. Their scores show whether the judge still agrees with people. Evaluating LLM Systems explains this kind of judge and how to check it against human scores.

Online, the proxy for summary quality is edit-rate. A proxy is a stand-in for a goal that is hard to measure directly. When users often correct a summary, it was probably wrong or unclear.

Action items get one more pair of numbers. Offline, owner accuracy is measured on a sample that people labeled. Online, the reassignment-rate counts how often users move an action item to a different owner. A rising reassignment-rate is the signature embarrassment, showing up in the data.

Archive Q&A: Retrieval Recall and Faithfulness

Archive Q&A is graded on retrieval recall and faithfulness. Retrieval recall is the share of test questions for which the right transcript chunks were found. Faithfulness is the share of claims in the answers that those chunks really support.

The two numbers fail in different ways. Low recall means the evidence never arrived. Low faithfulness means the model had the evidence and misused it. The RAG Pipeline measures them separately for this reason.

Time Limits and Scale

The three time limits follow from the three lanes.

  • Live captions appear within about 1 to 2 seconds of the spoken words.
  • The summary arrives within 10 minutes of the call's end. The promise to users is stated as "within 10 minutes," not "instantly."
  • Archive answers show their first words within about 2 seconds.

The scale starts from 5 million meetings a week. If the average meeting lasts 40 minutes, that is 200 million minutes of audio a week.

The load is not smooth, because many meetings end on the hour or the half hour. Suppose a busy weekday has about 1 million meetings. Averaged over the day, that is about 12 meetings ending per second. But suppose 60,000 of them end in the five minutes around 10:00. That is about 200 per second, roughly 17 times the average.

The load chart looks like the teeth of a comb. Each tooth is a sharp spike as calls end on the hour. Each gap between the teeth is a lull. The pattern repeats all day, so this traffic is called comb-shaped. Part 6 shows how the design absorbs it.

LaneOffline metricOnline metricTime limit, as an example
Real-time transcriptionWER, speaker attribution accuracyShare of low-confidence wordsAbout 1 to 2 seconds
SummariesJudge plus human anchorEdit-rateWithin 10 minutes
Action itemsOwner accuracyReassignment-rateWithin 10 minutes
Archive Q&ARetrieval recall, faithfulnessUser feedback on answersAbout 2 seconds to first words

Exit criterion: "Transcription is judged by WER and speaker attribution. Summaries use a judge with a human anchor offline, and edit-rate online. Action items track reassignment-rate, and archive answers need recall and faithfulness. Captions take about 2 seconds, summaries arrive within 10 minutes, and the load is comb-shaped."

Part 4: Map the Data (minutes 8 to 13)

What the Platform Already Has

The platform owns the call, so it already holds data that a stand-alone product would have to guess.

  • Audio streams. When a person joins from a laptop, the platform receives that person's audio as a separate stream.
  • The participant list. It gives each person's name and the times they joined and left.
  • The calendar entry. It gives the meeting's title and the organizer.

The first item matters most for speakers. When each person is on their own device, the platform already knows whose stream carries each voice. Speaker attribution is then close to free.

Diarization matters when several people share one room microphone. Then one stream carries several voices, and the system must split them by voice. Most wrong speakers, and so most wrong owners, are likely to come from these shared microphones.

Where Labels Come From

Each metric from Part 3 needs a label source, and the table below lists them.

What is measuredLabel sourceDelayCost
WERReference transcripts typed by peopleDaysPaid per minute of audio
Speaker attributionSeparate device streams, plus people checking room recordingsDaysFree for devices, paid for rooms

A golden set is a fixed set of test cases with known good answers. Here, each case pairs a question with the meeting moments that answer it.

Notice the two free rows. Edits and reassignments are behavioral labels: labels that come from what users do, not from paid graders. They arrive every day, at no extra cost, and Part 7 puts them to work.

Privacy Limits the Labels

Meetings are private business data, so using them to train or evaluate a shared model needs the customer's permission, under the customer's contract. So the paid labels come from customers who opted in, and from meetings recorded for testing. The free labels follow the same rule.

Freshness

The live transcript must be fresh within seconds. The archive index must include a meeting within minutes after its summary is written. A new share must take effect at once, because a person who was just given access expects to see the meeting. A removed share matters even more, because until it takes effect, the meeting can still leak.

Exit criterion: "Device streams give speakers for free, and diarization handles shared room microphones. WER and summary labels are paid samples. Edits and reassignments are free labels that arrive within hours. Customer data is used only with permission."

Part 5: Pick the Model (minutes 13 to 18)

Transcription

The baseline is a hosted streaming ASR service that also returns confidence scores and diarization. The platform's device streams correct the speakers wherever each person has their own microphone.

The upgrade path has two parts, and both depend on measurement. First, if WER is high on company terms, such as product names, add a custom vocabulary list. Second, Part 8 shows that transcription is the largest cost. At this volume, a self-hosted open-source ASR model may cost less per minute. Measure WER on the same slices before any switch.

Summaries: One Pass or Map-Reduce

Summaries use a small-to-mid model with fixed instructions and a fixed output format. A 40-minute transcript has about 5,000 words, and many models can read that in one pass.

Long meetings use map-reduce summarization, because a two-hour workshop may have about 15,000 words. The workflow splits the transcript into chunks of about 10 minutes, at topic changes where it can. Each chunk is summarized on its own, which is the map step. Then those chunk summaries are summarized together, which is the reduce step.

Map-reduce has three benefits over one long pass. Every input fits in the model's context window, which is the most text the model can read at once. The chunk summaries run at the same time, so a long meeting finishes sooner. And a small model often stays more accurate on a short input than on a long one.

It also has one risk that the design must handle. A question raised in minute 5 may be settled in minute 90, in a different chunk. So every chunk summary keeps its decisions and its open questions as separate fields. The reduce step can then connect them.

Action Items Link to the Moment

A separate extraction pass reads the transcript and returns action items as structured records. Each record has the task, the owner, the due date if one was said, and the source moment. The source moment is the start and end time of the transcript lines where the task was agreed.

Every extracted action item links back to its source transcript moment, and one click plays the audio. This makes a hallucinated commitment visible immediately. To hallucinate is to state something false as if it were true. An invented task has no real moment behind it. The link shows audio that does not say it, and an automatic check finds no match in the transcript.

Two more rules guard the owner. The owner must be one of the attendees on the participant list. And when the source moment is unclear, the owner field says "owner unconfirmed" instead of guessing.

Workflow, Not Agent

The post-call pipeline is a workflow, not an agent, because every step is enumerable in advance. The steps are the final transcript, diarization, chunking, map, reduce, action items, checks, indexing, and delivery. The choice of the next step never depends on what an earlier step finds. Even the choice between one pass and map-reduce is a fixed rule on the transcript's length.

So a workflow wins on cost and debuggability. No model calls are spent on choosing the next step. And when a run fails, the log shows exactly which step broke. What an Agent Actually Is uses a call-summary pipeline as its example of a workflow.

Archive Q&A

The archive lane reuses enterprise RAG. Hybrid retrieval combines keyword search with embedding search, which finds text with a similar meaning. A permission filter runs inside both searches. Then a reranker, a model that scores each candidate against the question, puts the best ones first. A frontier model writes the answer, and every claim cites a meeting moment. A frontier model is one of the largest and most capable models, and also one of the most expensive. Archive questions are rare compared with meetings, so the frontier model's total cost stays small.

Exit criterion: "Transcription starts with a hosted streaming ASR service, corrected by device streams. Summaries use a small-to-mid model, with map-reduce for long meetings. Action items carry a link to their source moment. The post-call pipeline is a fixed workflow. Archive answers use hybrid retrieval and a frontier model with citations."

Part 6: Design the Serving Path (minutes 18 to 30)

Follow One Meeting

The steps below follow one meeting through all three lanes. The food-delivery company from earlier modules is a customer of this platform, and the times and numbers are illustrative.

Step 1: The consent gate opens the call (9:00). The delivery-time team starts a 90-minute planning meeting. Ana and Ben join from laptops, while Chen, Dev, and Eli sit in a conference room with one shared microphone. The assistant checks the company's policy and the rules for each participant's location. The recording notice appears on every screen and plays as audio. All rules are met, so recording starts.

Step 2: Live captions (during the call). Each audio stream flows to the streaming ASR service while someone on it is speaking. Captions appear about 1.5 seconds after the words. The audio is also written to object storage, a low-cost store for large files, and the meeting record points to it. Every word in the transcript keeps its confidence score.

Step 3: The call ends (10:30). The platform marks the meeting record as ended, and the record sends a "meeting ended" event. A job enters the summary queue, keyed by the meeting ID. If the event arrives twice, the key stops a second summary. Tens of thousands of other meetings end in the same few minutes.

Step 4: The final transcript (10:31). A worker, a program that takes jobs from the queue and runs them, picks up this job. It splits the room audio by voice, so Chen, Dev, and Eli each get their own lines. If live captions paused at any point, it transcribes that part again from the stored audio. Spans with low confidence scores are marked, with their times.

Step 5: Map-reduce (10:32). The 90-minute transcript becomes nine chunks of about 10 minutes. Nine model calls summarize them at the same time. One more call combines the nine summaries into the meeting summary.

Step 6: Action items and checks (10:33). The extraction pass finds four action items. One reads "Chen will update the delay dashboard by Friday," linked to 10:12:40. The check confirms that Chen attended and that the linked lines contain the commitment. But Chen and Dev spoke at the same time there, so Step 4 marked that span as low-confidence. So that item is marked "owner unconfirmed: Chen or Dev."

Step 7: Store, deliver, and index (10:35). The summary and the action items are written to the meeting record. The delivery list comes from the record's ACL: the five attendees. Each one gets an email with the meeting title and a link to the summary page. The transcript is split into topic chunks for the archive. Each chunk carries the meeting ID, its time range, its speakers, and the meeting's ACL.

Step 8: A question, three weeks later. Ana asks, "When did we decide to retrain the delivery-time model every week?" The permission filter limits the search to meetings Ana can see. Retrieval finds the chunk from 9:47 in this meeting. The frontier model starts its answer within about 2 seconds and cites that moment.

The diagram below shows Steps 3 to 7, the post-call workflow, with the clock time of each stage.

Notice that every stage runs in a fixed order, and none of them waits on a user. That is why the whole lane can sit behind a queue.

Every Arrow Has a Time, a Cost, and a Failure Behavior

The architecture is not finished until each arrow says how long it takes, what it costs, and what happens when it fails.

ArrowTime, as an exampleCostWhen it fails
Audio to streaming ASR1 to 2 secondsPrice per audio minute, the largest costCaptions pause, recording continues, and the post-call pass still runs from the stored audio
Meeting ended to queueUnder 1 secondSmallThe event is retried, and the meeting ID stops a duplicate summary
Queue to workerSeconds normally, minutes at a spikeWorker timeSummaries arrive late, never wrong
Worker to summary modelTens of secondsUnder a cent per meetingRetry with a wait, then a second provider; a job that keeps failing goes to a dead-letter queue
Record to attendeesSecondsSmallDelivery retries, and the summary page already exists
Question to archive answerAbout 2 seconds to first wordsA few centsIf permissions cannot be checked, fail closed; if the model fails, show the matching moments

Two terms in the table need a plain definition. A dead-letter queue is a separate queue for jobs that keep failing. They wait there for an engineer, instead of crashing workers forever. To fail closed is to deny access when a safety check cannot run. Model Serving Basics explains both.

The Queue Absorbs the Comb

The comb-shaped load is the main serving problem of the async lane. At 10:00, about 200 meetings end per second, and a few minutes later the rate falls back toward the average.

A queue turns the spike into a backlog that workers drain. The spikes are predictable, because they follow the clock. So the worker pool can grow a few minutes before each hour and shrink after it. The 10-minute promise gives the workers room to spread one spike over several minutes.

If the queue still backs up, summaries arrive minutes late, never wrong. The design never switches to a weaker model to catch up. A late summary is a small annoyance. A wrong owner in a summary is the signature embarrassment. That is also why the promise says "within 10 minutes," not "instantly."

Caches

Summaries are cacheable forever, because each summary is a per-meeting artifact, generated once and served always. Every attendee sees the same summary, so one cached copy serves all of them. Only an edit, which makes a new version, or a deletion changes it, and a deletion also clears the caches. The cache holds the summary, not the permission. Every request still runs the access check before the page is shown.

Archive answers are different, because two users who ask the same question may see different meetings. So an answer is never shared between users.

Exit criterion: "The diagram is complete. Captions run live, summaries run as queued jobs within 10 minutes, and archive answers run on request. Every arrow has a time, a cost, and a failure behavior."

Part 7: Keep It Working (minutes 30 to 38)

Offline Gates

Every change must pass the offline checks for its lane before it reaches users. A change can be a new ASR version, a new summary instruction, or a new retrieval setting.

  • Transcription runs on reference recordings, scored for WER and speaker attribution. The scores are split by slice, such as accents, noisy rooms, and shared microphones.
  • Summaries are scored by the judge, and the judge is checked against the human anchor.
  • Action items are scored for owner accuracy on a labeled sample.
  • Archive answers are scored for retrieval recall and faithfulness on the golden set.

Online Decides: Two Free Labels

Online, the loop harvests two free behavioral labels: edit-rate and reassignment-rate. Users produce them every day, simply by fixing what the assistant got wrong.

These two labels feed two things.

  1. The evals. A reviewed sample of edited summaries and reassigned items becomes new test cases. The offline gates then check that the same error does not return.

  2. The summary fine-tune. Fine-tuning means giving an existing model additional training. Pairs of original and edited summaries show the model the output that users prefer. This is used only where customers allow it.

One caution applies, because an edit may be a style choice, not a correction. So a person reviews a sample before edits become training data. Here, production is generating the training set. Monitoring, Drift, and Retraining explains the risks of that loop, and how to retrain safely.

Safe Rollout

A new summary model first runs in shadow mode. It writes summaries for a sample of meetings, but nobody receives them. The judge compares them with the current model's summaries.

Then a canary release sends the new model's summaries to a few organizations, then more. The rollout stops if edit-rate or reassignment-rate rises. Rolling back is simple, because the old model version stays ready.

Drift

Drift here means that the audio or the words change. A customer adopts new product names, or a new office brings new accents and new room hardware. The first signal is often the share of low-confidence words for one organization. It can rise days before users complain.

Exit criterion: "Offline, WER by slice, the judge with its human anchor, owner accuracy, and recall with faithfulness gate each change. Online, edit-rate and reassignment-rate decide. They also feed new evals and the summary fine-tune, where customers allow it."

Part 8: Count the Costs (minutes 38 to 43)

The Failure Ledger

Five failures matter most, and each one has a response.

1. An action item has the wrong owner. This is the signature embarrassment, so the defenses are layered. The owner must be an attendee, and every item links to its source moment. A low-confidence moment produces "owner unconfirmed" instead of a guess. An alarm watches the reassignment-rate for each organization.

2. ASR degrades on accents or crosstalk. Confidence-scored transcripts handle this case. Spans with low scores are marked in the summary, for example "unclear audio at 10:14." The reader sees the doubt instead of a confident guess. An action item from a marked span is never shown as confirmed.

3. The async pipeline backs up. Summaries arrive minutes late, never wrong. The promise is stated as "within 10 minutes." An alarm on queue depth pages the on-call engineer before the promise is missed.

4. A summary leaks to a non-attendee. Suppose the calendar invite went to a 500-person mailing list, and six people joined. Sending the summary to the invite list would leak it to 494 people. The ACL model prevents this leak, because delivery lists derive from attendance too, the same way search access does.

The default permission model is simple: you can search what you attended, plus what was explicitly shared with you. The delivery list uses the same rule. One function reads the meeting record and returns who may see the meeting. Both search and delivery call it, so they can never disagree.

For fast filtering, each chunk in the index carries a copy of its meeting's ACL, written by that same function. When a share is added or removed, the meeting record changes first. Then a small, fast update rewrites the ACL on that meeting's chunks, without indexing anything again.

The diagram below shows this single access rule and its two uses.

5. A meeting must be deleted. A person may ask for deletion under a privacy law, such as the European Union's GDPR, or a retention period may end. The response is tombstone-and-propagate. First, a tombstone marks the meeting as dead in the meeting record, so no later job brings it back. Then the delete propagates through every downstream copy. That includes the audio, the transcript, the summary, the action items, both search indexes, and the caches. An audit then checks each copy.

Two details complete the deletion story. The email carries only the title and a link, not the summary. A copy inside an email cannot be deleted later, but a link can. Deleting the page removes what the link points to. And a legal hold works the other way. A legal hold is an order to keep records for a lawsuit, so it pauses deletion until it is lifted. Capstone: Enterprise Document Q&A uses the same tombstone-and-propagate contract.

The Cost Ledger

The cost ledger multiplies the cost of one unit by the volume. All prices below are assumptions for arithmetic, and a candidate should say that they would check current prices.

Transcription. 200 million audio minutes a week, at an example price of 1,000,000 a week. This counts each meeting's audio once. It holds because a device stream goes to ASR only while someone on it is speaking. Sending every stream in full would multiply the minutes by the number of people on the call.

Summaries. A token is a small piece of text, often part of a word, and models are priced per million tokens. One 40-minute meeting needs about 17,000 input tokens and 1,500 output tokens across all its calls. Take a small model at a tenth of the frontier price: 1.20 per million output tokens. One meeting then costs about 35,000 a week. Inference Economics shows this arithmetic in full.

Archive Q&A. Suppose users ask 1 million questions a week, at about 30,000 a week.

LaneUnit cost, as an exampleVolume per weekCost per week
Transcription$0.005 per audio minute200 million minutesAbout $1,000,000
SummariesAbout $0.007 per meeting5 million meetingsAbout $35,000
Archive Q&AAbout $0.03 per question1 million questionsAbout $30,000

So cost routes differently across the three lanes. Transcription is the volume cost, and summaries use small-to-mid models. Archive Q&A stays a low-volume frontier lane.

Exit criterion: "The top failures are a wrong owner, poor audio, a backed-up queue, a leak, and deletion, and each has a response. Transcription is about 1.07 million a week, so ASR price per minute is the number to manage."

Part 9: The Follow-Ups (minutes 43 to 45, and beyond)

The interviewer uses the slack minutes for follow-up questions. Answer each one out loud before you read its answer.

1. "Live translation becomes a requirement. What changes, and what does not?"

The real-time lane grows a second streaming model per target language. The ASR output flows into a streaming translation model, and each listener picks a caption language. The async spine does not move. Summaries, action items, the archive, and the access rules all stay the same. The parked feature lands in one lane because the lanes share only the spine.

2. "Users say they can't find the decisions that were made. What is the fix?"

Add a decision-and-commitment extraction pass to the workflow. It finds sentences such as "we agreed to ship on Friday" and stores each one as a small record. The record holds the decision, who made it, and its source moment. These records are indexed beside the topic chunks. A topic chunk talks about many things, so one decision can rank low inside it. A short decision record is easier to retrieve.

3. "Where does the money actually go?"

Transcription dominates. The cost is minutes of audio times the price per minute, at 5 million meetings a week. The model tokens for summaries are small next to it. So the levers are about minutes, not tokens:

  • Skip silence before transcription, such as the minutes before people start talking, so quiet minutes are never billed.
  • Transcribe only meetings where the assistant is turned on and consent is given. The consent gate is also a cost gate.
  • Compare the hosted price with a self-hosted ASR model at this volume, after checking WER on every slice.

4. "Two companies are on one call together. How do you handle it?"

Each organization gets its own copy, under its own ACLs and its own retention policy. The supplier's copy follows the supplier's rules, and the customer's copy follows the customer's rules. If one company deletes its copy, the other company's copy is not affected. Neither company's admins can see the other company's copy.

5. "The CEO asks what engineering committed to marketing this quarter. How do you answer?"

This is not a single meeting lookup, because the commitments are spread over many meetings. It is aggregation RAG under the CEO's ACLs. Aggregation means collecting matching items from many sources and combining them. The system retrieves decision records across the meetings the CEO can access, then groups and summarizes them.

A subtlety must be named: the CEO's access might miss the real commitment. It may have been made in a meeting the CEO did not attend and nobody shared. A senior title does not override the ACLs. So the answer states its coverage: "Based on 14 meetings you can access." Without that sentence, an incomplete answer looks complete.

Part 10: What Each Level Sounds Like

Mid-level. The candidate draws one pipeline: record, transcribe, summarize, and search. Privacy is only a login check, with no rule about who sees which meeting. The design works for one meeting, but it misses the three time limits, the comb-shaped load, and the wrong-owner risk.

Senior. The candidate names three lanes with different time limits and one data spine. A queue absorbs the comb, and map-reduce handles long meetings. Action items link to their source moments. Access comes from attendance, for both search and delivery. Each lane has its own metrics, and the cost ledger finds transcription as the main cost.

Staff. The candidate does all of that, and also shows that the privacy model shapes product truthfulness itself, not just visibility. The ACLs decide what an answer can see. So an answer from partial access must say what it could not see, as in the CEO question. The staff candidate also treats consent as both a legal gate and a cost gate. And they ask whether every meeting needs transcription at all, before sizing the ASR fleet.

Part 11: Variations to Practice

Run each variation on a fresh 45-minute clock, a few days after this one.

  • Support-call summaries for the food-delivery company. Every support phone call gets a summary and follow-up tasks for the ticket. What changes: there are two speakers, an agent and a customer, on separate channels. So diarization is easy, but the volume is high and the cost per call matters more.
  • A lecture-notes assistant for a university. It transcribes lectures and answers students' questions about them. What changes: one main speaker and many technical terms, so custom vocabulary matters more. Access follows course enrollment instead of attendance.
  • A medical visit scribe. It writes a clinical note from a doctor's visit. What changes: the stakes rise sharply. A wrong medication in a note can harm a patient, so a clinician reviews every note before it is saved.

Common Mistakes

Mistake 1: One Pipeline for Everything

Live captions, summaries, and archive answers have very different time limits. A single pipeline makes the live path too slow or the summary path too costly. Name three lanes in the first minutes.

Mistake 2: Summarizing a Long Transcript in One Pass

A two-hour meeting can exceed what a small model reads well. Use map-reduce, and keep decisions and open questions as separate fields in each chunk summary.

Mistake 3: Action Items Without Evidence

An action item with no link to its source moment cannot be checked. Link every item to its moment, require the owner to be an attendee, and mark unclear owners.

Mistake 4: Sending Summaries to the Invite List

The invite list can include hundreds of people who never joined. Derive the delivery list from attendance and explicit shares, with the same rule that search uses.

Mistake 5: Making the Post-Call Pipeline an Agent

Every post-call step is known in advance. A fixed workflow is cheaper, and its failures are easier to trace.

Mistake 6: Pricing Tokens and Ignoring Minutes

At this scale, transcription costs far more than the summaries. A cost ledger that counts only tokens misses most of the bill.

FAQs

Q: Should the live transcript also be the final transcript?

No, not always. The live pass must be fast, so it may make more errors, especially on shared microphones. The post-call pass has more time. It can run diarization on the whole recording and mark low-confidence spans.

Q: Is consent only the legal team's concern?

No. The legal team writes the rules, but the system must enforce them on every call. Without consent, no audio may enter the system, so consent shapes the design from minute 3.

Q: Why not use a frontier model for summaries too?

Summaries run 5 million times a week, so their unit cost multiplies by a large volume. A small-to-mid model is usually good enough for this task, and the judge and edit-rate show whether it is. Archive questions are far fewer, so a frontier model fits there.

Q: Can an administrator search every meeting in the company?

No, not by default. The default is what you attended, plus what was explicitly shared with you. Any wider access, such as for a legal investigation, is a separate, audited process with its own rules.

Q: Does a late summary break the product's promise?

No, if the promise was stated honestly. The promise is "within 10 minutes," and the queue protects quality during spikes. A summary that is a few minutes late is far better than a wrong one.

Key Takeaways

  • The recognition move finds three subsystems with different latency regimes. A real-time pipeline, an async generation pipeline, and a RAG system over the archive share one data spine.
  • Each lane has its own metrics. Transcription uses WER and speaker attribution. Summaries use a judge with a human anchor offline and edit-rate online. Archive answers use retrieval recall and faithfulness.
  • The signature embarrassment is a wrong owner. Every action item links to its source moment, the owner must be an attendee, and unclear owners are marked.
  • The async lane is a queued workflow. Its steps are known in advance, a queue absorbs the comb-shaped load, and a backlog makes summaries late, never wrong.
  • Access comes from attendance. Search and delivery use one rule: what you attended, plus what was shared with you. Deletion uses tombstone-and-propagate through every copy.
  • Transcription is the main cost. Minutes of audio times the price per minute outweighs the summary tokens.
  • In a system design interview, name the three lanes and the spine by minute 2. State consent as a hard gate, name the wrong-owner risk, and price the audio minutes, not only the tokens.

1. Why does this design use three lanes instead of one pipeline?

Show answer

Because the three outputs have very different time limits. Captions must appear within about 2 seconds. Summaries can take up to 10 minutes. Archive answers come on request, weeks later. One pipeline cannot meet all three limits well, so each lane is built for its own limit, and all three share the meeting record.

3. A summary says "Ben will send the contract." How does the design help users catch an error in that item?

Show answer

The action item links to its source moment in the transcript. One click plays the audio where the task was agreed. If the moment does not support the item, the error is visible at once. The owner must also be an attendee, and an unclear moment produces "owner unconfirmed" instead of a guess.
