Key Takeaways

Check Your Understanding

Know the Vocabulary

This lesson uses the terms below many times. Read them once now, and come back here when a later section needs one.

  • Harmful content: Content that breaks a platform's rules, such as harassment, hate speech, graphic violence, or scams. This lesson also calls it violating content.
  • Policy: The written rules that say what content is allowed. The policy is split into harm categories, such as harassment or graphic violence.
  • Trust and Safety: The team that writes the platform's content policy and decides how it is enforced.
  • Multimodal: Content that arrives as text, images, video, and audio together, such as a video with a caption and a soundtrack.
  • Classifier: A model that sorts an input into categories. Here it returns a score for each harm category, such as "harassment: 0.82."
  • Missed harm: A harmful post that the system leaves up, which is called a false negative.
  • Wrongful removal: An allowed post that the system takes down or limits, which is called a false positive.
  • Prevalence: The share of content views that land on violating content.
  • Perceptual hash: A short fingerprint of an image or a video that still matches after small edits, such as resizing or cropping.
  • Risk funnel: A chain of checks in which each stage costs more per item and sees fewer items. Each stage passes on only the items that still look risky.
  • Pass rate: The share of items that one stage sends on to the next stage.
  • Action ladder: The set of actions the system can take on a post, from leaving it alone to removing it.
  • Review queue: The list of posts waiting for a human reviewer to decide.
  • Active learning: A way to choose which items people label. It sends the items the model is most uncertain about to reviewers first.
  • Appeal: A request from a user to look again at a decision against their post.
  • Reach: How many people have seen a post, or are likely to see it soon.

Part 1: Frame the Problem

What Fraud Already Taught You

An interviewer says, "Design a system that detects harmful content on a social platform." This is a judging question. Each new post is one item that needs a decision, as in Design Fraud Detection.

Two lessons from fraud carry over directly. The first is the adversary, a person who studies the system and changes their behavior to get past it. Second, the costs are asymmetric, which means the two kinds of mistake do not cost the same.

Harmful content adds three complications on top of those two.

  1. The input is multimodal. A post can hold text, images, video, and audio together. A payment is a short row of numbers. A post may be a two-minute video with a caption, spoken words, and text drawn inside the frames.

  2. The costs are not monetary. Missed harm exposes people to harassment, threats, or scams. Wrongful removal silences a person who did nothing wrong. Neither cost is a dollar figure, so no spreadsheet can balance them. The balance needs a policy conversation with the people who own the rules.

  3. The benign volume is huge. Almost every post is harmless, and this lesson assumes that well under 1 percent of posts break a rule. The system must check every post, but nearly all of that work finds nothing.

Context Changes the Meaning

One more problem makes this question hard. The same words can mean different things in different contexts.

For example, a word can be a slur when an outsider uses it to attack someone. The same word can be reclamation when a member of the targeted group uses it about themselves. Reclamation means a group taking back a word that was used against it. So the same text can be harassment from one speaker and acceptable from another.

Images have the same problem, since a photo of a wounded person can be news in one post and glorified violence in another. A model that reads only the words or only the pixels will make mistakes. It also needs the context: who posted it, where, and with what caption.

Start With the Numbers

Ask for the scale, or state it as an assumption. The numbers below are illustrative assumptions for this lesson.

  • Volume: 200 million new posts and comments per day. That is about 2,300 items per second on average, with higher peaks.
  • Mix: text, photos, short videos, and audio clips.
  • Harm rate: well under 1 percent of items, and very different from one category to another.

Then state the scope. For example, design detection for public posts, comments, photos, and videos. Leave direct messages, ads, and live streams out of scope, because they have different privacy rules and time limits. Confirm the scope with the interviewer before you go on.

Why One Classifier Cannot Do It

A weak answer proposes one large classifier that reads every post. That fails for a reason you have seen before. A model good enough to judge context in a meme is expensive. Running it 200 million times a day would cost far more than the product can pay.

A model cheap enough to run on every post is too simple to judge context well. So the design splits the work into stages, like the recommendation funnel. Cheap checks look at everything, and expensive checks look only at the few items that still look risky. Part 4 builds this risk funnel.

Part 2: Define Success

Measure Views, Not Uploads

The main metric is prevalence: the share of content views that land on violating content. It answers the question of how often people see something harmful when they use the platform.

Why count views and not uploads? Because a viral miss and an unseen miss are very different failures. A harmful post seen by 2 million people does far more damage than one seen by 10 people. Counted by uploads, both are one miss. Counted by views, the viral miss is 200,000 times larger, which matches the real harm.

Prevalence is reported per harm category. One number averaged across harassment, scams, and graphic violence would hide a rise in any one of them.

How to Measure Prevalence

Nobody can review every view, so prevalence is estimated from a sample.

Step 1: Sample views at random. Take a random sample of views from the view logs, such as 100,000 views per category per week. A post with many views is more likely to be picked. So the sample is already weighted by real exposure.

Step 2: Reviewers label the sampled items. Trained reviewers decide whether each sampled item breaks the policy.

Step 3: Compute the share. Suppose 50 of the 100,000 sampled views landed on harassment, which gives an estimated prevalence of 0.05 percent.

Step 4: Report the error bar. A sample gives an estimate, not an exact value. With 50 hits, the true value could be roughly 0.04 to 0.07 percent. Rare categories need larger samples to give a useful range.

Notice that this sample is drawn without using the model. That matters, because it catches harm the model missed. A metric built only from the model's own flags would never show its blind spots.

The Guardrail in the Other Direction

Prevalence measures missed harm, and a system could push it down by removing far too much. So the design also tracks the opposite cost.

The guardrail is the false-removal rate: the share of removals that were wrong. Measure it in two ways. Reviewers audit a random sample of removed posts. The system also counts how often appeals overturn a removal. Appeals alone are not enough, because many people never appeal.

Offline Metrics for Each Stage

Each stage of the funnel has its own job, so each gets its own offline check on a human-labeled test set.

StageJobOffline metric
Fast classifierPass on almost every risky itemRecall at its pass rate, per category
Heavy modelSeparate real harm from look-alikesPrecision and recall at each threshold
Human reviewDecide correctly and consistentlyAgreement between reviewers on test items

Recall is the share of truly harmful items that a stage keeps. Precision is the share of flagged items that are truly harmful. The fast classifier is judged on recall, like retrieval in a recommendation funnel. A harmful post that it does not pass on gets no deeper check at upload.

Also track time to action: how long a harmful post stays up, and how many views it collects before action. These are example metrics, not a fixed list.

Part 3: Map the Data

Where Labels Come From

A label is the answer a model should learn for one past example. Here, no outcome in the product says whether a post is harmful, so a person must decide. That makes labels scarce and expensive.

Two label feeds keep this system's loop running.

1. Reviewer decisions from the queue. Every time a reviewer decides on a post, that decision becomes a label. These labels are high quality, because a trained person followed the written guideline, and they arrive within hours.

2. Outcomes from appeals. When a user appeals a removal, a second reviewer looks again. If the removal is overturned, the post becomes a labeled false positive. These are the examples the model most needs to learn from.

User reports are a third signal, but they are evidence, not labels. Reports are noisy, and groups of users can file false reports to attack someone. So a report raises a post's priority for review. It never removes a post by itself. Where Training Data Comes From explains label sources and review queues in general.

Active Learning Spends Reviewer Time Well

Reviewer hours are the scarcest resource in this design. A random sample of posts is almost all harmless, so labeling it teaches the model very little.

Active learning fits here because harm is rare and labels are costly. It sends the most uncertain items to reviewers first. Those are the items near the model's decision line, where a label changes the model the most. A post the model scores at 0.01 or at 0.99 usually teaches it little. A post it scores at 0.5 usually teaches it a lot.

Keep one random sample as well, because active learning picks only the items the model already finds hard. A random sample shows what the model misses with confidence.

The Guideline Is the Label

A label means only what the guideline says. When Trust and Safety changes the harassment policy, old labels may no longer match it. Store the guideline version with every label. Then training can drop or relabel examples made under an old version.

How Adversaries Evade Detection

People who post harm on purpose test the system and adapt. Four evasion tricks are especially common.

TrickWhat it looks likeDefense
LeetspeakLetters replaced by numbers or symbols, such as "h4te"Normalize the text before the model reads it
HomoglyphsLetters from another alphabet that look the same, such as a Cyrillic letter that looks like a Latin "a"Map look-alike characters back to standard letters
Text embedded in imagesA screenshot of a harmful sentence, so text models never see itOptical character recognition (OCR), which reads text out of images
Harm split across postsEach post looks harmless alone, but together they target one personFeatures about the account, the thread, and the target

Notice that each defense moves the data closer to what the model can read. Normalization and OCR turn hidden text into plain text. Account and thread features show the pattern that no single post shows.

This is concept drift driven by an opponent. Concept drift means the link between inputs and outcomes changes over time. Monitoring, Drift, and Retraining covers it in general. Here, someone changes the patterns on purpose.

Features

The models read several groups of features. A feature is one input value that a model uses.

  • Content: the text after normalization, the image pixels, sampled video frames, and a transcript of the audio.
  • Context: the caption, the thread the post replies to, and the community where it appears.
  • Account: the account's age, its past violations, and how fast it is posting.
  • Reach: the author's follower count and how fast views are growing.

Part 4: Pick the Model

Start With a Baseline

The simplest working system has three parts. Hash matching catches copies of known harmful media, keyword rules catch obvious phrases, and user reports send posts to human review. This baseline is cheap and easy to explain. It also misses most new harm, so every model must beat it.

The Risk Funnel

The full design is a risk funnel with four stages. Each stage costs more per item than the one before, and it sees fewer items. The pass rates below are examples.

Stage 1: Hashes and rules. Every uploaded image and video gets a perceptual hash. The system compares it with a database of hashes of known harmful media. A perceptual hash still matches after small edits, such as resizing, cropping, or saving in a new format. An exact hash would miss those copies, because even a tiny edit gives a completely different exact hash. Rules handle other clear cases, such as links to known scam sites. A strong match is acted on at once.

Stage 2: The fast classifier. Small models score every remaining item, one per modality. A text model reads the normalized text. An image model reads the photo and a few video frames. Their only job is to decide which items advance to the next, heavier stage. Even a very high score here does not remove a post, because it only sends the post onward. In this design, about 1 percent of items pass. These passed items are called the flagged slice.

Stage 3: The heavy multimodal models. These models read the text, the image, and the frames together, with the context. One of them can be an LLM classifier. An LLM, or large language model, is a model trained on huge amounts of text. Some LLMs can also read images. Given the policy text and the post, it can judge sarcasm, slang, and image-plus-caption jokes better than a small model. It is also far more expensive per item. Heavy models run only on the flagged slice of uncertain items, because running them on all content would be far too expensive.

Stage 4: Human review. Items the heavy models remain unsure about go to the review queue. In this design, about 0.1 percent of all items reach a person.

The diagram below shows the four stages, the daily count that reaches each one, and its job.

Notice how this funnel differs from the recommendation funnel it resembles. A recommendation funnel filters for relevance: it keeps the items a user would like. This funnel filters for risk at each stage: it keeps the items that might be harmful. An item that a model stage clears leaves the funnel and is allowed. Only a strong hash match or a very high heavy-model score removes an item before human review.

An Analogy: Airport Security

The risk funnel works like airport security. First, each name is checked against a list of people who may not fly. That is the hash and rule check against known harmful media. Next, every passenger walks through the metal detector, which is quick and cheap. That is the fast classifier on every post. A passenger who sets off the alarm gets a slower, careful search. That is the heavy model on the flagged slice. A few passengers still raise questions after the search, so an officer interviews them. That is human review. Most travelers pass the first checks and never see the later ones, just as most posts do.

The Action Ladder

A score is not a decision, so the action ladder turns scores into actions. The ladder has four actions.

  1. Auto-allow. The post stays up with normal distribution. This is the action for nearly all posts.

  2. Reduce or age-gate. The post stays up, but the system limits it. Reduce means it is not recommended to people who do not follow the author. Age-gate means only adult accounts can see it.

  3. Human review. A reviewer decides. While the post waits, it can be reduced.

  4. Auto-remove. The system takes the post down without a person, because the evidence is very strong.

For each harm category, two thresholds split the heavy model's score into three bands. Above the high threshold, the post is auto-removed. Between the two thresholds, it goes to human review. Below the low threshold, it is allowed. A strong hash match also leads to auto-removal. Items that the fast classifier does not pass on never get a heavy score, and they are auto-allowed.

One more check decides how an allowed post spreads. The heavy models also return a borderline score, which estimates how close the post sits to the line. If that score is high, the post is reduced or age-gated instead of fully allowed.

Reduce or age-gate is the softer action for "lawful but awful" borderline content. That is content that breaks no rule but sits close to the line, such as crude jokes that offend many people. Removing it would silence legal speech, but spreading it widely would expose many people to it. Limiting its reach is the middle path. The feed ranking lesson shows the feed side of this: it decides how far allowed posts spread.

Thresholds Are Policy, Not Statistics

A threshold is a score cutoff where the action changes. For fraud, thresholds can come from expected dollar loss. Here, there is no dollar figure to balance.

The precision-recall curve shows the trade-off at each possible threshold. It cannot say which trade-off is right. Deciding how much wrongful removal is acceptable to prevent one more harmful view is a values decision. So the lines are set with Trust and Safety and Legal, not by the engineering team alone.

The lines also differ by harm category and by jurisdiction. A jurisdiction is a country or region with its own laws. A threat of violence may get a strict line everywhere. A word may be legal in one country and banned in another. So engineering builds a policy engine: a service that stores each threshold as configuration, keyed by category and region. Trust and Safety can then change a line without a new model.

Part 5: Design the Serving Path

When Each Check Runs

Not every stage runs while the user waits. The times below are example targets, not guarantees.

StageWhen it runsExample time
Hashes and rulesBefore the post goes liveA few milliseconds
Fast classifierBefore the post goes liveTens of milliseconds for text and images
Heavy modelsRight after the post goes liveSeconds
Human reviewFrom the queueMinutes to hours

Videos take longer, so the fast check runs while the platform processes the upload. A post that the fast classifier flags goes live, but it is reduced until the heavy models finish. This short hold is a safety step, not a decision about the post. Each stage writes its scores to a log with the model version.

The Review Queue

The review queue orders posts by model uncertainty times potential reach. Uncertainty is high when the score is far from both a clear allow and a clear removal. Potential reach is high when the author has many followers or the views are growing fast.

This one rule serves two goals. Reach puts the most dangerous mistakes first. Uncertainty puts the most useful labels first, which is active learning again. A post that is certain needs no person. A post that nobody will see can wait.

Keep some capacity for other work. Reviewers also label the prevalence sample, handle appeals, and clear old items, so low-reach posts are not ignored forever. Some severe categories get their own fast lane with specially trained reviewers.

Follow One Post

The steps below follow one post through the system. The times are illustrative.

Step 1: The post arrives. At 7:15 PM, an account with 900,000 followers posts a meme. It is a photo of a named person with a caption. The upload service sends it to the checks.

Step 2: Hashes and rules find nothing. The image is new, so its perceptual hash matches nothing in the database, and no rule fires.

Step 3: The fast classifier flags it. OCR reads the words drawn on the image. The text model and the image model score the post. The harassment score is in the risky band, so the post advances. It goes live, but it is reduced.

Step 4: The heavy model is unsure. About 3 seconds later, the multimodal model reads the photo, the drawn words, and the caption together. It scores harassment at 0.6, which sits between the two thresholds, so the action is human review.

Step 5: The queue puts it near the front. The score is uncertain, and the author has a large audience. Uncertainty times reach is high, so the post jumps ahead of thousands of low-reach items.

Step 6: A reviewer decides. At 7:40 PM, a reviewer reads the post and the thread. The meme mocks a private person and repeats an insult from earlier posts. It breaks the harassment policy, so the post is removed. The decision is logged as a label.

Step 7: The author appeals. The next day, the author appeals, and a second reviewer upholds the removal. That outcome is logged too. If the second reviewer had overturned the removal, the post would be restored. That label would then teach the model about a false positive.

The diagram below shows the same seven steps. In the top row, the first box shows the clock time, and each later time counts from the moment the post arrives.

Notice that the post was never recommended widely while it waited. It was live but reduced, so it was not shown to people outside its followers. With 900,000 followers, that is still a large audience. That is why reach puts the post near the front of the queue. Notice also that both human decisions became labels for the next model.

Reach-Aware Routing: The Viral Defense

Some harmful posts get past the fast classifier, and most of them collect few views. The dangerous ones are the posts that spread fast.

Reach-aware routing escalates fast-spreading posts. A view tier is a count such as 10,000, 100,000, or 1 million views. When a post crosses a tier, the system re-scores it with the heavy models. If the score is uncertain, the post moves to the front of the review queue. So a post gets more checks as its audience grows.

This is affordable because few posts ever reach those tiers. It does not catch everything, because the heavy model can miss the same post twice. So the design also measures how many views harmful posts collect before action.

Appeals Belong in the Design

Appeals must be designed in from day one, not added later, for two reasons.

First, they are an ethical requirement. A person whose post was removed by a machine deserves a way to ask for another look. Some laws also require such a process.

Second, they are a source of false-positive labels. An overturned removal is a confirmed mistake, which is the evidence the model most needs. Without appeals, the system rarely learns where it removes too much.

When a Stage Fails

Decide in advance what happens when a model is slow or down. Fail open means the post goes live without that check. Fail closed means the post is held until the check runs.

Choose per harm category and per surface, not once for the whole platform. Most posts can fail open and be scored when the model recovers. A new account posting many links during an outage may be held. Model Serving Basics covers fallbacks and failure choices in general.

Part 6: Keep It Working

The Loop

Reviewer decisions and appeal outcomes feed the training data every day. The fast classifiers and the heavy models retrain often, such as weekly, and recent evasion patterns get extra weight. The hash database updates within minutes, because a new hash needs no training.

Before a new model replaces the current one, it passes three checks.

  1. Backtest. The new model scores a set of posts that reviewers already labeled and that it did not train on. Its precision and recall per category must match or beat the current model.
  2. Shadow. The new model scores live posts next to the current one. Its scores are logged but never used. Compare the pass rates and the flags per category and per language.
  3. Canary. The new model handles a small share of traffic first. Watch the false-removal rate and the review volume before sending it more.

Watch for Attacks

Monitor the pass rate, the action counts, and the scores per category every day. A sudden drop in flags is not always good news. It may mean that adversaries found a new trick. Treat it as a possible attack and check a sample.

Prevalence moves slowly, because it comes from a weekly sample. So the daily signals give the early warning, and prevalence confirms the trend.

A Brand-New Kind of Harm

Sometimes a new kind of harm appears that the model has never seen. A new scam may spread in a day. No model has examples of it, so retraining cannot help yet.

Rules and human review carry the load first. Analysts write rules and add hashes of known examples within hours. Reports about the new harm go straight to the review queue, where every decision becomes a label. Rules can also send matching posts to the heavy stage. If that stage uses an LLM classifier that reads the policy text, analysts can add the new rule to that text without retraining. They still check its results on labeled examples. After enough labels exist, often within weeks, the classifiers retrain to catch it.

When the policy itself changes, the old content needs a new check. A batch job re-scores older posts against the new lines, because a post that was allowed yesterday may break today's rule.

Care for the Reviewers

Reviewers see the worst content on the platform. The queue should limit how much graphic material one person sees in a shift. Tools can blur images by default and show them only when needed. These limits are part of the design, because they set real reviewer capacity.

Reviewers must also agree with each other. Mix test items with known answers into the queue. Measure each reviewer's agreement, and retrain reviewers when the guideline changes.

Part 7: Count the Costs

The Machine Bill

Assume, as an illustration, that the heavy model costs about 1,000 times more per item than the fast classifier.

  • Fast classifier on all 200 million items: call that cost 1 unit.
  • Heavy model on 1 percent, which is 2 million items: each costs as much as 1,000 fast checks. That equals 2 billion fast checks, or 10 units. It is about 23 heavy calls per second on average.
  • Heavy model on all 200 million items: 1,000 units.

So the funnel costs about 11 units, against about 1,000 for one heavy model on everything. That is why the heavy LLM classifier stays affordable. The funnel routes it only the flagged slice, not all traffic.

The single knob that really controls the compute bill is the fast stage's pass rate into the heavy model. Every extra tenth of a percent adds 200,000 heavy calls a day. Raising the pass rate can catch more harm, and it costs more. Lowering it saves money and lets more harm through.

The Human Bill

Reviewer hours are the hardest limit of all. If 0.1 percent of items reach review, that is 200,000 decisions a day. Suppose one reviewer makes about 250 decisions per shift, as an example. Then the queue needs about 800 reviewer shifts every day.

So the review threshold is also a headcount setting. Widening the review band by a little can add hundreds of shifts. Set it with the reviewer team, not only with the model team.

The Failure Ledger

Close with the top failures and the response to each.

  1. The viral miss: harm reaching millions before review. It is the top entry, because it does the most damage in the least time. Response: reach-aware routing and time-to-action alerts.
  2. Over-removal: many wrongful removals that nobody notices at once. It is quiet, because silenced users rarely make news. Response: the false-removal guardrail, audits, and appeals.
  3. An evasion wave: adversaries find a trick and the flags drop. Response: rules and hashes within hours, then retraining.
  4. A model outage: a stage stops working. Response: fail open or fail closed, chosen in advance per category.

In the Interview

The 30-second answer: "I'll design a system, not one classifier. This is fraud's judging shape with three new problems: multimodal input, costs that are not dollars, and a huge benign volume. I'll measure prevalence, the share of views that land on violating content, from a random sample of views per category. False removals are the guardrail. The design is a risk funnel that starts with hashes, rules, and a fast classifier on every item. Heavy multimodal models see about 1 percent, and human review sees about 0.1 percent. An action ladder maps scores to auto-allow, reduce or age-gate, human review, or auto-remove. Trust and Safety and Legal own the thresholds. Reviewer decisions and appeals are the labels, and reach-aware routing defends against the viral miss."

Notice the word "system" in a strong answer. Calling this a system instead of a classifier is the upgrade the interviewer wants to hear. It shows that the funnel is a cost-management structure, not one model.

Likely follow-ups:

  1. "A harmful post is going viral right now. How does the system catch it in time?" Reach-aware routing. When the post crosses a view tier, the heavy models score it again. If the result is uncertain, the post moves to the front of the review queue, because its reach is high.
  2. "A new kind of harm appears that the model has never seen. What do you do?" Rules and human review carry the load first. Analysts add rules and hashes within hours, and reports go straight to review. The reviewer decisions become labels, and the classifiers retrain once there are enough of them.
  3. "Why isn't the heavy LLM classifier too expensive?" The funnel routes it only the flagged slice. At a 1 percent pass rate, it sees 2 million items a day, not 200 million.

Same Design, Other Questions

Spam detection for direct messages. Privacy limits how much of the message content the system can inspect. So behavioral and graph signals lead. Graph signals come from the links between accounts, such as who messages whom. One example is a new account sending the same message to hundreds of strangers. How many recipients block or report the sender is another strong signal.

Abuse detection for restaurant reviews in a food-delivery app. The scale is far smaller, and the content is mostly text. A single text classifier plus human review may be enough. The action ladder can add a hold before a review is published.

Common Mistakes

Mistake 1: One Big Classifier Over Everything

A weak answer runs one large model on every post. It is too expensive at this scale, and it hides how the work is split. The strong alternative is to draw the funnel and explain that it spends compute on the risky slice.

Mistake 2: Counting Uploads Instead of Views

A metric based on uploads treats a viral miss and an unseen miss the same. Measure prevalence by views, per category, from a random sample.

Mistake 3: A Yes-or-No Decision

Removing or allowing is not enough. Borderline posts need reduce or age-gate, and uncertain posts need human review.

Mistake 4: Letting Engineering Set the Lines

The model team can show the trade-off at each threshold. Choosing the line is a values decision, so it belongs with Trust and Safety and Legal.

Mistake 5: Forgetting Appeals

A design without appeals has no fair way to fix mistakes. It also loses its best source of false-positive labels.

FAQs

Q: Can an LLM classifier replace the whole funnel?

No. An LLM classifier judges context well, but it costs far more per item. Running it on 200 million items a day would multiply the bill. It belongs in the heavy stage, where it sees only the flagged slice.

Q: Should a user report remove a post automatically?

No. Reports are noisy, and groups can file false reports to attack someone. A report raises the post's place in the review queue. A person or a strong model decides.

Q: Is a perceptual hash enough for known harmful media?

No. It catches copies with small edits, but larger edits can defeat it. New harmful media has no hash at all. The classifiers and human review handle what hashing misses.

Q: Does a lower prevalence always mean the system improved?

No. Prevalence can fall because the system removes too much. Always read it next to the false-removal rate. Check the error bar too, because a small change may be noise from the sample.

Key Takeaways

  • Harmful content adds three complications to fraud's adversary and asymmetric costs. The input is multimodal, the costs are not monetary, and the benign volume is huge.
  • Prevalence is measured by views, not uploads. A random sample of views, labeled by reviewers, weights the estimate by real exposure. The false-removal rate is the guardrail.
  • The risk funnel spends compute in proportion to risk. Hashes and rules, a fast classifier on everything, heavy multimodal models on the flagged slice, and human review on what remains.
  • The action ladder has four actions. Auto-allow, reduce or age-gate, human review, and auto-remove. Its thresholds are policy, set with Trust and Safety and Legal.
  • Reviewer decisions and appeals are the labels. Active learning and the uncertainty-times-reach queue send scarce reviewer time where it matters most.
  • The pass rate sets the machine bill, and the review band sets the headcount. The viral miss tops the failure ledger, and reach-aware routing is its defense.
  • In a system design interview, call it a system, not a classifier. Draw the funnel with its pass rates, name prevalence by views, and show who owns the thresholds.

Check Your Understanding

1. Why does the heavy multimodal model run on about 1 percent of items and not on all of them?

Show answer

Because running it on everything would be far too expensive. The fast classifier checks every item cheaply and passes on only the risky slice. In this lesson's example, the funnel costs about 11 units instead of about 1,000.

2. Two harmful posts are missed. One gets 3 million views, and the other gets 12. Why does prevalence treat them differently?

Show answer

Because prevalence counts views, not uploads. The viral miss exposed millions of people, and the other exposed almost nobody. A random sample of views is far more likely to land on the viral post, so the estimate reflects the real harm.

3. A post is allowed by the rules, but many people find it offensive. Which action fits it?

Show answer

Reduce or age-gate. The post stays up, so legal speech is not silenced. But it is not recommended widely, or it is shown only to adult accounts.

4. The number of harassment flags drops by half overnight, with no model change. What should you suspect first?

Show answer

A new evasion trick. Adversaries may have found a way past the classifier, such as homoglyphs or text inside images. Treat the drop as a possible attack, check a sample by hand, and add rules while new labels build up.
