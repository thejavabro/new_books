Check Your Understanding

Know the Vocabulary

This lesson uses the terms below many times. Read them once now, and come back here when a later section needs one.

  • Query: The words a user types into the search box, such as "red running shoes size 10."
  • Query understanding: The first stage of search, which cleans up the query, adds words with the same meaning, and works out what the user wants.
  • Retrieval: The stage that pulls a few hundred possible matches out of the whole catalog. It is also called candidate generation, and the matches it returns are called candidates.
  • Recall: Of all the products that would have been good results, the share that retrieval found.
  • Inverted index: A lookup table that maps each word to every product that contains it. It is the classic data structure behind keyword search.
  • Lexical retrieval: Finding products whose text contains the words of the query. BM25 is a common formula for scoring these matches.
  • Embedding: A list of numbers that captures what a query or a product is about. Things the model treats as similar get similar numbers.
  • Semantic retrieval: Finding products whose embeddings are close to the embedding of the query. It matches by meaning instead of exact words.
  • ANN index: An approximate nearest neighbor index, which finds the closest embeddings quickly without comparing the query with every product.
  • Ranker: A model that scores each candidate for one query and sorts the list. Training such a model is called learning to rank.
  • NDCG: Short for normalized discounted cumulative gain, an offline metric that checks whether the best results appear near the top of the list.
  • Position bias: The habit of clicking results near the top partly because they are near the top, not only because they are the best.
  • Preference pair: A training example that says one result was better than another for the same query.
  • Reformulation: Retyping or changing the query right after seeing the results.
  • Hard filter: A rule that removes every product that does not match, such as "size 10 only."
  • Business layer: The last stage before the results page. It applies stock filters, boosts, and sponsored results.

Part 1: Frame the Problem

What the Query Changes

The recommendation lesson built a funnel for a video homepage. Search uses the same funnel shape. But search adds one input that recommendations do not have: the query.

In recommendations, the system must guess what the user wants from their history. In search, the user typed what they want. That is the single biggest difference between the two problems. The system no longer guesses the intent. Its job is to understand the words and find the products that match them.

The query brings new problems, too. It is short, often only two or three words, and it may be misspelled. It may also use different words than the catalog uses. And a wrong result is easy to notice, because the user said exactly what they wanted.

Three Jobs, in Order

A search system does three jobs, in this order.

  1. Understand the query. Fix the spelling, add words that mean the same thing, and find the product type and the attributes the user asked for.

  2. Retrieve. Pull a few hundred possible matches out of the whole catalog.

  3. Rank. Score those matches and sort them, so the best ones appear first.

A business layer then adjusts the final page, and Part 5 walks through every stage.

The Scale

This lesson designs product search for a large online store. The numbers are illustrative assumptions for the interview, not facts about a real store.

  • The catalog holds 100 million products from many sellers.
  • Peak traffic is 10,000 queries per second (10K QPS).
  • Results must appear in about 300 milliseconds (ms). An example target is a p95 under 300 ms, which means 95 percent of searches finish within that time.

Notice what these numbers rule out. No ranking model can affordably score 100 million products in 300 ms, 10,000 times a second. So search needs the same funnel as recommendations. Cheap stages look at many products, and expensive stages look at a short list.

Then state the scope in a sentence or two. For example, say that you will design text search over the product catalog. Autocomplete, image search, and the ad auction stay out of scope. Confirm that scope with the interviewer before you go on.

Part 2: Define Success

Offline: Is the Best Result Near the Top?

Before a new model reaches users, it is graded on past queries. Two numbers do most of the work.

Recall@500 grades retrieval. Of the products that are good results for a query, how many made it into the 500 candidates? A product missed here cannot be ranked later.

NDCG grades the ranking. It checks whether the best results rank near the top. Each result gets a relevance grade, such as perfect, good, fair, or bad. Results lower on the page count for less. The total is then divided by the score of a perfect order, so 1.0 is the ideal page. NDCG@10 looks only at the top 10 results, because most users look only there.

The grades come from a judged set. A judged set is a fixed list of queries whose results human reviewers graded, using written guidelines. It is slow to build, but its grades do not depend on where a result was shown. Offline Evaluation: Choosing the Metric explains NDCG in more depth.

Online: Did the User Find It?

Offline grades decide which models are worth testing. Real users decide which model ships. A good online setup has one primary metric and a few guardrail metrics.

  • The primary metric decides a launch. For a store, it is often search conversion, the share of searches that lead to a purchase. Revenue per search is a common alternative.
  • Guardrail metrics must not get worse. Examples are the zero-results rate, the reformulation rate, and p95 latency.

Watch reformulation closely, because a user who retypes the query right away is telling you the results failed. It is one of the clearest warning signs in search. Some users refine a query on purpose, such as adding a size. So read it together with the rest of the session.

Other signals are less clear. A user who leaves without a click may have failed to find anything. Or the user may have seen the price on the results page and decided not to buy, so interpret no-click searches in context.

Part 3: Map the Data

Three Data Sources

  1. The catalog. Every product has a title, a description, a brand, and a category. It also has attributes such as color and size, a price, and a stock count. Sellers change prices and stock all day.

  2. Search logs. Every query, every result shown with its position, and what the user did next. The user may click, add to cart, buy, or reformulate.

  3. Judged sets. Queries with results graded by human reviewers, as Part 2 described.

Query Frequency Is Zipfian

Search logs show a strong pattern: query frequency is Zipfian. That means a small set of popular queries, such as "phone case" or "running shoes," repeats again and again. Behind them sits a long tail of rare queries, and many of them appear only once.

This pattern matters for the design in two ways. The popular queries, called head queries, are worth caching, because many users request the same results. The rare queries, called long-tail queries, are where search fails most often. They need the most help from query understanding and semantic retrieval.

Clicks Are Biased Labels

The search logs supply labels for the ranker. A click, an add-to-cart, or a purchase marks a result as good for that query. But search clicks are especially dangerous labels.

The reason is position bias: people click the top result partly because it is first, not only because it is best. Many users trust the order of the results, so the first result gets clicks it did not earn. A ranker trained on raw clicks learns to keep first whatever was already first. Its old mistakes become its new labels.

The best fix for search is to learn from preference pairs within one session. Look at what the user saw and what the user did. Suppose the user skipped results 1 and 2 and bought result 3. That gives two pairs for this query. Result 3 is better than result 1, and result 3 is better than result 2. Each pair compares two products that the same user saw at the same moment. So a pair says more about relevance than a raw click count does.

The diagram below shows how one session becomes preference pairs.

Notice that result 4 gives no pair. The user may never have looked that far down. So pairs come only from results above the one the user chose.

Pairs reduce position bias, but they do not remove it. Users still look at higher results more often. To check the correction, swap the order of two neighboring results for a small share of traffic. This shows how much position alone changes clicks.

Those swap results also support a second common correction, called propensity weighting. The swaps estimate how often users look at each position. A click on a rarely viewed position then counts for more in training, and a click on the top position counts for less.

Part 4: Pick the Model

Start With a Baseline

The simplest search that works is lexical retrieval with BM25. Sort the matches by their BM25 score, with a small boost for popular products. It is fast, needs no training, and handles exact words well. It also gives every later model a number to beat.

Query Understanding: Three Jobs

The query-understanding pipeline has three jobs. Take the query "red runing shoes sz 10."

  1. Normalize and spell-check. Change the text to lowercase, expand short forms such as "sz" to "size," and fix "runing" to "running."

  2. Expand synonyms. Add "sneakers" and "trainers," which mean the same as "running shoes" in this store. Synonym lists often come from the search logs. Two phrases are likely synonyms when queries that use them lead to the same purchases.

  3. Extract intent. Find the category, running shoes, and the attributes, color red and size 10.

Extracted attributes such as color and size become hard filters, not just ranking hints. A size 9 shoe is not a slightly worse result for "size 10." It is a wrong result. So when the parser is confident, retrieval returns only red products in size 10. When the parser is not confident, the attribute becomes a ranking feature instead. Then a wrong guess cannot hide good products.

Each of these steps is a small model or a lookup table. Together they fit in about 15 ms in the example budget of Part 5.

Two Retrieval Engines

Lexical retrieval uses an inverted index, and BM25 scores the matches. It is exact, so it finds the model number "HX-200" and rare words that an embedding may blur.

Its weakness is vocabulary mismatch, where the user and the catalog use different words for the same thing. Suppose a user searches for "something to keep coffee hot" in the box. The right product is titled "insulated travel mug" in the catalog. No words overlap, so lexical retrieval misses it.

Semantic retrieval uses embeddings from a two-tower model. One tower turns the query into an embedding. The other tower turns each product into an embedding. Training places each query close to the products that people bought after typing it. Embeddings and Two-Tower Retrieval explains how a two-tower model is trained and served. Its towers encode users and items, while here they encode queries and products. Product embeddings are computed ahead of time and stored in an ANN index. At request time, the system embeds the query and pulls the nearest products.

Semantic retrieval fixes vocabulary mismatch, because the embeddings of "keep coffee hot" and "insulated travel mug" end up close together. Its weakness is that it can confidently retrieve the wrong item entirely. For "HX-200 filter," it may return the HX-250 filter, because the two embeddings are almost the same. To a buyer, the wrong filter is useless.

Modern search systems run both engines at the same time, because they catch different failures. Lexical retrieval catches exact terms, and semantic retrieval catches meaning.

The Ranker and Its Three Kinds of Features

The ranker is a learning-to-rank model. A common choice is gradient-boosted trees: many small decision trees whose scores add up. It trains on the preference pairs from Part 3. It uses three kinds of features.

Feature kindWhat it describesExamples
Query featuresThe query alonequery length, predicted category, how often the query is searched
Product featuresThe product aloneaverage rating, sales last week, price, return rate
Pairwise featuresHow well one product matches one queryBM25 score, embedding distance, brand in the query, past purchases for this query and product

Here, "pairwise" means one query paired with one product. It is not the same as a preference pair. Pairwise features describe how well one specific item matches one specific query. They usually matter most, because a popular product can still be wrong for this query.

A new product has no sales or clicks yet, so its history features are empty. Features that need no history, such as the BM25 score and the embedding distance, still let it rank for the right queries. Some stores also give new products a small, capped boost for a short time, so they can collect their first clicks.

Two Levels: L1 and L2

Running the best ranker on all 500 candidates is too slow and too costly at 10K QPS, so ranking has two levels.

  • The L1 ranker is a cheap model with a few strong features. It trims the 500 candidates to about 100.
  • The L2 ranker is a heavier model with all the features, and it carefully finishes ranking those 100.

Why Not Rank With an LLM?

A large language model (LLM) can judge quite well whether a product matches a query. So why not ask it to rank every candidate? Latency and cost rule it out on the live path at 10K QPS, as Part 7 shows. LLMs can still help offline. For example, they can grade query-product pairs to grow the judged set, with human reviewers checking a sample.

Part 5: Design the Serving Path

The diagram below shows one query moving through the system. The candidate counts are example values.

The steps below follow the query "red runing shoes sz 10." The times are an example budget, not a guarantee.

Step 1: Check the cache. In about 2 ms, the system checks whether a head query already has a ranked list in the cache. If it does, the system skips to Step 6.

Step 2: Understand the query. In about 15 ms, the pipeline fixes the spelling, adds synonyms, and turns red and size 10 into hard filters.

Step 3: Retrieve from both engines at once. This step takes about 50 ms. An inverted index for 100 million products is too large and too busy for one machine to search quickly. So it is split into shards, and each shard holds part of the catalog. The query goes to every shard at the same time, and each shard returns its best matches. Then one service merges them, in a pattern called scatter-gather.

The slowest shard sets the total time. So each shard has a timeout, and a copy of a shard, called a replica, can answer when one is slow. The ANN index is split the same way. The two engines return about 500 merged candidates. Hybrid Search and Reranking shows a common way to merge two ranked lists.

Step 4: Rank in two levels. This step takes about 80 ms in total. The L1 ranker trims 500 candidates to about 100 in about 20 ms. The L2 ranker orders those 100 in about 60 ms.

Step 5: Apply the business layer. In about 10 ms, stock filters remove products that cannot ship. Boosts lift some products, such as those with fast delivery. Governed sponsored slots place paid products in fixed positions.

Step 6: Fetch fresh price and stock. In about 20 ms, the page gets each price and stock level as it is now, not as it was at ranking time. This is called fetching at render time, because it happens while the page is built. A product that sold out since ranking is dropped from the page.

Step 7: Log everything. The system records the query, each result with its position, the features the rankers used, and the model versions. Later it adds what the user did. These logs become the next preference pairs.

These steps add up to under 180 ms. That leaves room for the network inside the 300 ms target.

Personalization Stays Inside Relevance

Two users who type "running shoes" may prefer different brands. So the L2 ranker can use personal features, such as favorite brands or a usual size. But personalization reweights results within relevance. It is always capped, and it never overrides relevance.

For example, a favorite brand may move up a few places among results that already match well. It must never push a weak match above a strong one. For a query with one clear target, such as a model number, personal features should change almost nothing. Because the change is small and capped, it can also run on top of a cached list.

Revenue and Relevance

The business layer faces a real tension. Sponsored products pay for their place. Some products earn the store a higher profit. Both create pressure to rank them higher.

Name this tension in the interview, and handle it as a governed trade-off. A sponsored product must pass a relevance-quality floor, which is a minimum relevance score for this query. It appears only in fixed slots, with a "Sponsored" label. The product, ads, and search teams agree on these rules. Guardrail metrics, such as conversion and reformulation, check that users are not hurt. Without the floor, revenue slowly replaces relevance, and users stop trusting the results.

When Nothing Matches

Some queries return zero results, and an empty page is the weakest response. First, diagnose the cause.

  • No inventory: the store does not sell the product.
  • Vocabulary mismatch: the store sells it, but under different words.

Semantic retrieval helps tell them apart. If it finds close products that buyers bought for similar queries, the cause is probably a mismatch.

Then fall back in order. Relax the least confident hard filter first, and then try semantic results alone. If nothing fits, show close alternatives with a clear message that they are not exact matches. Log every zero-results query, because mismatches become new synonyms, and missing products become requests for the catalog team.

Part 6: Keep It Working

Two Indexes, Two Speeds

The two indexes refresh at different speeds. The inverted index takes updates almost instantly, often within a second or two. A new product or a changed title becomes searchable right away.

In many systems, the ANN index refreshes on a cycle, such as every few hours or nightly. A model must compute each new embedding, and the index must then be rebuilt or extended. Some vector indexes can add new items one at a time, but a batch cycle is still common. This gap is one more reason to run both engines. A product added an hour ago can be found by lexical retrieval before the ANN index knows about it.

The Retraining Loop

An example schedule, to check against real data, looks like this.

  • Retrain the rankers daily or weekly from new preference pairs.
  • Update synonym lists and spelling models from the search logs.
  • Recompute product embeddings and refresh the ANN index nightly.

Every new ranker passes three gates. First, NDCG on the judged set must not drop, overall or for important groups of queries. Second, the ranker runs in shadow mode: it scores live queries, but users never see its results. Third, an A/B test compares it with the current ranker on search conversion, with the guardrail metrics watching. In an A/B test, a random share of users gets the new ranker, and everyone else keeps the current one. Model Serving Basics describes shadow mode and safe rollouts in more detail.

What to Monitor

SignalWhat a change can reveal
Zero-results ratea broken synonym list, a failed index update, or a new trend the catalog lacks

Clicks on sold-out productsa stale cache or a slow stock update

Part 7: Count the Costs

Three Cost Levers

At 10K QPS, three levers control most of the cost.

1. The cache hit rate. Query frequency is Zipfian, so a cache of head queries serves a large share of traffic. For example, if 30 percent of searches hit the cache, retrieval and ranking run for only 7,000 searches per second.

2. The L1/L2 split. The heavy L2 model scores 100 candidates instead of 500. Without the cache, that is 1 million heavy scores per second at peak instead of 5 million.

3. No LLM call for each item. An LLM call for each of 100 candidates means 1 million LLM calls per second. Assume a small model, at an illustrative price of one tenth of a cent per call. That costs about 40 million per day. Each call can also take hundreds of milliseconds, which leaves no room in a 300 ms budget.

Memory needs a quick estimate too. Assume 100 million products with 128 numbers per embedding and 4 bytes per number. That is 51.2 GB of raw vectors, before index structures and replicas.

What Breaks

The parts of search can fail independently. That helps, because search can get worse without going down.

  • The ANN index goes down. Lexical retrieval keeps serving results. Exact and head queries barely notice, because lexical retrieval already handles them well. Long-tail matches suffer, such as descriptive queries that share no words with the catalog.
  • Query understanding goes down. Send the raw query to both engines, with no filters. Search still works, but misspelled queries and attribute queries, such as "size 10," get worse results.
  • The L2 ranker times out. Show the L1 order instead, which is a little worse but fast.
  • One shard is slow to answer. Return results from the other shards after the timeout, and log the partial result.

The most dangerous failure is silent: a stale cache. Imagine a cached results page from yesterday. It still lists a product that sold out this morning, at yesterday's price. No error appears anywhere, yet the site quietly keeps selling yesterday's inventory. Buyers find out only at checkout.

The fix is to cache only the ranking, which is the ordered list of product IDs. Price and stock change fast, so they are fetched fresh at render time, when the page is built in Step 6. Cached lists also expire after a short time, such as a few minutes.

In the Interview

The 30-second answer: "Search is the ranking funnel with a query at the front. Query understanding fixes spelling, expands synonyms, and turns attributes such as size into hard filters. Lexical and semantic retrieval run in parallel, because one catches exact terms and the other catches meaning. Together they return about 500 candidates. A cheap L1 ranker trims them to 100, and a heavy L2 ranker finishes the order. A business layer applies stock filters, boosts, and sponsored slots above a relevance floor. I train on in-session preference pairs, because raw clicks carry position bias. NDCG gates new rankers offline, and an A/B test on search conversion decides. Head queries are cached, but price and stock are always fetched fresh."

Likely follow-ups:

  1. "Why not use embeddings for everything?" Because semantic retrieval can confidently return the wrong item. A buyer who types "HX-200 filter" needs that exact part, and only lexical matching guarantees the exact term. Run both, and let the ranker weigh an exact match as a strong pairwise feature.

  2. "How would this design change for app-store search?" Exact title and brand matching dominate retrieval. Many users type the name of an app they already know. The catalog is much smaller, so the funnel can be shorter. Semantic retrieval still helps with queries such as "app to track my runs."

  3. "Sales wants high-profit products ranked higher. What do you say?" Name it as a governed trade-off. Boosts are allowed only among results that pass a relevance-quality floor. Then an A/B test checks that conversion and reformulation do not get worse.

Common Mistakes

Mistake 1: Jumping Straight to Embeddings

A weak answer opens with embedding retrieval and nothing else. A strong answer runs lexical and semantic retrieval in parallel and names what each would miss. Lexical retrieval misses different words for the same thing. Semantic retrieval misses exact model numbers and rare terms.

Mistake 2: Training on Raw Clicks

Raw clicks reward whatever was already ranked first. Build preference pairs within each session, and check the correction with a small randomized slice of traffic.

Mistake 3: Treating Attributes as Hints

When a user types "size 10," a size 9 shoe is a wrong result. Turn confident attributes into hard filters, and keep uncertain ones as ranking features.

Mistake 4: Caching the Whole Page

A cached page with prices and stock will show sold-out products at old prices. Cache only the ranking, and fetch price and stock at render time.

Mistake 5: Leaving Revenue Ungoverned

Sponsored slots and profit boosts are normal. Placing them without a relevance-quality floor slowly teaches users to distrust search.

Mistake 6: Showing an Empty Page

A zero-results page loses the sale. Diagnose no inventory versus vocabulary mismatch, then fall back to relaxed filters, semantic results, or close alternatives.

FAQs

Q: Is search just recommendations with a text box?

No. The funnel shape is the same, but the query changes every stage. It adds query understanding, hard filters, exact-term retrieval, and pairwise features. It also makes position bias stronger, because users trust the order of search results.

Q: Do I need to know the BM25 formula for an interview?

No. Know what it rewards: products that contain the query's words, especially rare words. Also know its weakness, which is vocabulary mismatch.

Q: Should search results be personalized?

Yes, but only a little. Personal features can reorder results that already match well. They are capped, and they never push a weak match above a strong one.

Q: Can one model do both retrieval and ranking at this scale?

No. A model good enough to rank well is too costly to run on 100 million products for every query. Retrieval narrows the catalog cheaply, and the rankers spend their effort on a short list.

Key Takeaways

  • The query is the biggest difference from recommendations. The user typed what they want, so the system must understand the words instead of guessing from history.
  • Search does three jobs in order. It understands the query, retrieves candidates, and ranks them. A business layer then adjusts the page.
  • Lexical and semantic retrieval run together. Lexical catches exact terms but suffers from vocabulary mismatch. Semantic catches meaning but can confidently return the wrong item.
  • Search clicks are biased labels. People click the top result partly because it is first. Preference pairs within a session reduce that bias.
  • Two ranking levels and a cache control the cost. L1 trims cheaply, L2 finishes carefully, and head queries come from the cache, with price and stock fetched fresh.
  • In a system design interview, open with both retrieval engines and what each would miss. Then name position bias, the relevance floor for sponsored results, and the stale-cache rule before the interviewer asks.

The next lesson, Design Ad Click Prediction, keeps the ranking idea but adds money. There, a predicted click probability becomes part of an ad's value in an auction, so the probability itself must be accurate.

Check Your Understanding

1. A user searches "HX-200 filter," and the top result is the HX-250 filter. Which retrieval engine most likely caused this, and what fixes it?

Show answer

Semantic retrieval most likely caused it. The two model numbers have almost the same embedding, so semantic retrieval returned a confident wrong match. Lexical retrieval matches the exact term, and the ranker should treat an exact model-number match as a strong pairwise feature.

2. Why does the cache store the ranked list of product IDs but not the prices and stock?

Show answer

Because prices and stock change fast. A cached price or stock level can be wrong within minutes, and nothing shows an error. So the system fetches them fresh at render time, while the ranking itself can be reused for a few minutes.

3. For one query, a user skipped results 1, 2, and 3 and bought result 4. Which preference pairs does this session give?

Show answer

Three pairs: result 4 is better than results 1, 2, and 3. The user likely saw those three results and passed over them. Results below result 4 give no pairs, because the user may never have looked at them.

4. The ANN index fails during peak traffic, while everything else keeps running. What do users notice?

Show answer

Most users notice very little. Lexical retrieval keeps serving, so exact and head queries barely change. Long-tail queries that share no words with the catalog get worse matches until the ANN index returns.
