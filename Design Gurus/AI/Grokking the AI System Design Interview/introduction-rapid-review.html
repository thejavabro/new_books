Check Your Understanding

Know the Vocabulary

This lesson uses the terms below many times. Read them once now, and come back here when a later section needs one.

  • Recall: Bringing an answer back from memory, without looking at it first. This differs from recall as a metric, the share of the right items a model finds, in Modules 2, 3, and 5.
  • Drill: A short, timed practice. You answer one question out loud before you check the written answer.
  • Miss: An answer you could not give, gave wrongly, or gave too slowly. An answer that leaves out the deciding variable also counts as a miss.
  • Miss list: A written list of your misses. Each entry names the question and the lesson that teaches it.
  • Spaced review: Coming back to the same material after a gap of a day or more, instead of reviewing it all at once.
  • Red flag: A sentence that makes a candidate sound less experienced than they are. A common example is a claim with no number, no trade-off, or no failure case.
  • Deciding variable: The one fact about the problem that picks between two or more options. For example, who chooses the next step decides between a workflow and an agent.

Part 1: Why a Review Module

Modules 2 to 7 taught the parts of AI systems, one at a time. Module 8 trained the performance: one question, about 45 minutes, and every step said out loud. This module adds no new material. It helps you keep what you learned and bring it back quickly under pressure.

That second skill is different from understanding. When you reread a lesson, every idea feels familiar. But familiar is not the same as being able to say it. In an interview, nobody shows you the lesson first. You must produce the answer from memory, in a few sentences, while someone listens.

The gap shows up in small moments. An interviewer asks, "Why not fine-tune the model?" A candidate who studied the topic knows the answer somewhere. Then the words come slowly, or the deciding variable is missing. Another candidate says two clear sentences and names the reason. Both candidates read the same lesson, but only one practiced recall.

What Interviewers Expect

Interviewers ask many short follow-up questions during a design. Each one tests a single idea, such as "What happens when the model is down?" A strong answer is short and exact. It names the choice, the reason, and often a number.

Interviewers also listen to how you say things. Some sentences sound junior even when the design behind them is fine. Saying "the system should be fast and accurate" is one example. The strong version gives a success metric, a latency target, and a cost ceiling, each with a number.

Finally, interviewers watch what you bring up without being asked. How to Run a Case Study describes the strong-hire bar. A strong candidate raises the feedback loop, the risks, and the cost unprompted. You can do that only if those ideas come back to you quickly.

Part 2: The Three Review Tools

The Definition

This module gives you three review tools. Each one trains a different skill, and each item in it links to the lesson that teaches it.

  1. Rapid-fire questions train recall. They are short questions grouped by module, across the whole course. Each answer takes one to three sentences, and a pass must name the deciding variable.

  2. Red flags train your wording. They are twelve statements that make a candidate sound junior. For each one, the lesson explains why it is weak and gives a stronger sentence to say instead. It also names three habits that sound junior across a whole interview.

  3. Decision cheat sheets train your choices. They put the course's main decisions into compact tables, one table for each decision. Each table leads with the deciding variable. Its rows show what you might see in a question and the option that it picks.

The table below compares the three tools.

ToolWhat it trainsWhat a miss tells you
Rapid-fire questionsRecalling one fact or idea in a few sentencesYou cannot yet explain that idea without help.
Red flagsSaying a sound idea in a strong wayYour words hide what you know, or skip a trade-off.
Decision cheat sheetsChoosing between options for a stated reasonYou know the options, but not the fact that decides between them.

Notice that each tool finds a different kind of gap. A candidate can know a fact and still choose badly. Another candidate can choose well and still say it in a weak way. So use all three tools, not only the one that feels easiest.

What the Review Module Is Not

The review module is not a replacement for the lessons. Each item is a short summary. When you miss an item, the lesson behind it holds the full reasoning, the example, and the numbers.

The review module is not a list of lines to memorize. Interviewers change one number or one constraint, and a memorized line stops fitting. Learn the reason behind each answer, so you can adapt it.

The review module is not for reading. Reading an answer that you already know feels like progress, but it tests nothing. Every item works only if you answer it out loud before you look.

Part 3: How to Drill

A drill has five steps. Use the same steps for all three tools. The rapid-fire lesson groups the same work into four steps.

Step 1: Cover the answer. Read only the question, the weak statement, or the decision. Keep the answer hidden. In the rapid-fire lesson, each answer stays closed until you open it.

Step 2: Answer out loud, on a timer. Speak your answer as you would to an interviewer. As an example limit, give yourself about 30 seconds for each item. Speaking takes longer than thinking, and it shows where your words run out.

Step 3: Check, and mark the misses. Open the written answer and compare it with yours. Mark the item as a miss if you could not answer, if you answered wrongly, or if you ran out of time. Also mark it if you left out the deciding variable, or a number that the answer needs.

Step 4: Go back to the lesson. Follow the link on each miss. Reread the part of the lesson that teaches it, not the whole lesson. Then write one sentence of the answer in your own words, on your miss list.

Step 5: Drill the miss again on a later day. After a gap of at least a day, answer the item again, out loud and timed. A simple rule works well: remove an item only after two correct answers in a row, on two different days.

The diagram below shows the same five steps.

Notice that a correct answer costs about 30 seconds and moves on. A miss costs more, because it sends you back to the lesson. That is the point of the drill. It spends your study time on what you do not yet know, and very little on what you already know.

Why the Gap Between Days Matters

An answer you reread five minutes ago is still fresh, so it proves little. An answer you can give after a day has passed is closer to the interview. There, the lesson may be weeks behind you. So spread your drills across several days, and do not repeat one item many times in one sitting.

What a Good Answer Sounds Like

A good drill answer has the same shape as a good interview answer. It states the choice first, then the reason. Where the course gave a number, it uses the number and says that it is an example. Where the course named a failure case or a fallback, it names that too.

Part 4: A Review Schedule

The three tools work best on a schedule, not in one long session. The plan below assumes about two weeks before the interview and about 45 minutes a day. Both numbers are illustrative assumptions. Shrink or stretch each stage to the time you have.

WhenMain toolWhat to do
About two weeks beforeRapid-fire questionsDrill one or two modules a day, until every module is covered. Start your miss list.
About one week beforeDecision cheat sheetsCover the right-hand columns of the summary table, and read one decision at a time. Say its deciding variable, then the option it picks. Drill your miss list.
About three days beforeRed flagsRead each weak statement, and say a stronger version before you read the lesson's version. Run one full case study from Module 8, recorded.
The day beforeYour miss list onlyDrill the items still on the list. Reread the 45-minute clock in How to Run a Case Study. Start no new material.

The diagram below shows the same schedule as a timeline.

Notice that the miss list runs through every stage. The tools change, but your misses come with you. By the last day, the miss list is the only review tool left.

Keep the Case Studies in the Plan

The review tools drill single ideas. An interview also needs those ideas joined into one answer, on a clock. So keep running case studies from Module 8 during the review. Each run should be out loud, timed, and recorded. Then grade it against the five dimensions in How to Run a Case Study. Each gap from a run goes onto the same miss list. The example schedule holds one run, three days before the interview. With more time, add a run each week.

When You Have Less Time

With only a few days, do one short pass of each tool. Start with the modules closest to the role, such as the LLM and agent modules for an LLM role. Then cover every other module once, more briefly. The later modules reuse Module 2 constantly, so a gap there shows up in many designs.

FAQs

Q: Can I use this module instead of the lessons?

No. Each review item is a short summary of a lesson. It tells you whether you know an idea, but it does not teach the idea. When you miss an item, the lesson is where you learn it.

Q: Should I drill every item, even the ones I find easy?

Yes, once. An easy item still costs only about 30 seconds, and it sometimes turns out to be a miss. After one correct pass, spend your time on the miss list.

Q: Is it enough to think the answer silently?

No. A silent answer skips the hard part, which is finding the words. Answer out loud, as you would in the interview.

Q: Do I have to follow the two-week schedule?

No. The schedule is an example. Keep its order and its habits, and scale the stages to the time you have. Recall comes first, then decisions, then wording, and the miss list runs through all of it.

Key Takeaways

  • This module trains recall, not new content. In an interview, you must produce each answer from memory, quickly, and out loud.
  • The three tools find three kinds of gaps. Rapid-fire questions test what you know. Red flags test how you say it. Decision cheat sheets test how you choose.
  • Every drill follows five steps. Cover the answer, and answer out loud on a timer. Mark your misses, reread their lessons, and drill each miss on a later day.
  • The miss list is your real study plan. It grows from all three tools and from recorded case study runs, and it shrinks only after correct answers on different days.
  • Spread the review across days. An answer you can give after a gap is closer to what the interview demands.
  • In a system design interview, answer each follow-up with the choice first, then the deciding reason, and a number where the course gave one.

The next lesson, Rapid-Fire Questions, starts the first tool. It asks short questions from every module, and each answer links the lesson that teaches it.

Check Your Understanding

1. You reread a lesson, and every idea feels familiar. Are you ready to answer questions on it in an interview?

Show answer

Not necessarily. Feeling familiar while reading is easier than producing the answer from memory. Test it with a drill: cover the answer, say it out loud on a timer, and then check it.

2. You answer a rapid-fire question correctly, but you leave out the fact that decides the choice. Is it a miss?

Show answer

Yes. An answer without its deciding variable will not hold up when the interviewer asks why. Mark it, reread the part of the lesson that gives the reason, and drill it again on a later day.

3. Which tool would catch a candidate who knows both options well but cannot say when to pick each one?

Show answer

The decision cheat sheets. Each table leads with the deciding variable, the fact that picks between the options. A candidate who knows the options but not the variable misses the row, even when the option they pick is right.

4. Why should an item stay on the miss list until you answer it correctly on two different days?

Show answer

Because a correct answer right after rereading proves little. The answer is still fresh in memory. A correct answer after a gap of a day or more shows that you can recall it, which is closer to the interview.
