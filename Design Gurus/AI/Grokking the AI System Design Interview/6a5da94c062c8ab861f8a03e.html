Check Your Understanding

Know the Vocabulary

This lesson uses the terms below many times. Read them once now, and come back here when a later section needs one.

  • Connection graph: The record of who is connected to whom. Each member is a point, and each connection between two members is a line, called an edge.
  • Mutual connection: A person connected to both of two members. If Ana and Chen both know Ben, then Ben is their mutual connection.
  • Topology: The structure of the connection graph itself: who links to whom, and how closely.
  • Triangle closing: Suggesting two members who share a mutual connection. It is also called friends-of-friends, because the suggested person is a connection of a connection.
  • Affiliation: Something two members share, such as the same company, school, or group. It is a stronger signal when they were there at the same time.
  • Imported contacts: An address book that a member uploads, with consent, so the network can find people they already know.
  • Embedding: A list of numbers that captures what a member is about. Members the model treats as similar get similar numbers.
  • Candidate: One member the system might suggest to the viewer.
  • Impression: One suggestion shown to one member, one time.
  • Impression discounting: Lowering a suggestion's score each time it is shown and ignored, and dropping it once it has been ignored too often.
  • Key-value (KV) store: A database that returns a stored value when you give it a key, such as a member ID. One lookup often takes about a millisecond.
  • Idempotent: A job is idempotent when running it twice gives the same result as running it once.
  • Fail soft and fail closed: Two ways to behave when a part breaks. Fail soft means keep working in a reduced way. Fail closed means stop the action completely.
  • Guardrail metric: A number that must not get worse, even when the primary metric improves.
  • Holdback group: A small slice of members kept on the old version for weeks, so the long-term effect can be measured.

The Exercise

Read the question below, then stop and answer it yourself before you read on.

"We run a professional network with about 900 million members. Design the People You May Know module. It suggests members you might want to connect with, on the home page and the network page."

Give yourself about 40 minutes, and say your answer out loud. Use the seven steps of Module 1's framework. The steps are: frame the problem, define success, map the data, pick the model, design the serving path, keep it working, and count the costs.

Then compare your answer with the reference answer below. It reuses ideas from the earlier designs of this module, and Part 8 lists where each idea came from. All numbers are illustrative assumptions, not figures from a real company.

Part 1: Frame the Problem

Step 1 names the system and what is new about it. Say which known design the question is based on before you draw anything.

This is a recommendation system in which the items are people. It has the ranking shape: one request arrives, and the system picks about 10 members out of 900 million. So the funnel from Design a Recommendation System applies. Candidate generation finds a few hundred possible members. A ranker puts them in order. A rule layer, the re-ranking stage, shapes the final list.

Two things make this question harder than recommending videos.

  1. The goal has two sides. A video has no opinion about who watches it. A person does. A suggestion is only good if the viewer sends an invite and the other member accepts it.

  2. A bad suggestion can be a privacy incident. A bad video suggestion wastes a few seconds, but a bad people suggestion can reveal a link that someone wanted to keep private. Suppose two patients of the same clinic both uploaded the clinic's phone number. If the system suggests them to each other, it has revealed something about their health.

This gap is a social-cost asymmetry: a wrong suggestion costs far more when the item is a person. Name it yourself, without waiting for the interviewer to ask. It shapes the whole design, because privacy rules must hold even when other parts fail.

Then state the scope out loud.

  • In scope: the suggestion list on the home page and the network page, for every member.
  • Out of scope for now: suggestions in email, job and company suggestions, and the invite flow itself.

Confirm it with one question: "Does that scope match what you want to discuss?"

Part 2: Define Success

Step 2 turns "good suggestions" into numbers. Start by naming the metric that looks right but is not.

The Trap in Acceptance Rate

Acceptance rate is the share of sent invites that the other member accepts. It is easy to measure, so many candidates make it the primary metric, but it can be gamed.

The easiest way to raise acceptance rate is to suggest easy, low-value reconnections. Think of an old classmate the member has not spoken to in years. The member sends an invite, and the classmate accepts out of politeness. Neither one ever sends a message, so the metric goes up while the network gains nothing.

What Network Value Means

The real goal is network value. Network value means connections that lead to real engagement and follow-through. Examples are a message exchanged, a profile visit, an introduction, or a job referral in the weeks after the connection.

Network value is slow to measure, because it takes weeks to know whether a new connection was worth anything. So it cannot be the number that decides a one-week test.

Three Metric Layers

The design uses three layers of metrics, and each one has its own job.

LayerMetricIts job
Primary metricAccepted invites per member who sees the moduleDecides most tests
Guardrail metricsIgnore rate and report rate, plus the viewer's dismiss rateStops a launch if suggestions annoy or scare people
North starDownstream network value, measured weeks laterChecks that accepted invites become real connections

The ignore rate is the share of received invites that the other member ignores. The report rate is the share that the other member reports, for example with the option "I don't know this person" in the menu. Both are measured on the receiving side. A ranker that floods members with unwanted invites will show up here first. The dismiss rate covers the other side: how often viewers close suggestions they do not want.

Notice that accepted invites, not sent invites, form the primary metric. A ranker that only raised sent invites could still annoy the people who receive them.

Accepted invites can still rise through easy reconnections, just as acceptance rate can. That is the north star's job. Those connections never lead to messages or visits, so network value exposes them weeks later.

Latency and Scale

The module must load within about 200 milliseconds (ms). That is an example target, not a guarantee.

Assume about 300 million members were active in the last 30 days. Assume the module loads about 150 million times a day. That is about 1,700 requests per second on average. A KV store handles that load easily.

Part 3: Map the Data

Step 3 asks where candidates and labels come from. This design has four candidate sources, and each one has its own strength and cost.

The Strongest Signal: Topology

The strongest signal for candidate generation is topology, the structure of the connection graph itself. People who share many mutual connections often know each other. This is the idea behind triangle closing.

The diagram below shows triangle closing for one member. Ana and Chen are not connected, but both know Ben and Eve.

Notice that the count of mutual connections is not the whole signal. A mutual connection who is a close colleague says more than a mutual connection with 30,000 connections. Almost everyone is connected to that second person, so sharing them proves little.

The Four Candidate Sources

SourceWhat it findsStrengthCost or risk
Triangle closingConnections of the member's connectionsThe strongest signal for members with a graphHeavy to compute at scale
Imported contactsPeople in the member's uploaded address bookHighest precisionThe heaviest privacy obligation
AffiliationsPeople from the same company, school, or groupWorks before a member has any connectionsWeak alone, since a big company has many strangers
EmbeddingsMembers with similar embeddings, found by searching for the closest onesFinds people the other sources missA model and an index to build and maintain

Why Imported Contacts Cut Both Ways

Imported contacts have the highest precision of any source. If a member has someone's phone number, they usually know that person.

But contacts also carry the heaviest privacy obligation. The people in the address book never agreed to anything. So the design limits how contacts are used:

  • Use them only with the member's clear consent.
  • Use them only for that member's own suggestions.
  • Delete them when the member asks.
  • Never reveal to anyone that their number was uploaded.
  • Never suggest two members only because both uploaded the same third number.

The clinic example from Part 1 came from this source. The two patients did not know each other. They only shared the clinic's number, and the last rule stops that suggestion.

Labels

Each impression can lead to a chain of outcomes, and each outcome arrives at a different time.

  1. Invite sent. The viewer sends an invite, usually within minutes, or never.

  2. Invite accepted, ignored, or reported. The other member answers, sometimes days later. So the label waits for a fixed window, such as 14 days.

  3. Network value. Messages or visits between the two members appear over the following weeks.

Negative actions are labels too. A dismissed suggestion, an ignored invite, and a report all teach the ranker what not to show.

Privacy State

One more data source is not used for prediction at all. It is the privacy state: blocks, hidden connections, and the setting "Appear in suggestions," which a member can turn off. The privacy filter in Part 5 reads it. This data must be fresh, because a block made a minute ago must already apply.

Part 4: Pick the Model

Step 4 starts with the simplest model that works.

The Baseline

The baseline is triangle closing, ranked by the number of mutual connections, and it needs no training. It gives the team a number to beat, and it is a sensible fallback.

What the Ranker Predicts

The learned ranker predicts two probabilities and multiplies them.

  • P(send): the chance that the viewer sends an invite to this candidate.
  • P(accept): the chance that the candidate accepts, once the invite is sent.

The ranking score is P(send) times P(accept). That product is the chance that this suggestion becomes an accepted connection.

The two terms matter because both members have to want the edge, not just the viewer. This is why the design differs from ordinary item ranking, where a video ranker only asks whether the viewer will click. A video has no opinion, but a person does.

Here is an example with two candidates. The numbers are illustrative.

CandidateP(send)P(accept)Score
A well-known chief executive0.300.050.015
A former teammate0.100.600.06

The viewer is three times more likely to invite the chief executive, but the executive rarely accepts invites from strangers. So the former teammate ranks first, with four times the score.

Because the two probabilities are multiplied, each one must be accurate, not just well ordered. This is calibration, meaning predicted probabilities match real rates. Design Ad Click Prediction explains why a probability that feeds a multiplication must be calibrated.

Features

The ranker uses features from both members and from the pair.

  • The pair: how many mutual connections they share, and how strong those links are. It also covers shared affiliations, any overlap in time, and which source found the candidate.
  • The viewer: how often they send invites, and how they responded to past suggestions.
  • The candidate: how often they accept invites, and how often they ignore them.

Two gradient-boosted tree models, one for each probability, are a reasonable start. A small neural network with two outputs also works. Both options run well on ordinary CPUs.

P(accept) learns only from invites that were really sent. It never sees how a candidate would answer an invite nobody sent. So its estimates are least reliable for candidates the old ranker rarely showed.

The Rule Layer

After ranking, a rule layer shapes the final list. Three rules matter most.

  1. Impression discounting. Each time a suggestion is shown and ignored, its score drops. After three ignores, for example, the suggestion stops appearing for good. A member who has passed over the same suggestion three times has already given an answer.

  2. Exposure caps. No member is suggested more than a set number of times a day across the network. Part 6 explains why.

  3. Source variety. The list mixes sources, so it is not all one company or one school.

The Whole Funnel

The diagram below shows the funnel for one member, with example counts. The four sources, a privacy filter, and the ranker run in the nightly job. A second privacy check and the rules run on each request.

Notice that the privacy filter sits before the ranker, not after it. Part 5 explains why that placement matters.

Part 5: Design the Serving Path

Step 5 decides what runs ahead of time and what runs per request.

Why This Is Mostly Batch

Triangle closing across 900 million members is far too heavy to run while a member waits, and most of its inputs change slowly. So the design precomputes the lists.

The serving pattern combines a nightly batch with a streaming path.

  • Nightly KV precompute. A batch job generates candidates for each active member and ranks them. It writes the top 200, for example, into the KV store under the member's ID.
  • Streamed freshness updates. An event stream carries dismissals, new connections, blocks, and contact uploads. A streaming job applies each one to the member's stored list within seconds.

Batch vs Online Inference explains when to precompute into a KV store like this.

The diagram below shows both paths and the request that reads from them.

Notice that the request does almost no work, since it only reads one stored list, checks it, and shapes it.

One Request, Step by Step

Step 1: The home page opens. The app asks the suggestion service for this member's list.

Step 2: The stored list is read. One KV lookup returns the precomputed candidates. Streamed writes have already removed anyone dismissed or connected since last night.

Step 3: The privacy filter checks it. The filter compares each candidate with the current privacy state. It drops anyone blocked, anyone with "Appear in suggestions" turned off, and any pair covered by a privacy rule.

Step 4: The rules shape the list. Impression discounting and the exposure caps apply, and about 10 suggestions remain.

Step 5: Profiles are added. The service adds names, photos, and headlines, and returns the list.

StageExample budget
Routing and checks5 ms
KV read2 ms
Privacy filter10 ms, with a 30 ms timeout
Rules5 ms
Profile details20 ms
Network and response20 ms

A typical request takes about 60 ms, well inside the 200 ms target.

Why Dismissals Cannot Wait for the Batch

Suppose a member dismisses a suggestion for Dana. If Dana returns on the next page load, the system seems to ignore the member's clear choice. Re-showing a dismissed suggestion right after reads as creepy.

So a dismissal must take effect through a streamed write, not the nightly batch. The streaming job removes Dana from the stored list within seconds. It also adds the pair to a lasting "never suggest again" list, which the nightly job reads too.

New Members

A brand-new member has no connections, so triangle closing has nothing to work with. Contacts and affiliations carry the first session, since there is no graph yet.

When the member signs up, they may import contacts and list a company and a school. The streaming path builds a starter list from those within seconds. As the member accepts their first invites, triangle closing begins to work. The next nightly run then treats the member like everyone else.

Filter at Retrieval, Not at Display

The privacy filter runs at retrieval, meaning wherever candidates are generated or read. It runs in the nightly job, before ranking. It runs again on each request, before any rule shapes the list. It never waits for the display step.

Filtering only at display time is not enough, because the excluded member would still sit in stored lists, logs, and other surfaces. Nobody could prove they never appeared.

Every exclusion is also logged, with which member was dropped, which rule dropped them, and which filter version ran. So the team can prove to a legal reviewer that a member with suggestions turned off never appeared. A nightly check also scans every stored list for excluded members, and it alerts on any match.

Fail Soft or Fail Closed

The two parts of this design fail in opposite ways, on purpose.

  • The graph batch fails soft. If tonight's job fails, the service keeps serving yesterday's lists. A list that is one day old is still useful, and streamed writes still remove dismissals from it.
  • The privacy filter fails closed. If it times out, the service suppresses the whole module. The page loads without suggestions.

Here a blank screen beats a degraded one. An empty module costs a few invites for a few minutes. An unfiltered list could cause a privacy incident that cannot be undone.

Fail closed is the same rule the Design Fraud Detection lesson applies to large payments when scoring fails. Decide it ahead of time, not during an outage.

Bounding Friends-of-Friends

The nightly job cannot compute the full friends-of-friends set for every member. Here is why.

Suppose a member has 500 connections, and each of them has 500. That is 250,000 two-step paths for one member. Members with 30,000 connections make it far worse. So the job uses three limits.

  1. Cap per-member expansion. Start from only the member's 100 strongest connections, for example. With 500 connections at the next step, that cuts 250,000 paths to 50,000.

  2. Scope by activity. Compute lists only for members active in the last 30 days.

  3. Sample huge accounts. When a member with 30,000 connections is the mutual connection, follow only a random sample of their connections, such as 500. Otherwise that one account would add 30,000 paths by itself. A shared link through them is weak evidence anyway.

The scope rule limits whose lists are computed, not who can be suggested. When an inactive member returns, the streaming path builds a starter list on demand.

Making the Batch Job Safe to Retry

The nightly job is idempotent, because it writes each member's list under a key made of the member's ID and the run date. It also reads a fixed snapshot of the graph and uses a fixed random seed for sampling. So running it again overwrites the same keys with the same results. Serving switches to the new run only after a check confirms that the run is complete.

So a retried or partly failed run never corrupts state. Members keep seeing yesterday's complete lists until tonight's lists are whole.

Part 6: Keep It Working

Step 6 watches the network, not only the model. A suggestion system changes the graph it learns from. Two risks follow from that.

The Rich-Get-Richer Trap

Well-connected members sit on many paths, so they appear in many lists. They receive more invites and gain more connections, and then they sit on even more paths. So well-connected members keep generating even more suggestions, while new members stay hard to find.

The response has two parts.

  • Exposure caps limit how often any one member is suggested each day.
  • Honest exploration gives a small share of slots to under-connected members. The share is approved in advance, and each exploration slot is marked in the logs. Training and reports can then tell these slots apart from normal ones.

Segregation Amplification

Triangle closing suggests people inside the member's existing clusters, such as their school, company, or city. Each accepted suggestion makes the cluster tighter. Over time, the network can split into closed groups. This is segregation amplification: triangle closing reinforces clusters.

Monitor the cross-cluster rate. It is the share of accepted connections that link two different clusters. If it falls month after month, add more candidates from affiliations, embeddings, or exploration.

Testing a New Ranker

An A/B test here can hurt people outside the treatment group, because a treatment member's invites reach members in the control group too.

So guardrail both sides' rates. On the sending side, watch invites sent and the dismiss rate. On the receiving side, watch the accept, ignore, and report rates, and the number of invites each member receives. Count each invite under the ranker that produced it.

Back the test with a holdback group. A small slice of members stays on the old ranker for weeks. Comparing them with everyone else measures network value, which a one-week test cannot see. Online Experiments and A/B Testing explains how a holdback group measures the long-term effect.

Retraining

The ranker retrains on a regular schedule, such as weekly. Its labels need about 14 days to mature, so daily runs add little. Every candidate model passes an offline check, shadow mode, and then the A/B test above.

Part 7: Count the Costs

Step 7 asks where the money goes. The answer surprises many candidates.

The real cost lives in the nightly graph pipeline, not in serving lookups. Even with caps in place, the job follows about 50,000 paths for each of 300 million members. That is about 15 trillion steps every night, on a large cluster.

Cost lineWhy it costs money
Nightly graph jobFriends-of-friends expansion, ranking, and writing every list
Streaming jobRuns all day to apply dismissals, blocks, and new connections
KV storeAbout 1 TB per run: 300 million lists of 200 entries, at about 16 bytes each
Ranker trainingA weekly job on labeled impressions
Privacy logsEvery exclusion kept for audits
Serving lookupsAbout 1,700 KV reads per second, the smallest line

This is why the 30-day scope matters so much. Most impressions come from the members active recently, who are only a third of all members in this example. Computing lists for members who will not visit spends money on lists nobody reads.

Part 8: What This Capstone Reused

Notice that almost nothing in this design is new. Each part came from an earlier lesson in this module.

Part of this designWhere it came from
The funnel, cold start for new members, and the feedback loopDesign a Recommendation System
A primary metric guarded by negative signals, such as report ratesDesign Feed Ranking
Calibrated probabilities that are multipliedDesign Ad Click Prediction
Fail-closed filtering and honest explorationDesign Fraud Detection

The fraud lesson uses both of these ideas. It decides in advance that a large payment is declined, or sent to an extra check, when scoring fails. It also keeps an exploration slice: a tiny, capped sample of would-be blocks that the risk team approves. Both designs let a small, approved share of decisions go against the model, so the system learns what it would otherwise never see.

The new part is the two-sided goal and the privacy rules around it. That is where an interviewer will spend the most time.

Part 9: What Each Level Sounds Like

Interviewers judge more than whether the boxes are right. Here is how the same design sounds at three levels.

Mid-level. "I'd find friends of friends, rank them by mutual connections, and cache the results. I'd measure acceptance rate."

This answer is correct but thin, because acceptance rate can be gamed, privacy is missing, and nothing handles new members.

Senior. A senior answer covers the full architecture. It names the four sources and the batch plus streaming path. It filters privacy at retrieval and fails closed. It discounts ignored suggestions.

The mark of a senior answer is that P(send) times P(accept) surfaces unprompted, not just in ranking. The two-sided idea also shapes the metrics and the A/B guardrails on both sides.

Staff. A staff answer does all of that, and adds judgment about order and risk.

  • It sequences the roadmap. "If we were starting fresh, contacts and affiliations launch first. They work before anyone has a graph, and they are simple. Triangle closing follows as the graph fills in. Embeddings wait until an offline test shows they find good candidates the other sources miss."
  • It names the network risks. Rich-get-richer and segregation amplification are effects on the whole graph, not on one ranking.
  • It names the cost. "Serving is cheap. Most of the money goes to the nightly graph job, so I'd scope and cap it first."

Part 10: Follow-Ups to Practice

Answer each question out loud before you read the answer below it.

1. "How do you compute friends-of-friends for 900 million members without overloading the cluster?"

Cap per-member expansion, scope by activity, and sample huge accounts. Start from each member's strongest connections only. Compute lists only for members active in the last 30 days. Sample the connections of very large accounts, since links through them say little.

2. "How do you A/B test a new ranker without hurting the network?"

Guardrail both sides' rates, backed by a holdback group. Invites from treatment members reach control members, so watch the receiving side as closely as the sending side. The holdback measures long-term network value.

3. "Legal asks you to prove that a member with suggestions turned off never appears. How?"

Enforce and log the filter at retrieval, not at display time. The nightly job drops the member before any list is stored. Each request checks again before anything is shown. The exclusion log, plus a nightly scan of every list, gives the proof.

4. "A brand-new member signs up. What do they see?"

Contacts and affiliations carry the first session, since there is no graph yet. The streaming path builds a starter list within seconds. Triangle closing takes over as their first invites are accepted.

5. "Adapt this design to suggest contacts for sales reps in a CRM."

A CRM is a tool that tracks a company's customers and sales. An account is a customer company. Here, account overlap outranks triangle closing. A contact who works at an account the rep already sells to is the best candidate. The goal becomes replies, not accepted invites, because a rep wants the contact to answer. The two-sided idea stays: the rep must reach out, and the contact must reply. The privacy rules stay too, since customer data has its own access rules.

FAQs

Q: Is People You May Know just a recommendation system?

Yes, in shape. It uses the same funnel, cold start ideas, and feedback loop. But the two-sided goal and the privacy rules change the metrics, the ranker, and the failure policy.

Q: Should the ranker predict only P(accept)?

No. A candidate who would accept is worthless if the viewer never sends the invite. The product of both probabilities ranks what matters: a connection that actually forms.

Q: Can the privacy filter fail soft, like the batch job?

No. The batch job can serve yesterday's lists because the privacy filter still checks them on each request. When the filter itself fails, nothing checks the list, so the filter must fail closed and hide the module.

Q: Should the nightly job cover all 900 million members?

No. Most impressions come from members active in the last 30 days. Inactive members get a starter list on demand if they return.

Key Takeaways

  • This is a recommendation system in which the items are people. The funnel, cold start, and feedback loop all carry over from earlier lessons.
  • Both members have to want the edge. The ranker scores P(send) times P(accept), and the metrics and A/B guardrails watch both sides.
  • Acceptance rate alone can be gamed. Use accepted invites as the primary metric, ignore and report rates as guardrails, and network value as the north star.
  • Topology is the strongest signal, and contacts are the most precise. Contacts also carry the heaviest privacy obligation. Contacts and affiliations carry new members.
  • Precompute nightly, then stream the freshness. Dismissals must apply within seconds, and the batch job must be idempotent.
  • The batch fails soft, but the privacy filter fails closed. Filter at retrieval, log every exclusion, and hide the module when the filter cannot answer.
  • In a system design interview, name the social-cost asymmetry unprompted. Then say where the cost really sits: in the nightly graph pipeline, not in serving.

This capstone completes the classic ML designs. The next lesson, Introduction: LLM Serving & Inference, turns to large language models, where the model call itself becomes a main cost.

Check Your Understanding

1. Candidate A has P(send) of 0.4 and P(accept) of 0.1. Candidate B has P(send) of 0.2 and P(accept) of 0.5. Which ranks first?

Show answer

Candidate B, with a score of 0.10 against 0.04. The ranker multiplies the two probabilities. The viewer is more likely to invite A, but A rarely accepts, so B is more likely to become a real connection.

2. A member dismisses a suggestion. Why is a nightly update not good enough?

Show answer

Because the dismissed person could reappear on the next page load. Re-showing a dismissed suggestion right after reads as creepy. A streamed write removes it within seconds and records the pair so the nightly job never suggests it again.

3. Tonight's graph job fails, and a minute later the privacy filter times out. What does each failure do to the module?

Show answer

The batch failure changes nothing visible, but the filter timeout hides the module. The batch fails soft, so members see yesterday's lists. The privacy filter fails closed, so the module is suppressed until the filter answers again.

4. A new ranker raises accepted invites in an A/B test, but the report rate on the receiving side rises too. Should it ship?

Show answer

No. The report rate is a guardrail metric, and it must not get worse. More members are being bothered by invites they do not want, which hurts the network even as the primary metric improves.
