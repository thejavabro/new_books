Key Takeaways

Check Your Understanding

Know the Vocabulary

This lesson uses the terms below many times. Read them once now, and come back here when a later section needs one.

  • Feed: The scrolling list of posts a user sees when they open a social app. The system chooses the posts and their order.
  • Funnel: A series of stages that turns a large set of items into a short list. Each stage handles fewer items, so it can spend more work on each one.
  • Candidate: A post that an early stage has picked as worth scoring. Later stages choose among the candidates.
  • Fan-in: Collecting the recent posts of all the accounts a user follows into one list for that user.
  • Fan-out: Delivering a new post to the followers of its author. It can happen when the post is written or when a follower opens the app.
  • Impression: One post shown to one user, one time.
  • Engagement event: An action a user takes on a post, such as a like, a comment, or a hide.
  • Dwell time: How long a user stays on a post before scrolling past it.
  • Negative event: An action that says "I did not want this," such as hiding a post, reporting it, or unfollowing its author.
  • P(event): Short for the predicted probability of an event. P(like) = 0.08 means the model expects an 8 percent chance of a like.
  • Multi-task model: One model that predicts several events at once. It has one shared body and a small output part, called a head, for each event.
  • Value formula: A weighted sum that turns the predictions for one post into a single score. The feed sorts posts by that score.
  • Primary metric: The one number that decides whether a change launches. It is also called the north-star metric.
  • Guardrail metric: A number that must not get worse. A guardrail can stop a launch on its own, even when the primary metric improves.
  • Integrity layer: The step after the value formula that pushes low-quality posts down. It also enforces limits, such as how many top slots one author can fill.
  • Distillation: Training a small model to copy the behavior of a larger one. The small model gives similar answers for much less compute.
  • Micro-batching: Grouping many small scoring requests into one model call, so the hardware does the work more efficiently.

Part 1: Frame the Problem

The question: "Design the home feed for a social app like Instagram or LinkedIn."

You have seen this shape before. Design a Recommendation System built a funnel for a video platform. Retrieval picked a few hundred videos out of 50 million, a ranker scored them, and a rule layer adjusted the final list. A feed uses the same funnel.

So the strong opening move is short: say that the recommendation funnel transfers, then spend your time on what changes. Rebuilding the funnel from scratch can take ten minutes, and the new problems need that time.

What Changes From the Recommendation Funnel

Three things change when the funnel becomes a social feed.

  1. The candidate pool is social. The video design searched a catalog of 50 million items. A feed takes most of its candidates from the accounts the user follows. So the pool is small: a few hundred to a few thousand posts per session, not millions.

  2. "Good" is plural. A good video was mostly one you watched. A good post is one you like, comment on, reshare, stay on, or use to visit the author's profile. It is also one you do not hide or report. The ranker must predict all of these and balance them.

  3. Integrity sits inside ranking. A feed that ranks by engagement alone fills up with bait, meaning posts built to provoke reactions. So quality checks are part of the ranking path, not a separate system added later.

Say this framing out loud in the first minutes. For example: "The recommendation funnel transfers. Three things change: the candidates are social, good is plural, and integrity sits inside ranking. I'll spend my time on those."

Scope and Scale

Next, state the scope. This design covers the main ranked feed, while ads, stories, and direct messages are out of scope.

Removing posts that break the rules is also out of scope. A separate system does that, as Design Harmful Content Detection shows. The feed still decides how far allowed posts spread.

Then state the scale. These numbers are assumptions for this example:

  • 500 million daily users.
  • About 5 sessions per user per day.
  • About 3 ranking calls per session: one when the app opens, and more as the user scrolls.
  • Each ranking call finishes in under 200 milliseconds (ms), as an example target rather than a guarantee.

Part 7 turns these numbers into a workload.

Part 2: Define Success

The Primary Metric

A feed needs one primary metric, also called the north-star metric, that decides whether a change launches. A common choice is a session-level measure, such as daily sessions per user. It shows whether people keep coming back, which a count of likes cannot show.

The Guardrails

A feed also needs guardrail metrics. Two matter most here: the hide rate and the report rate. Each one is the share of impressions that end in a hide or a report.

Set an acceptable limit for each guardrail before the experiment starts. Suppose a new model raises daily sessions but pushes the hide rate past its limit. It does not launch, because either guardrail can veto a win on its own.

Online Experiments and A/B Testing describes this order in general: the primary metric decides, and the guardrails can veto.

Notice what this rules out: a feed is never judged by one engagement number. With one number, a model can win by showing posts that people react to but do not value.

Measuring More Than Engagement

Engagement shows what people did, but not whether they were glad they did it. Someone may comment on a post only because it made them angry.

So feeds also ask people directly, through continuous surveys. Every day, the app asks a small random sample of users about a post they just saw: "Was this post worth your time?"

The answers have two uses. First, they become labels for a head that predicts P(worthwhile), the chance a user would call the post worth their time. Second, they give the team a quality measure that raw engagement cannot provide.

Offline Checks

Before any online test, check each candidate model on held-out data, and grade each head on its own. One common grade is AUC. It measures how often the model scores a post where the event happened above a post where it did not.

Also check that each head's probabilities are reliable. The value formula in Part 4 adds them together, so a wrong scale in one head distorts every score. Offline results decide which models get an online test. The primary metric and the guardrails decide which model ships.

Part 3: Map the Data

Where Candidates Come From

The feed's retrieval stage is built mostly from the user's network. It has three sources.

  1. Fan-in from your network. The system collects recent posts from the accounts you follow. This source usually supplies most of the candidates.

  2. Spillover. These are posts that your network engaged with, from accounts you do not follow. If several of your friends comment on a stranger's post, that post can spill over into your candidates.

  3. Suggestions. These are posts from accounts you do not follow, chosen because they match your interests. They are usually found with embeddings, as in Embeddings and Two-Tower Retrieval. An embedding is a list of numbers that captures what a post or a user is about.

Here is an illustrative example. A user named Maya follows 400 accounts. Since her last visit, they have shared about 1,500 posts she has not seen. Spillover adds about 300 more, and suggestions add about 200. Her pool is about 2,000 candidates.

Part 5 shows how the system finds those 1,500 network posts quickly.

The Events

Every impression is logged with its screen position, its features, its predictions, and the model version. The events that follow are joined to that record, and they fall into two groups.

Positive eventsNegative events
LikeHide
CommentReport
ReshareUnfollow the author
Dwell time"See less like this"
Profile visit

Dwell time becomes a yes-or-no label through a cutoff. For example, staying on a post for more than 10 seconds can count as a positive event. The right cutoff is a design choice to test.

Most of these labels arrive fast. A like usually comes within seconds or minutes of the impression, so a day's data is nearly complete when the daily retrain runs. Survey answers are different: they are sparse, because only a small sample of users is asked.

Position matters too. Posts near the top get more engagement partly because they are near the top. Log the position, and correct for it in training, as Design a Recommendation System explains.

Why Negative Events Are Precious

Negative events are rare: most impressions end in a scroll, and only a small share end in a hide or a report.

Negative events are also deliberate. To hide a post, a user must open a menu and choose an option. That action has one clear meaning: "I did not want this."

Compare that with a missing like. People often skip the like button on posts they enjoyed. So a missing positive event is a weak signal, while a negative event is a strong one.

Because negative events are rare and unambiguous, they are precious. Give them extra weight in training, so the shared body does not learn to ignore them. A simple way is to count errors on the hide and report heads more heavily. If you instead add extra copies of the impressions that ended in a hide or a report, the predicted P(hide) comes out too high. Then rescale the probabilities before the value formula uses them, because the formula needs their true scale.

Include them in evaluation as well, or a model that predicts likes well but misses hides can look good offline.

Part 4: Pick the Model

Start With a Baseline

The simplest feed is chronological, with the newest posts first. A slightly better baseline multiplies a recency score, which is higher for newer posts, by affinity. Affinity means how often the user interacts with the author. Both baselines are cheap and easy to explain, and they give every later model a number to beat.

The Multi-Task Ranker

The common model shape for feed ranking is a multi-task model. It has one shared body and a separate small head for each event type.

The body reads features of the user, the post, the author, and the context, such as the hour. It learns one shared summary of them. Each head turns that summary into one probability, such as P(like) or P(hide).

Why not train one separate model per event? There are three reasons.

  • Rare events learn from common ones. Reports are rare, so a report model alone would have few examples. A shared body lets the report head use patterns learned from the common events.
  • One call gives every prediction. The model runs once per post and returns all the probabilities together. Separate models would multiply the serving cost.
  • New heads are cheap. Adding the P(worthwhile) head does not need a new model.

The Value Formula

The heads give about eight probabilities for each post. Sorting needs one number, and the value formula provides it. Here is a simplified version with four positive events and two negative ones:

value = w1 x P(like) + w2 x P(comment) + w3 x P(reshare) + w4 x P(dwell)
        - w5 x P(hide) - w6 x P(report)

Each w is a weight. A full formula has a term for every head, including profile visits and P(worthwhile).

The negative terms subtract predicted harm. When a post has a high P(hide), the term -w5 x P(hide) lowers its score. A post can be likely to draw comments and still rank low, because people are also likely to hide it.

Who Sets the Weights

The model does not learn the weights. Product leadership sets them, because they state what the product values. Is a comment worth four likes? How much does one hide cost? Those are product decisions, not facts hidden in the data. In short, the model predicts each event, and product leadership sets the weights.

The weights are tuned with A/B tests. Offline analysis can propose a few candidate sets of weights. Each set then runs as an experiment, judged by the primary metric and the guardrails, and leadership approves the winner.

There is a deeper reason to keep the weights outside the model: policy can change without retraining. Suppose leadership decides that comments are overvalued. The team lowers w2 and tests the change, while the model and its training data stay the same.

This works only while each head's probabilities stay reliable. So monitor each head's calibration, meaning whether its predicted probabilities match how often the events really happen.

Part 5: Design the Serving Path

The diagram below shows one ranking call, from the app opening to the posts on screen. It also shows the work done earlier, when each post was written.

Follow one ranking call through it, with illustrative times.

Step 1: The app opens. At 8:00 AM, Maya opens the app. It sends a ranking call to the feed service, which has 200 ms to answer.

Step 2: Candidates are gathered. The service reads her network posts, adds spillover and suggestions, and removes posts she has already seen. About 2,000 candidates remain, and this step takes about 30 ms.

Step 3: The light ranker cuts the list. A small distilled model scores all 2,000 posts with a few cheap features. It keeps the top 500, in about 20 ms.

Step 4: The heavy ranker scores 500 posts. The multi-task model fetches richer features and returns every head's probability for each post. This takes about 60 ms, including the feature reads.

Step 5: The value formula sorts. The service applies the formula with the current weights, which takes a few milliseconds.

Step 6: The integrity layer adjusts the order. It demotes bait, caps authors, and applies Maya's past "see less" choices. The top posts go to the app. When she scrolls near the end of them, the app makes the next ranking call, which can also use what she just did.

Step 7: The impressions are logged. Each shown post is logged with its features, its predictions, the model version, and the version of the weights.

The steps add up to about 120 ms. The rest of the 200 ms covers the network and spikes in load.

Push, Pull, and the Hybrid

How does Step 2 find Maya's 1,500 network posts so quickly? There are two basic ways to do fan-out.

Push, also called fan-out on write, does the work ahead of time. When an author shares a post, the system copies the post's ID into the inbox of every follower. Opening the app is then a fast read of one inbox.

Pull, also called fan-out on read, does the work at open time. The post is stored once. When a user opens the app, the system reads the recent posts of every account that user follows.

ApproachWhen the work happensStrengthWeakness
PushWhen a post is writtenOpening the app is fastOne post from a huge account needs millions of writes
PullWhen the app opensWriting a post is cheapSlow for users who follow thousands of accounts
HybridBothAvoids both weaknessesTwo paths to build and merge

Push breaks for very large accounts, because an account with 100 million followers would trigger 100 million inbox writes for every post. Pull breaks for heavy readers, who follow thousands of accounts.

The standard fix is a hybrid. Push the posts of ordinary accounts into inboxes. Pull the posts of the few very large accounts at open time, and merge the two lists. The follower count that moves an account from push to pull is a tuning choice.

The diagram below compares the three approaches.

The Integrity Layer

The integrity layer runs right after the value formula and changes the order that the formula produced. It has four main jobs.

  • Demote bait. Engagement bait begs for reactions, for example by telling people to comment "yes" if they agree. Clickbait headlines and borderline content are pushed down too. Borderline content means posts that are allowed but close to breaking the rules.
  • Cap authors. No single author fills the top of the feed. For example, one author may get at most 2 of the top 10 slots.
  • Keep variety. Mix topics and formats, so the feed is not all one kind of post.
  • Make "see less" last. When a user asks to see less of something, the suppression should hold for weeks, not fade after one session.

Why is this layer needed at all? Optimizing for engagement alone has a well-known result: outrage, clickbait, and borderline content rise to the top. People react strongly to these posts, so they score well on comments and dwell time. The negative terms in the formula help, but they are not enough, so the integrity layer adds rules that the product owns directly.

Integrity Scores Are Computed Once per Post

The integrity layer needs scores, such as the chance that a post is bait. Those scores come from classifiers, which are models that sort an input into categories.

Run these classifiers once per post, when the post is written, and store the scores with the post. Do not run them for every impression.

The reason is cost, because one popular post can be shown millions of times. If the system writes 100 million posts a day, that is only about 1,200 posts per second to classify. The heavy ranker, in contrast, scores tens of millions of posts per second, as Part 7 shows. One stored score reused across all impressions avoids a huge repeated cost.

Recompute a post's scores when the post is edited, when reports about it arrive, or when the policy changes. Checks that depend on the viewer still run during the ranking call, such as blocked accounts and privacy settings.

Part 6: Keep It Working

Three Loop Speeds

Three loops run at the same time, each at its own speed.

LoopSpeedWhat it updatesExample
In-session stateSecondsWhat the user just didMaya hides a post about a TV show, and her next page shows fewer like it.
Per-post featuresMinutesCounts and rates for each postA streaming job updates a post's likes and hides every few minutes.
Model retrainDailyThe multi-task modelYesterday's impressions and events train a new candidate model.

The per-post features come from a streaming job, as Features and Feature Stores describes. The daily model follows the safe rollout steps in Model Serving Basics. Offline checks come first, then shadow mode, then a canary and an A/B test judged on the primary metric and the guardrails.

Ranking a Post That Is 10 Seconds Old

A new post has no engagement counts yet, and the per-post loop needs minutes to fill them in. So the ranker relies on what it already knows.

  • Author features: the author's past engagement rates, and how often this viewer interacts with the author.
  • Content features: what the text and image are about, taken from embeddings.
  • A fresh-content boost: a small bonus for new posts, so they get enough impressions to collect real counts.

Within minutes, real counts arrive and take over from the guesses.

The Feedback Loop Risk

The feed trains on engagement that the feed itself caused. Posts ranked high get more impressions and more engagement, so they carry more weight in the next training run.

Suppose the feed favors bait slightly. The next model learns from that engagement and favors bait a little more. Over time, an engagement-optimized feed drifts toward bait unless something checks it. Nothing crashes, and no error appears.

Monitoring, Drift, and Retraining covers feedback loops in general. For a feed, four checks work together:

  • the hide and report guardrails,
  • the P(worthwhile) surveys,
  • the integrity layer,
  • a holdback group, meaning a small set of users kept on a simpler ranking for long-term comparison.

The loop has a second effect. Posts the feed never shows collect no engagement, so the next model learns little about them. The fresh-content boost helps here. So does a small exploration share of impressions, which that lesson explains.

Tag Degraded Sessions

Sometimes the heavy ranker fails, and users get one of the fallback orders listed in Part 7. Mark these sessions in the logs.

Without the tag, the next training run learns from impressions that the main model never chose. The metrics also mix two different systems and move for the wrong reason.

Part 7: Count the Costs

The Workload

Turn the scale from Part 1 into ranking calls.

Step 1: Sessions. 500 million users times 5 sessions is 2.5 billion sessions per day.

Step 2: Ranking calls. 2.5 billion sessions times 3 calls is 7.5 billion ranking calls per day.

Step 3: Average rate. A day has 86,400 seconds, so the average is about 86,800 ranking calls per second.

Step 4: Peak rate. Assume the peak is twice the average, which gives about 174,000 calls per second. In an interview, say "on the order of 100,000 ranking calls per second."

Step 5: Model scores. The heavy ranker scores 500 posts per call, which is about 43.4 million heavy scores per second on average. The light ranker scores 2,000 posts per call, or about 174 million light scores per second.

These numbers make one point clear: every millisecond and every feature read is multiplied many millions of times.

Three Tools That Make It Affordable

All three tools come from Model Serving Basics.

  1. A distilled first-pass model. The light ranker is a small model trained to copy the heavy one. It handles most of the volume, so the heavy model sees only a quarter of the candidates.

  2. Micro-batching. The heavy ranker groups the scoring work of many requests into one model call, which uses the hardware better. The short wait to fill a batch adds latency, so test it against the 200 ms budget.

  3. Feature caching. Keep the features of popular posts in memory, close to the ranker. Each cached value expires after a short time, such as a few seconds, so the counts stay fresh. One viral post can appear in millions of feeds, so reusing its cached features saves millions of reads.

The write-time integrity scores from Part 5 save cost in the same way.

The Failure Ledger

Decide in advance what the user sees when each part fails.

What failsWhat the user sees
The heavy ranker times outThe light ranker's order
Ranking is down entirelyNetwork posts in recency order
The suggestions source is downA feed from the network and spillover only

In every row, the user still sees a feed, and viewer checks such as blocked accounts still apply. Every degraded session is tagged in the logs, as Part 6 explains.

In the Interview

The 30-second answer: "The recommendation funnel transfers, so I'll focus on what changes. Candidates come from fan-in from the user's network, plus spillover and suggestions: a few thousand posts, not millions. A distilled light ranker keeps 500. A multi-task model predicts likes, comments, reshares, dwell, profile visits, hides, and reports. A value formula combines them, and its negative terms subtract predicted harm. Product leadership owns the weights and tunes them with A/B tests. An integrity layer then demotes bait and caps authors. Daily sessions decide launches, and hide and report rates can veto them."

Likely follow-ups:

  1. "Why not just show posts in time order?" Defend ranking, and admit its costs. The case for ranking is that it raises satisfaction, because people see the posts they would otherwise miss. Support that claim with experiments and surveys. But a ranked feed is harder to understand, and it can bury posts that people wanted. Offer a chronological tab for users who prefer it.
  2. "How do you rank a post created 10 seconds ago?" It has no engagement counts yet. So the ranker relies on author and content features, plus a small fresh-content boost. Real counts replace the guesses within minutes.
  3. "Can we track one metric for the whole feed?" No. One engagement number is how feeds drift toward bait. Use a primary metric, such as daily sessions, with hide and report rates as guardrails that can veto a win.

Same Design, Other Questions

Notifications ranking. The decision is no longer an order: for each notification, it becomes a choice to send or stay silent. Each extra notification makes the next one less welcome, so fatigue dominates the design. The formula gains a strong P(annoy) term, the chance that a notification annoys the user enough to turn notifications off.

A "Following" tab next to "For You." Retrieval collapses to fan-in, with no spillover or suggestions. Ranking collapses to near-recency, because users of this tab expect time order. Both tabs can share the same logs and the same viewer checks.

Common Mistakes

Mistake 1: Rebuilding the Funnel From Scratch

A candidate who spends ten minutes redrawing retrieval and ranking leaves little time for the feed's real problems. State that the funnel transfers, then spend the time on what changes.

Mistake 2: Ranking on One Score

A ranker that predicts only clicks cannot tell a post people value from one that makes them angry. Predict each event with its own head, and combine the heads with a value formula.

Mistake 3: Hiding the Weights Inside the Model

If the model learns the trade-offs itself, every policy change needs a retrain. Keep the weights outside the model, owned by product and tuned with A/B tests.

Mistake 4: Treating Integrity as Someone Else's Problem

Removing posts that break the rules is a separate system. Demoting bait, capping authors, and honoring "see less" belong to the feed. Put the integrity layer right after the value formula.

Mistake 5: Forgetting the Negative Events

A model trained only on likes and comments never learns what people hide. Weight negative events heavily in training, include them in evaluation, and give them guardrails.

FAQs

Q: Is feed ranking just a recommendation system with a different catalog?

No. The funnel transfers, but three things change: the candidates are social, good is plural, and integrity sits inside ranking. Most of the interview should go to those three.

Q: Should the model learn the value-formula weights by itself?

No. The weights state what the product values, so product leadership sets them. Keeping them outside the model lets the team test new weights with an A/B test and no retrain.

Q: Do I need to explain push and pull in every feed answer?

No. One sentence about the hybrid is usually enough. Go deeper if the interviewer asks about very large accounts or about how fan-in stays fast.

Q: Can the integrity classifiers run during the ranking call instead?

No, not the heavy ones. A post can be shown millions of times, so classifying it on every impression repeats a large cost. Classify once per post at write time, and keep only the viewer checks in the ranking call.

Key Takeaways

  • The recommendation funnel transfers to a feed. Say so early, and spend the interview on what changes: the candidates are social, good is plural, and integrity sits inside ranking.
  • Candidates come mostly from the network. Fan-in supplies most of them, and spillover and suggestions add the rest. The pool is a few hundred to a few thousand posts, not millions.
  • One multi-task model predicts every event. A value formula combines the predictions, and its negative terms subtract predicted harm. Product leadership owns the weights and tunes them with A/B tests, with no retrain needed.
  • Negative events are rare and unambiguous. Weight them heavily in training and evaluation, and make hide and report rates the guardrails.
  • The scale forces cheap tools. At about 86,800 ranking calls per second, use a distilled light ranker, micro-batching, and feature caching. Compute integrity scores once per post.
  • In a system design interview, draw the funnel quickly, then show the heads, the value formula, and the integrity layer. Name the three loop speeds, the feedback-loop risk, and what the user sees when ranking fails.

The next lesson, Design Search Ranking, adds a typed query to the funnel. It also shows why clicks near the top of a results page are biased.

Check Your Understanding

1. A post is likely to get many comments, yet it ranks low. How can the value formula cause this?

Show answer

Its negative terms subtract predicted harm. If the post also has a high P(hide) or P(report), terms such as -w5 x P(hide) lower its score. Strong positive predictions cannot save a post that many people are likely to hide.

2. A new model raises daily sessions by 1 percent, but the report rate rises past its agreed limit. Does it launch?

Show answer

No. Daily sessions is the primary metric, but the report rate is a guardrail. A guardrail can veto a win on its own when it passes its limit.

3. Why does a feed use a hybrid of push and pull fan-out?

Show answer

Because each approach fails for a different kind of account. Push makes opening the app fast, but a post from a 100-million-follower account would need 100 million inbox writes. Pull avoids those writes but is slow for users who follow many accounts. The hybrid pushes ordinary accounts and pulls the few very large ones.

4. The heavy ranker times out during a traffic peak. What does the user see, and what should the logs record?

Show answer

The light ranker's order, and a tag that marks the session as degraded. The user still gets a ranked feed. The tag keeps the next training run and the metrics from mixing fallback impressions with the main model's choices.
