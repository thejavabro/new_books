Know the Vocabulary

This lesson uses the terms below many times. Read them once now, and come back here when a later section needs one.

  • Seven-step framework: The course's order for answering a design question. The steps are frame the problem, define success, map the data, pick the model, design the serving path, keep it working, and count the costs.
  • Question family: One of three groups of AI design questions, sorted by what the model outputs. Predictive systems output a score, a number, or a label. Generative systems output content, and agentic systems output actions.
  • Funnel: The usual shape of a predictive design that ranks many items. It starts with many candidates and keeps fewer at each stage, with a more careful check each time.
  • Pipeline: The usual shape of a generative design. It is a fixed series of stages that turns a request into content, such as retrieve, assemble the context, generate, and check.
  • Loop: The usual shape of an agentic design. A model picks an action, a tool runs it, and the model reads the result before it picks the next action.
  • Offline plane: The part of a system that works in the background on past data, such as training jobs.
  • Online plane: The part of a system that answers live requests while a user waits.
  • Feedback loop: The path that brings real outcomes back into the system, so the team can measure quality and improve the next version.
  • Label: The answer a model should learn for one past example, such as the delivery time that really happened.
  • Golden set: A fixed set of test inputs with known good answers. Every change runs against it before it ships.
  • Fallback: What the system does, as decided in advance, when a part fails or is too slow.
  • Fail closed: To block the action, or show only safe content, when a check cannot run. To fail open is the opposite: the action goes through without the check.
  • Fail soft: To keep working in a reduced way when a part breaks, such as serving yesterday's results.
  • Recognition move: Saying, in the first minutes of an interview, which known system the question is, and what is different this time.
  • Exit criterion: The short sentence you say out loud to close one step before you start the next.
  • Strong-hire bar: The top level on a rubric, which is a list of the qualities an interviewer grades. It is the level that makes an interviewer argue to hire you.
  • Variation: A case study with a different product or constraint, used to practice the same structure again.

Part 1: Where You Started, and Where You Are Now

The course opened with one problem. An interviewer gives you a vague question about an AI feature and about 45 minutes. You must turn the question into a design and explain each choice out loud. You must also reach the end before time runs out.

At the start, that problem had no structure. Now it has three tools.

  1. The family test gives the shape. Ask what the model outputs. A score points to a funnel, or to a score that turns into an action. Content points to a pipeline, and actions point to a loop.
  2. The seven steps give the order. You frame the problem, define success, and map the data. Then you pick the model, design the serving path, keep it working, and count the costs.
  3. The 45-minute clock gives the time. Each step has a share of the interview, and the serving path gets the largest one.

The course also followed one company the whole way. A food-delivery company appeared in Introduction: The AI Interview Landscape. Module 2 built its delivery-time prediction. Modules 4 to 7 grew its support assistant. It started as a system that answers questions. It became an agent that can apply credits and issue small refunds, within limits set in code.

This lesson has four more parts. Part 2 lists what you can now do, module by module. Part 3 names the ideas that run through every module. Part 4 gives a routine for practice, and Part 5 says what to study next.

Part 2: What You Can Now Do, Module by Module

Each module below lists the skills it gave you, with a link to the lesson that teaches each one. When a skill feels weak, follow its link.

Module 1: The AI Interview Landscape

You can now recognize the question in front of you before you design anything.

  • Name the form of the question. How AI System Design Shows Up in Interviews named five forms of AI design question. You may design a known machine learning (ML) product or a generative AI (GenAI) product. You may add AI to an existing system or design an agent. Or you may critique or scale an existing AI design.
  • Explain why a learning system needs new parts. An AI system is still a distributed system, but four classic assumptions break. What Changes When the System Learns adds one new piece for each. Quality needs measurement, and drift needs a feedback loop. Drift is a loss of accuracy as the world changes, often slow but sometimes sudden. Cost needs controls, and silent failures need a safety and control layer.
  • Sort the question into a family. The Three Question Families gives the family test: ask what the model outputs. A score or a label points to a predictive design, content to a generative design, and actions to an agentic design.
  • Answer in seven steps. A Framework for AI Design Answers gives the order of the steps and each step's share of 45 minutes.
  • Scope a vague question. Capstone: Scoping an AI Design Question listed several AI features for the food-delivery company. It chose the support assistant over delivery-time prediction and said why.

That capstone also set the example numbers that later modules reused. The company expected about 40,000 support conversations a day. A ticket handled by a person cost about 10 went to a person.

Module 2: ML Systems Foundations

You can now draw the system around a model, not only the model. Introduction: ML Systems Foundations split it into three parts. The offline plane builds the model, the online plane serves it, and the feedback loop keeps it accurate.

  • Find the labels. Name where they come from, how late they arrive, and how they can be wrong. Where Training Data Comes From teaches this.
  • Keep features consistent. A feature is one input value the model uses. If training and serving compute a feature differently, quality drops with no error. This is training-serving skew, and Features and Feature Stores shows how to prevent it.
  • Choose batch or online inference. Batch inference computes predictions ahead of time, and online inference runs the model when the request arrives. If a prediction depends on information that exists only at request time, it must run online. Batch vs Online Inference gives this test.
  • Ship a new model in stages. It passes offline evaluation, then shadow mode, then a canary release, then a gradual ramp. In shadow mode, it scores live requests, but nobody sees its results. A canary release sends it a small share of traffic first. Model Serving Basics teaches the full ladder.
  • Pick the metric, then prove the win online. Offline Evaluation: Choosing the Metric picks the number that fits the problem. Online Experiments and A/B Testing tests the new model against the current one on real users.
  • Notice decay early. Monitoring, Drift, and Retraining watches four layers, from system health to outcome quality. It retrains on a schedule plus a trigger.

Capstone: An ML Platform for ETA Prediction put these parts into one design. It measured the model with its average error and its bad tail. It measured the promise to the customer with the on-time rate.

Module 3: Classic ML Design Questions

You can now answer the well-known predictive questions. Introduction: Classic ML Design Questions showed two shapes behind them. Ranking questions choose a few items out of many with a funnel: retrieve, rank, then apply the rules. Judging questions score one item and turn the score into an action.

  • Recommend. Design a Recommendation System builds the full funnel. Retrieval is judged on recall, the share of good items it keeps. Ranking is judged on precision at the top.
  • Rank a feed. Design Feed Ranking predicts several user reactions at once and combines them with a value formula.
  • Rank search results. Design Search Ranking runs keyword search and meaning-based search together. It also corrects for the bias that people click the top result because it is first.
  • Predict ad clicks. Design Ad Click Prediction shows why the click probability must be calibrated, not just well ordered. The auction multiplies it by the bid, so it decides which ad wins and what the winner pays.
  • Stop fraud. Design Fraud Detection faces an adversary, a person who changes behavior to get past the system. Its labels are slow, because chargebacks can take 2 to 8 weeks to arrive.
  • Find harmful content. Design Harmful Content Detection measures harm by views, not uploads. It spends compute in proportion to risk.

Capstone: People You May Know combined these designs. It is a recommendation system in which the items are people, and both members must want the connection.

Module 4: LLM Serving & Inference

You can now treat a large language model (LLM) as one component with a known latency shape and a price. LLMs at Interview Altitude gives that view.

  • Explain the two phases of a call. Prefill reads the whole prompt and sets most of the time to the first token. A token is a small piece of text, often part of a word. Decode then writes the answer one token at a time and sets the time to finish.
  • Price a conversation. Inference Economics counts input and output tokens and multiplies by volume. Back of the Envelope Math for AI Systems turns that into a few anchors you can use out loud.
  • Cut the bill in order. Serving Optimizations first avoids calling the model, then tunes the serving engine, then changes the model.
  • Choose how to adapt a model. Prompting, Fine-Tuning, or RAG starts with a prompt as the baseline. Missing facts point to retrieval, and wrong behavior points to the prompt, then to fine-tuning.
  • Test every change. Evaluating LLM Systems uses cheap programmatic checks, an LLM judge checked against people, and human review. A golden set runs on every change.

Capstone: The Inference Bill priced a full inference layer step by step. In its example, routing and caching brought a bill of about 9,000.

Module 5: RAG & Retrieval

You can now design retrieval-augmented generation (RAG). The system first finds the evidence for a question, then the model answers from it. Introduction: RAG & Retrieval split it into an ingestion path, a request path, and a feedback loop.

  • Prepare the documents. Embeddings and Vector Databases turns text into embeddings, lists of numbers that capture meaning. Chunking and Indexing cuts documents into chunks and keeps the index in sync with edits and deletes.
  • Answer in five stages. The RAG Pipeline runs query processing, retrieval, context assembly, generation, and post-checks.
  • Find both exact words and meaning. Hybrid Search and Reranking runs keyword search and semantic search together. It merges the two lists, then reranks a short list.
  • Keep answers grounded. Keeping Answers Grounded stacks four layers: instructions, citations, entailment checks, and a designed refusal. An entailment check confirms that the cited text really supports the claim.

Capstone: Enterprise Document Q&A made permissions the main constraint. The permission filter runs inside the index query. When the service cannot check a user's groups, it fails closed to public content only.

Module 6: GenAI Product Designs

You can now design a full product around a model that is usually bought, not built. Introduction: GenAI Product Designs gave the request path six stages. They are the gateway, the input gate, context assembly, the router and model, the output gate, and delivery.

  • Find the product's extreme. Each product pushes one limit, and that limit picks the shape.
    • State and scale shape Design a ChatGPT-Style Assistant.
    • Latency shapes Design a Code Assistant.
    • An existing search system that must not get worse shapes Design AI-Powered Search.
    • Compute per request, mostly GPU time, shapes Design a Text-to-Image Service.
  • Defend against prompt injection. Prompt injection is text that tries to give the model new instructions. Safety and Guardrails places gates outside the model, because the model cannot guard itself.

Capstone: AI Features for a SaaS Product designed three features at once. They shared one platform, and they shipped in order of risk.

Module 7: Agentic Systems

You can now decide when a task needs an agent, and how to keep it under control. Introduction: Agentic Systems gave the main rule: the model proposes, and the runtime decides.

  • Tell an agent from a workflow. What an Agent Actually Is asks who chooses the next step. Code that fixed the steps in advance makes a workflow. A model that chooses them while the task runs makes an agent.
  • Keep authority in code. Tool Use and Function Calling checks each proposed tool call before it runs. It sorts tools into tiers by how much harm a wrong call can cause.
  • Add agents only for a reason. Orchestration and Multi-Agent Systems starts from one agent. A second agent must earn its extra cost.
  • Survive a crash. Agent Memory and Context keeps task state in storage outside the model, so a task can resume.
  • Earn trust with evidence. Evaluating Agent Trajectories tests the whole path of a task in a sandbox, where actions change nothing real. Evaluating and Controlling Agents grants more freedom one tier of actions at a time.

Capstone: A Customer-Support Agent gave the support assistant real tools. Only problems with an active order entered the loop. Each case had example budgets of 12 steps, 90 seconds, and 30 cap in its own code, and people reviewed a sample of refunds afterward. A refund above $30 always waited for a person.

Module 8: System Design Interview Case Studies

You can now run a full question on the clock. Introduction: System Design Interview Case Studies and How to Run a Case Study teach the method.

  • Recognize, then adapt. Name the family, the known system, and what changes, in the first few minutes.
  • Keep to the clock. Give each step its minutes, and close it with an exit criterion.
  • Handle interruptions. Each kind of interviewer question, such as a redirect or a depth probe, has a move.
  • Grade yourself. Score each run on five dimensions, from a recording.
  • Critique a design. Critiquing a System Design ranks findings by how much harm they can do. Each finding has a problem, its consequence for users, and a fix.

Seven timed case studies gave you practice across the three families. They are music discovery, harmful content at platform scale, and visual search. Then come a legal research assistant, a meeting assistant, and text-to-image at scale. The last is an autonomous coding agent.

Module 9: Rapid Review

You now have three short tools for the weeks before an interview. Introduction: Rapid Review explains how to use them on a schedule.

  • Rapid-Fire Questions drills short answers, grouped by module.
  • Red Flags That Sound Junior lists statements that make a candidate sound junior, with a stronger sentence to say instead.
  • Decision Cheat Sheets puts the course's main decisions into compact tables.

Part 3: Seven Ideas That Run Through the Course

The lessons covered very different products. Yet the same few ideas came back again and again. When a question is new to you, these ideas still apply.

1. The design is the system around the model. In Module 2, the model used a few milliseconds of a 100 ms request. In Module 4, the LLM call became a main cost, but the design work still sat around it. That work was context, routing, checks, and the bill. In Module 6, the model was usually bought, so the product was everything else.

2. Cheap work comes first, and expensive work comes last. The funnel cuts millions of items to a few hundred before a richer model runs. Harmful content detection runs its heavy models only on the posts a fast model flags. Serving Optimizations tries to avoid a model call before it speeds one up.

3. A stage that misses something cannot be rescued later. An item that retrieval drops never reaches the ranker. A passage that RAG never retrieves cannot ground the answer. So retrieval is measured on recall, and it is often the first thing to check.

4. Rules that matter live in code, not in the prompt. Permissions run inside the index query. Guardrails sit outside the model. A refund limit lives in the tool's code, where a fooled model cannot change it.

5. Every change passes gates before it reaches everyone. A new predictive model moves from offline evaluation to shadow mode to a canary release. An LLM change runs against the golden set first. An agent earns freedom one tier of actions at a time, with measured results.

6. Failure behavior is decided in advance. Every dependency gets a time limit and a fallback. A quality feature often fails soft, as a homepage falls back to a simpler order. A permission check always fails closed, and a safety check often does, so nothing unchecked is shown. Whether a check fails open or closed is a product and policy decision, as Model Serving Basics explains.

7. Price one unit, then multiply. One prediction, one conversation, one image, or one agent task has a cost. Multiply it by the volume, and the result points to a design decision. In the fake-review run of the case studies introduction, the largest cost was human review, not the model.

Notice that none of these ideas belongs to one family. A strong answer to a predictive, generative, or agentic question uses all seven.

Part 4: How to Keep Practicing

Knowing the course is not the same as answering under a timer. The routine below turns what you know into what you can say. It is one practical routine, so adjust the order to your own schedule.

Step 1: Run a variation on a fresh clock. Each timed case study ends with variations. Pick one you have not run. Set a 45-minute timer, speak out loud, and draw as you would on a whiteboard.

Step 2: Record the run, and grade it from the recording. Use the five dimensions from How to Run a Case Study. They are problem navigation, data and metrics fluency, architecture and trade-offs, production maturity, and communication and clock. Never grade from memory, because memory quietly upgrades your own performance.

Step 3: Fix the weakest dimension at its source. Find your lowest score, and go back to the lessons that teach that skill. The table after these steps maps each dimension to lessons.

Step 4: Make new questions from products you use. Pick an app you use and one AI feature it could add. Say the recognition move in one minute: the family, the known system, and what changes. This builds the habit that saves the most interview time.

Step 5: Critique your own old answers. Take a recording from a week ago, and critique it the way Critiquing a System Design teaches. Start with any broken rule that must always hold, such as a permission leak. Then check for a missing feedback loop, gaps in reliability, the costs, and quality.

Step 6: Practice with a partner who interrupts. Ask the partner to redirect you, to test what happens when a part fails, and to ask for depth. A partner finds gaps that a solo run hides.

Step 7: Follow the review schedule. Introduction: Rapid Review starts about two weeks before the interview. It uses the three Module 9 tools and a miss list, a written list of the questions you got wrong.

Part 5: What to Study Next

Keep the Numbers Honest

The numbers in this course are illustrative assumptions. Token prices, model names, and hardware change often. So do not memorize a price. Learn the method instead: state each assumption out loud, price one unit, and multiply by the volume. Before an interview, check whether the main prices have moved.

Build One Small System

Reading about a system is not the same as running one. A small project makes the parts concrete. For example, you could build a RAG system over your own notes. Write a golden set of a few dozen questions, then measure retrieval recall and faithfulness separately. Faithfulness asks whether every claim in the answer is supported by the retrieved text. You will meet chunking, embeddings, and grounding failures on a scale you can debug.

An AI design round often sits inside a longer interview loop. Many loops also include a general system design round, a coding round, and a behavioral round. FAQs

Q: Do I need to run every case study before my interview?

No. A few case studies run out loud, recorded, and graded teach more than all of them read in silence. Then use variations to practice the same moves on new questions.

Q: My interview is only about LLMs. Can I skip the predictive modules when I review?

No. The LLM and agent modules reuse Module 2's ideas: serving, evaluation, monitoring, and safe rollout. Running an LLM is still a serving problem, with a latency budget, a fallback, and a safe rollout.

Q: What if my question matches nothing in the course?

Use the family test alone. The family gives you the shape, and the seven steps give you the order. That is enough to build an honest answer in front of the interviewer.

Q: Should I memorize the numbers in this course?

Only a few. Back of the Envelope Math for AI Systems gives five anchors worth remembering, such as about 4 characters of English text per token. But every price in the course is an illustrative assumption. The method of estimation matters more than any single number.

Q: Is the 45-minute clock a strict rule?

No. It is a plan, and real interviews vary in length. Scale each step's share to the time you have. Keep the serving path the largest share, and keep a few minutes free for follow-up questions.

Key Takeaways

  • You now have three tools for any AI design question. The family test gives the shape, the seven steps give the order, and the clock gives the time.
  • Modules 2 to 7 taught the parts, and each module reused the ones before it. Predictive designs, LLM serving, retrieval, generative products, and agents share many of their parts.
  • Seven ideas repeat across the course. The design is the system around the model, and cheap work comes first. A missed item cannot be rescued, and rules live in code. Changes pass gates, failure is planned, and every cost starts from one unit.
  • Practice is a cycle. Run a variation, record it, grade it on five dimensions, and fix the weakest dimension at its source.
  • Study next where your recording is weakest. Start inside this course, then move to general system design and AI fundamentals.
  • In a system design interview, name the family and the known system in the first minutes, and keep to the clock. Always reach the feedback loop, the risks, and the cost.

This lesson completes the course. In the weeks before your interview, drill with Rapid-Fire Questions and review the Decision Cheat Sheets. Then run one more variation, out loud and on the clock.

Check Your Understanding

1. An interviewer asks for a feature that drafts replies to customer reviews for restaurant owners. What do your first two sentences sound like?

Show answer

"This is generative, because the system produces content. The closest known system is email drafting from the SaaS capstone, where a person reviews every draft." Then name what changes. Here, the replies are public, so the owner's review before posting matters even more. Then confirm the scope with the interviewer.

2. Your recording shows that you reached minute 43 without saying what the system costs. Which dimension does this lower, and what do you study?

Show answer

Communication and clock. The strong-hire bar closes with the loop, the risks, and the bill. Practice the clock from How to Run a Case Study. Then practice pricing one unit out loud with Back of the Envelope Math for AI Systems.

3. Name one place where the course put a rule in code instead of in the prompt. Why there?

Show answer

The refund limit lives in the tool's code. A model can be wrong, and it can be tricked by prompt injection. An instruction in the prompt is only a request to the model. A limit in code holds even when the model is fooled. The permission filter inside the index query follows the same idea.

4. Which idea links Module 3's funnel to Module 5's RAG pipeline?

Show answer

A stage that misses something cannot be rescued later. In the funnel, an item that retrieval drops never reaches the ranker. In RAG, a passage that is never retrieved cannot ground the answer. In both, retrieval sets the ceiling, so its recall is measured on its own.
