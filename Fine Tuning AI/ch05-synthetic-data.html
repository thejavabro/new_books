Synthetic Data

When real examples are scarce, a large model can write them for you. Done carelessly it produces bland, repetitive data; done well it can bootstrap a specialist.

1

The teacher-student idea

Sometimes there is simply not enough real data: the domain is niche, or the data is locked behind privacy law (real patient questions fall under HIPAA). Synthetic data uses a large, capable teacher model to demonstrate a task many times, and a small student learns from the resulting dataset. It is a form of distillation, but the student never sees the teacher's probabilities or weights, only its examples. You pay the big-model bill once, to create data, instead of on every request forever.

Check the teacher's termsMany hosted models forbid using their output to train other models. Running an open-weights teacher yourself (the book uses GPT-OSS: 20B for runnable code, 120B for its published datasets) removes the ambiguity, because you own the outputs. The running example: 1,000 medical question-answer pairs, a domain where accuracy matters and real data is protected.
2

Why 'generate 10 Q&A pairs' fails

Asking a teacher to "generate medical Q&A pairs" a hundred times gives you a hundred variations of the same few questions: same diseases, same openings ("What are the symptoms of..."). The student learns a narrow, formulaic style. The fix is seeds: constrain each request with a specific category, condition and question type, drawn at random. The book's 8 categories, 5 conditions each and 8 question types give 320 combinations. Seeds are combinatorial rather than drawn from real-world frequencies, so rare edge cases still need real data.

3

Temperature and batching

The generator in the book runs at temperature 0.9 for variety, stores each seed with its output for traceability, and bans invented drug names and doses in the system prompt. Temperature reshapes the next-token distribution: low values pile probability onto the top choice (predictable), high values flatten it (diverse, riskier). Verifiers, by contrast, run cold, around 0.1, for consistency.

4

Evol-Instruct: grow harder questions

Seeded generation lands everything at the teacher's default difficulty. Real users range from "is my headache a sign of high blood pressure?" to tangled multi-condition questions. Evol-Instruct (from the WizardLM paper) rewrites questions to be harder: add constraints, demand deeper reasoning, increase specificity, broaden scope. Evolve about 20 to 30% of examples. Two cautions: vary your few-shot examples or every evolved patient becomes the same 72-year-old, and always regenerate the answer from scratch, since the old one no longer fits.

Chain-of-thought examplesTo teach a student to show its work, the training answers must contain the reasoning. The book's CoT prompt asks for labeled sections (clinical considerations, reasoning, recommendation). Simple factual questions do not need it; roughly two thirds plain pairs and one third CoT, concentrated on the evolved, harder questions, is a sensible mix.
5

Quality control is most of the work

Teachers hallucinate: invented drugs, phantom guidelines, wrong doses, all sounding plausible. A second model acting as verifier (ideally a bigger or independently trained one, at low temperature) flags suspect pairs; in the book's experience around 10% get flagged, mostly true errors. Then measure diversity: the share of n-grams that are unique. Below about 0.7 means the teacher has fallen into templates. Raise temperature and vary seeds.
