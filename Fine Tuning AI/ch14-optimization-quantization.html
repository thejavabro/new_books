From it trains to it ships

Your merged medical Gemma is about 15 to 18 GB in 16-bit and needs that much VRAM before it reads one token. That is fine on a rented A100, but not on a clinician's laptop, an air-gapped mini-PC or a $200/month budget. If the model you own only runs on hardware you rent, owning it loses much of its point. Three independent levers close the gap: merge the adapter into the base, quantize 16-bit weights to about 4, and tame the KV cache. They move you around a triangle of size, speed and accuracy, and you cannot max out all three.

Treat optimization as a pipeline of reversible, measured decisions. After merging, check parity. After quantizing, measure perplexity and task accuracy. The people who get burned quantize once, eyeball three outputs and ship.

2

Merging the adapter, and the 4-bit trap

Merging evaluates Wmerged = Wbase + (α / r) · BA once per adapted layer, writes it into the weight and drops A and B. You get a plain model that any serving stack loads, with no per-token adapter matmuls (worth a few percent of throughput). It does not make the model smaller. The trap: calling merge_and_unload() on the 4-bit QLoRA base adds the 16-bit delta to already-compressed weights, a sloppy double quantization. Reload the base in bfloat16, merge, save the tokenizer from the adapter folder, then quantize as a separate step.

Verify parity

Run a few prompts through base + adapter and through the merged model. Compare next-token argmax and max |Δlogit|, not whole greedy transcripts: tiny rounding differences make long decodes drift apart even when the merge is correct. A broken merge (wrong dtype, wrong tokenizer, corrupted weights) shows a different top token with a large gap. If protobuf errors appear, set USE_TF=0.

3

How quantization actually works

float32 spends 1 sign, 8 exponent (range) and 23 mantissa (precision) bits. float16 keeps 5 and 10; bfloat16 keeps float32's 8-bit exponent with only 7 mantissa bits, which makes it the training default. Gemma 4 E4B's on-disk size is larger than "4B × 2 bytes" because Per-Layer Embeddings add large lookup tables. "E" means effective parameters. The quantizer shrinks the stored weights.

For inference you need far fewer bits, because no gradients are flowing. Store small integers plus a recipe: scale = (max − min) / 15, zero_point = round(−min / scale), q = clamp(round(w / scale) + zp, 0, 15). Try it, and notice what one outlier does.

Smaller groups (32, 64 or 128; 128 is the common default) mean better fidelity and a few more scales to store. Most LLM weight quantizers are symmetric or nearly so, per group. They are also weight-only: activations have per-input outliers, so quantizing them (int8 end to end) is a high-throughput production technique, not the default.

4

The format zoo: where will it run?

Hard wallsQuantized files are one-way: you cannot fine-tune a GGUF or GPTQ, so archive the bf16 merged model and regenerate quants from it. GPTQ, AWQ and EXL2 do not run on CPUs or Macs. bitsandbytes is never a deployment format. And the speed comes from fused kernels (llama.cpp's per-backend K-quant kernels, Marlin in vLLM), which unpack 4-bit tiles on-chip straight into the matmul. Inference is memory-bound, so moving a quarter of the bytes is the win. Benchmark on the runtime you will deploy.
5

GGUF, K-quants and the bit-width sweep

Two llama.cpp steps: convert_hf_to_gguf.py --outtype f16 (a container change, no compression), then llama-quantize … Q4_K_M. Read Q4_K_M as: about 4 bits average, a K-quant (block structure with per-block scales), size tier M (S, M, L: how many important tensors keep extra bits). An importance matrix computed over in-domain calibration text tells the quantizer which weights to protect. The book's run gave 14,236 MiB at 16 BPW and 5,042 MiB at 5.67 BPW.

A better way forward: QAT

Post-training quantization (PTQ) compresses weights that never had to tolerate rounding, so some quality is simply lost. Quantization-aware training simulates the rounding during training, so the weights settle onto the grid. Full QAT used to mean a second, expensive training run. Gemma 4 ships official QAT checkpoints: -qat-q4_0-gguf (llama.cpp tools), -qat-w4a16-ct (vLLM or SGLang) and -qat-q4_0-unquantized, plus -assistant draft models.

The one-line win for fine-tunersShipping Google's QAT GGUF would throw away your fine-tune. Instead, fine-tune on top of google/gemma-4-e4b-it-qat-q4_0-unquantized, then merge and quantize as usual. You inherit quantization-robust weights. It is not identical to running end-to-end QAT yourself, so still measure. If your model has no QAT checkpoint, use PTQ and measure.

The KV cache: the memory that grows

Each token's keys and values are computed once and reused by every later token. That is what makes generation fast, and it costs memory that grows linearly with context. On long inputs it can exceed the weights. kv_bytes = 2 × layers × kv_heads × head_dim × seq_len × batch × bytes.
