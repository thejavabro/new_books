The Mechanics of Adaptation

1

Why not just retrain everything?

Full fine-tuning updates every weight. It works, but at modern scale it brings three problems. Memory: an 8B model needs about 16 GB to run, yet with Adam's two moment estimates, gradients and activations, training it in 16-bit needs roughly 68 to 72 GB. Catastrophic forgetting: gradients for your task overwrite what the model knew, so it may ace medical answers and lose conversation, summarizing or reasoning skills. Storage: every task is another full multi-gigabyte copy to store and serve.

2

Parameter-efficient fine-tuning

PEFT methods freeze the base model and train a tiny number of new parameters, like a crash course that brings a topic to the front of the mind rather than a brain rewrite. Less memory, much less forgetting, and outputs measured in megabytes. Earlier methods had drawbacks: prefix tuning eats context with virtual tokens; bottleneck adapters add inference latency. LoRA avoids both and became the default.

The analogyTo change a photo's contrast you can repaint every pixel (full fine-tuning) or lay a thin tinted film over it (LoRA). Or: instead of retraining every employee in a company, hire a small team of specialists to work alongside them. The rank decides how many specialists.
3

LoRA: a low-rank update

Take one frozen weight matrix W₀ of size d × d. Full fine-tuning learns an update ΔW of the same size. LoRA (Hu et al., 2021) bets that useful updates are low rank, and factors ΔW = B·A with B of size d × r and A of size r × d, r much smaller than d. The layer computes h = W₀x + (α/r)·B·A·x. B starts at zero (so training begins exactly at the base model) and A gets a random Kaiming init, so gradients can flow from the first step.

Where to attach adapters

The original paper adapted only the query and value projections and already got strong results. Practice since then favors all linear layers: Q, K, V, O and the feed-forward projections (gated FFNs like Gemma's have three). PEFT accepts target_modules="all-linear" so you do not need each model's layer names. Embeddings and the output head are usually left alone because touching them can destabilize training, unless you truly need new vocabulary.

5

Merge it in, or keep it separate

After training, the update is plain arithmetic: W = W₀ + (α/r)·B·A. Merge it and you get a model with exactly the original shape and zero extra latency. Or keep adapters separate and swap them per request, one base model serving many specialists (Chapter 15).

QLoRA: a 4-bit base underneath

LoRA shrinks what you train, but the frozen base still sits in VRAM at 16 bits: about 16 GB for an 8B model, 20 to 24 GB in total with activations. QLoRA loads the base in 4 bits and trains 16-bit adapters on top. For Gemma 4 E4B that means roughly 10 to 12 GB (not a clean 4× cut, because large embedding tables are not quantized). DoRA: magnitude and direction

DoRA splits each weight into a magnitude and a direction and applies the low-rank update to the direction, with the magnitude as a separate trainable knob, closer to how full fine-tuning moves weights. Reported gains are modest but consistent (around 1 to 4%) and cost nothing at inference. In PEFT it is a single flag, use_dora=True.

Figures are the author's rough experience. His guidance: start with QLoRA on modest GPUs, move to LoRA if you have the VRAM, then try DoRA as a cheap experiment once a baseline works. Common mistakes: forgetting prepare_model_for_kbit_training, using the wrong chat template, using high ranks on small datasets, and using full-fine-tuning learning rates (LoRA usually wants higher ones).
