1

Why teach a model to see

The text-only medical model can talk about pneumonia but, handed a chest X-ray, can only guess. Many of the most valuable AI tasks pair pictures with words: reading scans, spotting a bad solder joint, turning a screenshot of a broken layout into a bug report. Vision language models take an image and a question and answer with genuine visual grounding. Three things made them practical: CLIP-style encoders that understand images in terms of language, instruction tuning, and the PEFT stack you already know. Gemma 4 is natively multimodal: the language_model prefix you saw in adapter names sits beside a vision_tower.

2

The two-tower architecture

Every modern VLM follows one blueprint: an image encoder turns pixels into vectors, a projector maps them into the language model's embedding space, and the language model reads them as if they were more words. Trace one request: a phone screenshot and "What buttons are visible on this screen?"

3

Patches, pooling and the token budget

Gemma 4 keeps the image's aspect ratio, resizes to a pixel budget (sides divisible by 48 = 16-pixel patches × 3 pooling), encodes 16 × 16 patches, then pools 3 × 3 blocks into soft tokens. You choose the budget: 70, 140, 280, 560 or 1,120 tokens per image. Fine print or hairline fractures need more; counting ducks needs fewer. More tokens means more sequence and more activation memory.

2D RoPE: where is this patch?

Each patch vector is split in half: one half is rotated by its column, the other by its row. Attention can then tell "above" from "to the right of", which the 1D positions of SigLIP had to infer.

4

Inside Gemma 4 E4B

Loaded as Gemma4ForConditionalGeneration, the model holds a vision tower (Gemma4VisionModel, a compact ViT), a projector (Gemma4MultimodalEmbedder, embed_vision) and the decoder (Gemma4TextModel), plus an audio tower in some variants. LLaVA first showed the recipe: a CLIP encoder, a Llama model and one linear layer, trained on GPT-4-written visual instructions. The encoder knows how to see, the LM how to talk; fine-tuning mostly teaches what to say about what it sees.

5

What to fine-tune, what to freeze

How to choose: ask the base model to "describe this image in detail". If it names the main elements, strategy 2. If it misses the obvious (calling an X-ray "a black-and-white picture"), the encoder may need strategy 3. After a projector-level fine-tune, a model that misses visually obvious features needs encoder work; one that sees them but describes them badly needs language-side work.

No QLoRA here (for now)bitsandbytes quantizes every linear layer, including the small vision encoder, which then stops encoding images properly. The model hallucinates confidently, training looks fine, and only inspection reveals it. So the book trains plain LoRA on a bf16 base: about 19 to 22 GB (LM ~15 GB, vision ~0.35 GB, audio ~0.6 GB, LoRA ~70 MB, activations and image features 2 to 5 GB). Plan for a 24 GB GPU or a Colab A100.
6

A vision dataset: UI screenshots to JSON

Each example is a conversation whose user turn contains an image plus text. The book's dataset: 250 synthetic phone screenshots (13 screen types, 6 palettes) drawn with Pillow, recording every element as it is drawn, then annotated by a teacher VLM (Gemma 4 31B) for layout, elements and accessibility issues, as JSON. 200 to 500 good, diverse examples are usually enough; quality beats quantity. Randomized question phrasings stop the student overfitting to one prompt.

"Analyze this mobile app screenshot."

    

  

  
Why JSON? The base already understands screenshots well, so training on prose would barely change anything. Teaching a format it does not produce makes the value obvious and parseable by CI or a dashboard. In domains like radiology or defect inspection you gain on both fronts: seeing new things and saying them your way.

8
Is it actually looking?

  
VLMs add a new failure: answering from the question alone. Run the mismatch test first: send two very different images with the same question and compare the answers. Near-identical answers mean it is not looking, and nothing else matters until that is fixed. Then use a vision-capable judge that also sees the image (accuracy, completeness, specificity, usefulness, 1 to 5), plus programmatic checks: does the JSON parse and have the expected fields? Keep generation temperature at 0.1 to 0.3, or greedy, for JSON.
