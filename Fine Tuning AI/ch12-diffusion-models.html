Diffusion Models

Image generators do not predict the next token. They learn to remove noise, step by step, guided by text. Meet the four parts of a diffusion pipeline.

1

From next token to denoise a bit

Chapter 11 taught a transformer to read pixels. Diffusion models do the reverse: they paint. You could try generating an image autoregressively, one pixel at a time in raster order (PixelRNN and PixelCNN did). But a 512 × 512 RGB image has 786,432 values, so that means 786,432 sequential passes. Worse, early pixels are locked in: the ears you committed to at the top may belong to a fox by the time you reach the bottom.

Diffusion starts with every pixel at once, as pure static, and refines the whole canvas in parallel for a few dozen steps. It also works on a compressed latent, so there are fewer values to refine. The good news for a fine-tuner: LoRA, PEFT, data curation and the train, save, evaluate loop all carry over. What changes is the network (a UNet), the loss (mean squared error on noise, not cross-entropy on tokens) and the data (image-caption pairs).

2

Controlled destruction: the forward process

Take any image and add a whisper of Gaussian noise (ε ~ N(0, 1): one bell-curve random number per value, centered on zero). Repeat. By step T = 1,000 it is indistinguishable from static. A beta schedule is the volume knob. Stable Diffusion's "scaled linear" schedule runs from β₁ = 0.00085 (√β ≈ 0.029, so a +1.5 draw becomes a +0.044 nudge) to β₁₀₀₀ = 0.012 (√β ≈ 0.11, so the same draw becomes +0.17). Thanks to the schedule you can jump straight to any step: xt = √ᾱt · x₀ + √(1 − ᾱt) · ε.

Why a schedule, not a constant?With fixed noise per step, most timesteps would be trivially easy (almost clean) or hopeless (almost pure noise). The schedule spreads training across the middle range, where the model has to decide what is signal and what is noise.
3

Learning to undo it

Training creates triples: noisy latent xt, timestep t, and the exact noise ε that was added. The network sees only xt and t and guesses εθ. Comparing a known target with a same-shaped prediction is a textbook case for MSE. Timesteps are sampled at random so every step is learned on its own. At inference you start from pure noise, which has no original image behind it. The network's knowledge of real images makes it "hallucinate" something plausible, and conditioning on a prompt steers it. Here hallucination is the feature.

999current timestep

Idealized: the noise predictor is assumed perfect, so each step moves exactly along the schedule. A real model's guesses are noisier, and the prompt decides what emerges.

4

The UNet: global context, pixel precision

To decide whether a value is noise or signal, the network needs the whole scene (global context). Its output must also line up exactly with each position (pixel precision). Downsampling gives context: each halving doubles a feature's view. Skip connections carry high-resolution detail straight across to the upsampling side. SD 1.5's UNet adds attention layers and a timestep embedding on top of the classic encoder-decoder. Tap a block.

5

The latent trick and the full pipeline

A variational autoencoder learns to compress, unlike ZIP or JPEG, which follow hand-designed rules. SD's VAE packs 512 × 512 × 3 (786,432 values) into a 64 × 64 × 4 latent (16,384 values), so the diffusion works on 48× less data. One generation is three kinds of forward pass: CLIP once, the UNet about 20 times, the VAE decoder once.

Cross-attention: tap a region of the latent

"a corgi wearing sunglasses"

Image features are the queries; the 77 × 768 text vectors are the keys and values. Each location asks which words apply to it and pulls in a weighted blend. It is the VLM's cross-modal attention running the other way. The timestep enters separately, as a sinusoidal embedding (like transformer positions) that conditions every layer.

6

Schedulers and classifier-free guidance

The scheduler has no weights. In training it defines how much noise sits at step t. At inference it turns (latent, predicted noise, t) into the next, cleaner latent. DDPM's 1,000 steps are too slow, so modern solvers jump:

Typical step counts. Swapping SD 1.5 from a 50-step default to DPM++ at 25 steps takes it from about 12 to 15 seconds down to 6 to 8, with little visible difference.

Classifier-free guidance

During training the prompt is dropped 5 to 10% of the time, so one network learns both conditional and unconditional prediction. At inference each step runs the UNet twice and computes ε = εuncond + s · (εcond − εuncond). That doubles the compute, but prompt adherence improves a lot. Replace the empty prompt with a negative prompt ("blurry, watermark, extra fingers") and the push is away from those. Keep the negative prompt fixed when comparing base vs. LoRA.

7

Where the LoRA goes

Almost every diffusion fine-tune attaches LoRA to the UNet's attention projections. The same four names cover self-attention (the image attending to itself) and cross-attention (the image attending to the text). The text encoder (123.1M) and VAE (83.7M) stay frozen in nearly all cases. The exceptions are rare-token training such as DreamBooth or Textual Inversion, where a new token like <my-subject> needs a meaningful embedding.

lora_config = LoraConfig(
    r=8, lora_alpha=8,           # 8 to 32 typical for images
    target_modules=["to_q", "to_k", "to_v", "to_out.0"],
    lora_dropout=0.0, bias="none")   # note: no task_type

Pipeline components in millions of parameters. The rank-8 LoRA adds about 0.8M (0.09% of the UNet), placed exactly where image and text meet.

The objective stays the same: MSE between predicted and actual noise. You need no preference data or reward model, only image-caption pairs and gradient descent. Chapter 13 puts this to work with DreamBooth and style LoRAs.
