Excavating Data

The model is open; your data is the moat. Where training data comes from, how to dig it out, and how to clean it until it is worth learning from.

1

Data is the moat

Architectures are published, training tricks are on blogs, base models are a download away. What competitors cannot download is the data your organization has gathered over years. That is the moat. The author also sees a second opportunity: modern models can extract value from unstructured data that used to be ignored, and that work moves developers up the value chain. Keep three pillars in mind, with equal weight:

RelevanceDoes it match your target domain?
CorrectnessIs it accurate?
DiversityDoes it cover the inputs you will see in production?

And a reassuring rule of thumb: with LoRA, a few hundred good examples can already move the needle.

2

Where raw data comes from

Hugging Face Hub datasets load in one line and arrive with named columns. Good learning sets: Alpaca-cleaned (52k instruction pairs), OpenOrca (about 1M chain-of-thought traces), UltraChat 200k (multiturn dialogue) and Dolly-15k (human-written, CC BY-SA, one of the few cleanly commercial options). Kaggle has hundreds of thousands of messier, domain-rich datasets, great practice for cleaning. Your real advantage, though, is internal data: wikis, tickets, manuals, CRM records.

Pick a source

Licensing is a production blockerApache 2.0 and MIT are permissive, but many popular sets were distilled from proprietary model outputs whose terms forbid building competing models. Kaggle sets are often non-commercial or competition-only. Check the license and the data source of the data, and of whatever it was derived from.
The data interviewBefore scraping anything, sit with the people who do the work. What does a great answer look like? What mistakes do juniors make? What matters most? Their answers define "quality" in a way no metric can, and decide what you collect.

Cleaning step 1: normalize the text

Garbage in, garbage out is worse for fine-tuning than for pretraining: tuning overwrites behavior, so sloppy data teaches the model to be wrong with confidence. Start by making text consistent. The same visible "é" can be one code point or "e" plus a combining accent, giving different tokens. NFC merges true equivalents safely; NFKC also folds lookalikes like the "ﬁ" ligature and "²" (lossy: "10²" becomes "102"). Cleaning step 2: filter what does not belong

Next, drop examples that do not belong. Length filters are often the biggest win: tiny examples are fragments, huge ones get truncated mid-thought. Language detection (fastText, langdetect) removes stray languages. Quality scoring uses heuristics such as stop-word ratio or brace density, and perplexity: how surprised a reference model is by the text. Topic filters range from keywords to embedding similarity against reference documents.

A tiny support-ticket corpus

Perplexity in one lineAverage the log-probabilities the model gives each token, negate, exponentiate. The result is the model's "effective number of choices" per token: fluent prose scores in the tens, character soup in the thousands.
5

Cleaning step 3: deduplicate

Duplicates make the model memorize phrasings instead of learning patterns, turning an understanding engine into a lookup table. Exact duplicates fall to a hash. Near-duplicates need fingerprints: MinHash estimates the overlap of word or n-gram sets and LSH finds similar fingerprints fast. Substring containment (a paragraph also inside a longer document) needs n-gram overlap or suffix-array tools. Scraped web data is usually more duplicated than you expect.

Similarity here is Jaccard overlap of word sets, which is what MinHash approximates.

6

Cleaning step 4: remove personal data before training

Models can memorize training text and repeat it: researchers extracted verbatim training data simply by asking a model to repeat one word forever until it drifted. A model cannot unlearn PII, and GDPR, CCPA and HIPAA make the stakes legal. Detect with regular expressions for structured patterns (emails, phones, card numbers), NER models for names and places, or Microsoft Presidio, which combines both and gives confidence scores you threshold. 7

Audit quality before you train

Profile lengths (in your model's tokens), look for outliers and especially for imbalance: a model is strong where data is plentiful and weak elsewhere. Fix it by upsampling, downsampling or, best, collecting more. Embedding plots (UMAP, t-SNE) reveal off-topic clusters. Then apply the "random 50" rule: read 50 random examples carefully; if more than two or three make you wince, there is a systemic problem. Finally check for contamination: eval examples leaking into training make your scores meaningless.

A reproducible, config-driven pipeline

Chain the stages so each reads the previous stage's output and never edits the raw data. Put thresholds (lengths, language, MinHash threshold, PII strategy) in a YAML config, so changing one means rerunning from that stage onward. Frameworks like NeMo Curator or Unstructured exist, but build it by hand once so you learn your data's quirks. Save as JSONL (one JSON object per line) or, past about 1 GB, Arrow/Parquet. And write a dataset card: sources, dates, steps, size, limitations, license.
