Hosted giant models are wonderful for demos and painful for products. This chapter adds up what renting intelligence really costs, and argues for owning a small specialist instead.

The honeymoon and the API wall

Most teams start the same way: an API key, a clever system prompt, and a demo that works beautifully. The trouble comes when the demo turns into a product. The book calls the barrier the API wall: the point where cost at scale, unpredictable latency and the security team's reaction to "we send customer data to a third party" stop the project.

The author's answer is to stop renting intelligence by the token and start owning it: take a small open model and fine-tune it into a specialist that knows your domain, runs where you choose, and does not change unless you change it.

The token tax: a cost calculator

Hosted models charge per token, separately for input and output, and a token is roughly three quarters of a word. That feels like nothing during prototyping. Multiply it by real traffic and it becomes the biggest line on the bill. The book's running example is a contract analyzer: about 15,000 tokens of contract plus a long system prompt in, around 1,000 tokens of findings out, 5,000 documents a day.

The instruction tax

A generalist knows nothing about your task until you tell it, so every request carries the same rule book: role, format, edge cases, JSON schema. You pay for those tokens on every single call. Long instructions also hurt quality: models tend to lose track of material buried in the middle of a long context, which is why prompts fill up with shouting in CAPITALS.

Fine-tuning moves the rules out of the context window, which you pay for every time, and into the weights, which you pay for once.

Latency: TTFT and TPS

Cost is a business problem; latency is a user experience problem. Two numbers matter. Time to first token (TTFT) is how long before anything appears. Tokens per second (TPS) is how fast the rest streams out. A chatbot can live with a half-second pause. Code autocomplete and voice cannot: if the suggestion arrives after you have typed it yourself, it is useless. A huge shared model also queues your request behind everyone else's.

The specialist revolution

For years the rule was "bigger is better", and big meant you had to rent from whoever owned the data center. That changed when capable open models small enough to self-host appeared (LLaMA in early 2023 kicked it off), with licenses that allow it. Research shifted from pure scale toward smarter training, and small models began to punch above their weight. Hardware stopped being the blocker too: unified memory on modern laptops can hold very large models, so a fine-tuning lab now fits in a backpack.
