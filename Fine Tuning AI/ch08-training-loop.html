The Training Loop

The loop itself is short: forward, loss, backward, step. The craft is in the learning rate, the batch, the curves you watch and the failures you learn to read.

Assembling the pieces

Everything so far comes together here: the medical Q&A dataset (Chapters 5 and 6), the instruction-tuned Gemma 4 E4B base, and a QLoRA adapter on all linear layers (Chapter 7). What remains is the training loop: feed batches, compute the loss, backpropagate, update the adapter, repeat. You rarely write that loop yourself. You declare it in a config and hand it to a trainer.

2

Read the loss curves

The two curves you watch are training loss and validation loss. The book's run (549 training and 62 validation examples, rank 4, 20 epochs) shows both dropping fast for about 50 steps, flattening, and validation leveling near 0.88 while a small gap opens after step 75: the first signs of memorization. Play with the knobs and learn to recognize the five classic shapes. This is a toy model of the behavior, not a real trainer.

3

Learning rate and its schedule

The learning rate is the most important knob. LoRA usually wants 1e-4 to 2e-4, higher than full fine-tuning's 1e-5 to 5e-5, because adapters start at zero and must move quickly. Rules of thumb: smooth but slow, raise it 2 to 5×; spiky or jumping, cut it 2 to 5×. Rather than holding it constant, warm up from near zero (big early gradients can throw the model somewhere bad), then decay. Cosine decay, gentle at both ends, is the author's default.

4

Batch size and gradient accumulation

Bigger batches give smoother gradients but cost memory. Gradient accumulation fakes a big batch: run several small forward-backward passes, add up their gradients, then take one optimizer step. Effective batch = per-device batch × accumulation steps × GPUs. The book uses 8 × 8 × 1 = 64, so 549 examples make about 9 optimizer steps per epoch, and 20 epochs only about 180 updates. Memory holds just 8 examples at a time; the price is speed.

5

Gradient clipping and the optimizer

Gather every gradient into one vector and you can measure the size of the proposed step. max_grad_norm caps it: if the norm is above the limit, every gradient is scaled down by the same factor, keeping direction and shrinking size. The book uses an aggressive 0.3, common for QLoRA, whose quantized base makes gradients noisy. If grad_norm sits at the cap all the time, you have effectively lowered your learning rate; try 0.5 or 1.0.

paged_adamw_8bitAdamW is Adam with decoupled weight decay, keeping a running mean and variance per parameter. "8bit" stores those states in 8 bits (4× smaller); "paged" spills rarely used state to CPU memory during spikes to avoid out-of-memory crashes. For a 16-bit setup, plain adamw_torch is fine. Weight decay (0.05 here, higher than the usual 0.01 because the dataset is tiny) gently shrinks weights to discourage memorization.
6

The memory budget

Gemma 4 E4B is the cautionary tale: of about 8B parameters only about 4.5B sit in quantizable linear layers (about 2.8 GB at 4-bit with overhead); about 3.5B are embedding tables kept in bfloat16 (about 7 GB). A "4-bit model" is not 4-bit everywhere. Adapters, gradients and 8-bit optimizer states for 9.7M parameters are tiny; activations and framework overhead are not. Toggle the savings:

0

Gradient checkpointing stores only layer-boundary activations and recomputes the rest during backprop: about 60 to 70% less activation memory for extra compute (use use_reentrant: False with PEFT). bf16 keeps float32's range with less precision, so no loss scaler is needed (Ampere GPUs and newer; otherwise fp16 with a scaler). Leave torch.compile off with 4-bit QLoRA until everything else works.

Packing

median example ≈ 435 tokens, sequence limit 1,024

Without packing, roughly half of each sequence is padding the GPU still processes. Packing fits about two examples per sequence for nearly double the throughput. The book leaves it off for a 30-minute run; recheck the loss curve if you turn it on.

7

The loss, masking, padding and checkpoints

The loss is cross-entropy on next-token prediction, averaged over the tokens you count. With Gemma 4 the model scores every one of 262,144 vocabulary entries at every position. Data prep applies the chat template into a single text column (dataset_text_field="text"). Interesting choice in the book's run: no label masking, so the model also learns question patterns, arguably useful for long conversations. For tasks with long inputs and short outputs, such as summarization, masking matters a lot.
