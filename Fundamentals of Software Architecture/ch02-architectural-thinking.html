Architecture versus design

The old picture: the architect hands a document down to developers and walks away. Decisions do not survive contact with the code because nothing flows back. The book argues for a two-way, collaborative relationship.

Strategic or tactical: a spectrum

Most decisions are neither pure architecture nor pure design. Three questions place a decision on the spectrum: how many people are involved, how long it will last, and how much effort it takes. Slide them or pick an example.

3

Technical breadth beats depth

Think of everything you know as a pyramid. A developer grows the top: deep expertise. An architect grows the middle: many things they know about, even without hands-on depth. Click a layer.

Stuff you know

Technologies you use daily and are an expert in. Expertise needs constant maintenance, so this layer is expensive to keep large.

Stuff you know you don't know

You know a graph database or a stream processor exists and roughly what it is good for, but you have not built with it. This is the architect's most valuable layer: a wide middle gives more options when solving problems.

Stuff you don't know you don't know

The best solution to your problem may live here, and you cannot pick it because you have never heard of it. Reading, conferences and a personal radar move items up.

Frozen caveman antipattern. An architect who reverts to an irrational fear because of one bad experience, insisting every design must handle that one failure, no matter how unlikely in this context.
4

Build a personal technology radar

Thoughtworks publishes a Technology Radar: four quadrants of things, four rings of confidence. The book suggests keeping your own, so you invest deliberately instead of following hype. Click a ring.

Adopt

Things you are confident in and actively use or recommend. For a personal radar, this is your toolkit.

Trial

Worth real use in a low-risk setting: a side project, a spike, a proof of concept.

Assess

Worth reading about and understanding. You have heard good things and want to know more before committing time.

Hold

Proceed with caution. For a personal radar, this is also where things go to stop absorbing your attention: bad habits and hype.

Quadrants: tools, languages and frameworks, techniques, platforms.

5

Analysing trade-offs: topic or queues?

The book's classic example: an auction system where a producer sends bids to three consumers. Publish-and-subscribe (one topic) or point-to-point (one queue per consumer)? Neither is "best". Compare.

GainsExtensibility: add a consumer without touching the producer. Decoupled: producer does not know who listens.
CostsSecurity: anyone can subscribe and see every bid. One contract for all consumers. Cannot monitor or scale per consumer.
GainsEach queue can carry a different contract, be secured on its own and be monitored and auto-scaled by queue depth.
CostsThe producer is coupled to every consumer. Adding a consumer means a new queue and a producer change.

Add Bid History. With the topic, it just subscribes: zero changes upstream. With queues, someone edits the producer. Which matters more here, extensibility or security and per-consumer control? That depends on the business: first law.
