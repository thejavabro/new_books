Architectural Characteristics Defined

Architectural characteristics are the -ilities a system must support. This chapter defines what counts, sorts them into families, and explains why you aim for the least worst architecture.

What makes something an architectural characteristic

A requirement is an architectural characteristic only when it meets three criteria at once. Click each circle.

Specifies a non-domain design consideration. Domain requirements say what the system does (calculate tax). Characteristics say how well it must operate (respond in 200 ms, survive a data-center loss).

Influences some structural aspect of the design. If a requirement can be satisfied by ordinary design inside one component, it is not architectural. Payment security that needs its own isolated, audited service is.

Is critical or important to application success. Each characteristic adds complexity, so support as few as possible. The aim is the least worst architecture, not every -ility.

2

Explicit and implicit characteristics

Some characteristics show up in requirement documents. Many more are never written down because everyone assumes them. Nobody writes "the site should not leak card numbers", but it is required.

3

Four families of characteristics

The book groups characteristics into operational (how it runs), structural (code quality and structure), cloud (new in the 2nd edition) and cross-cutting (everything that spans the others). Sort these.

Trade-offs and the least worst architecture

Characteristics fight each other. More security (encryption, extra hops) slows performance; more availability adds moving parts and kills simplicity. Push a slider up and watch the others respond. The goal is not the best architecture, but the least worst one for your context.

5

Words mean different things to different people

There is no standard list of characteristics, and the same word can mean several things. Flip the cards to see how ambiguous common terms are.

The fix is ubiquitous language for your organisation: agree on definitions so teams argue about trade-offs, not words.
