How to Model Microservices

Good boundaries are the whole game. Hide information, keep related behavior together, keep coupling loose, and use the business domain to find where the seams belong.

1

Information hiding: fewer assumptions, freer change

Microservices are just modular decomposition with a network in between. So the best guidance for boundaries comes from decades of thinking about modules, starting with David Parnas. He argued that modules should deliver three things: faster development (work in parallel), comprehensibility (understand each part alone) and flexibility (change one part without the others). Having modules doesn't guarantee any of this. What decides it is where the boundaries go and what they hide.

Parnas's insight: the connections between modules are the assumptions they make about each other. Fewer assumptions, fewer connections. Toggle what the Order Processor is allowed to know about the Warehouse.

2

Strong cohesion, loose coupling

Cohesion: "the code that changes together, stays together." Related behavior should sit in one place so a change touches one service. Coupling: how much a change to one service forces changes in another. A structure is stable when cohesion is strong and coupling is low, and stable boundaries are what make independent deployment workable.

3

Four kinds of coupling, from loose to tight

Some coupling is unavoidable; the aim is to minimize it and prefer the looser kinds. The book adapts ideas from structured programming into four types for microservices. Click a rung.

A note on temporal couplingIf Order Processor calls Warehouse synchronously, both must be up at the same moment for the operation to complete. That's temporal coupling. It isn't always bad, but asynchronous messaging (Chapter 4) can remove it when it hurts.
4

Three ways out of pass-through coupling

Order Processor builds a shipping manifest and hands it to Warehouse, which only forwards it to Shipping. When Shipping changes the manifest format, all three must change. Step through the options.

5

Just enough domain-driven design

Domain-driven design (Eric Evans) supplies three ideas that do most of the work for finding boundaries.

An aggregate has identity, state and a life cycle, and it enforces its own rules: if an outside party asks for an invalid state change, the aggregate says no. Try driving an Order through its life cycle.

Make cross-service references explicitWhen Finance stores a reference to a customer that lives in another service, a bare cust_id column hides the relationship. Storing a URI such as /customers/123 makes it obvious where the data lives and, in a REST system, gives you a way to fetch it.
6

Bounded contexts: hidden and shared models

A bounded context is a larger boundary inside the business, with explicit responsibilities and hidden internals. MusicCorp's Warehouse and Finance are two. Each has its own concerns (forklifts here, payroll there). A Stock Item appears in both, but it doesn't mean the same thing: Warehouse cares where it sits on a shelf, Finance cares what it's worth. Each context keeps its own model and shares only what the other needs.

7

Event storming in four passes

Event storming, from Alberto Brandolini, is a workshop where technical and nontechnical people model the domain together with sticky notes. Get everyone in one room, give them a long wall, and build the model in passes.

8

Not only the domain

Domain and organization are the author's starting points, but other forces can shape boundaries too. Mixing them is fine; beware anyone who says there is only one way.
