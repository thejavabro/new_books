What Are Microservices?

Microservices are independently releasable services modeled around a business domain. This chapter sets out the core ideas, the kinds of monolith they replace, and the honest trade-offs.

1

A black box with a business purpose

The book's working definition: microservices are independently releasable services modeled around a business domain. Each one wraps some functionality and offers it over the network, through endpoints such as an HTTP API or a queue. From outside, a microservice is a black box. How it is written, and where it keeps its data, is nobody else's business.

That is information hiding: keep as much as possible inside, expose as little as possible. What is hidden can change freely. What is exposed becomes a contract other teams lean on. Try both kinds of change below.

SOA done wellMicroservices are a kind of service-oriented architecture. They are opinionated about where to draw service boundaries and they insist on independent deployability. Many problems blamed on "SOA" came from protocols, vendor middleware and services that still shared a database, not from the idea of services.
2

The ideas that hold it together

Several ideas define the style. One matters more than the rest: if you take a single thing away, make it independent deployability. You should be able to change one microservice, deploy it and release the change to users without deploying anything else. Everything else on this list exists partly to make that possible.

Don't share databasesA shared database is the quickest way to lose independent deployability. If service B reads service A's tables, A can no longer change its own schema. If B needs A's data, it should ask A.
3

Turn the dial, don't flip a switch

"Microservices buy you options," as James Lewis puts it, and options have a price. Adopting them is less like flipping a switch and more like turning a dial: each extra service adds flexibility and also adds pain. Turn it slowly and stop where the trade feels right.

4

Organize around the business, not the tier

MusicCorp, the book's fictional CD retailer, has a classic three-tier system and three matching teams: frontend, backend, database. Conway's law predicts exactly that: systems copy the communication structure of the people who build them. Now the business asks to let customers pick a favorite genre. Who has to change something?

Most change requests are about business functionality. When that functionality is smeared across layers, every change means a cross-team handoff. Slice the system so that a stream-aligned team (a term from Team Topologies) owns a thin slice of UI, logic and data for one area, such as customer profiles, and the same change stays inside one team.

5

Three kinds of monolith

In this book a monolith is a unit of deployment: if everything must be deployed together, it is a monolith. That definition gives three shapes.

Delivery contentionAs more people work in one deployable unit, they get in each other's way: two teams want different changes in the same code, or a release waits for someone else's work. Ownership blurs. Monoliths make this more likely but don't guarantee it; microservices make it less likely but don't prevent it.
Monoliths are a valid choiceSimple deployment topology, easier end-to-end testing, easy monitoring and simple code reuse. The monolith is not the enemy; it should be the default, and microservices need a reason.
6

Enabling technology, adopted slowly

You don't need lots of new technology to start. Adopt each tool when a real problem appears. One exception: get log aggregation in place early, because it's how you'll learn whether everything else is working.

7

Benefits, and the pain that comes with them

Most advantages come from being a distributed system; microservices push them further because the boundaries follow the domain. Each one has a matching cost. Pick a benefit to see what it costs.
