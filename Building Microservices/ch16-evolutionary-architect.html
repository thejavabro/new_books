The Evolutionary Architect

Architects are less like building architects and more like town planners: set zones and principles, make change possible, and keep the whole system habitable.

1

Not that kind of architect

Our young industry borrows titles from older ones: "engineer," "architect." But a building's architect delivers a fixed plan for something that will barely change; software changes constantly after it ships, as users show us what they need. The author argues the term may have done more harm than good if it suggests detailed blueprints handed down from on high.

Ralph Johnson's famous definition: architecture is about "the important stuff. Whatever that is." Better still, it's a social construct: the shared understanding expert developers have of the system's design. Architecture happens, by design or by accident. A dedicated architect is someone who sees the whole and helps shape it.

2

The architect as town planner

A town planner doesn't say "build this building there." They define zones (residential, commercial, industrial) and leave what goes inside each to others, while caring a lot about roads, water and power between zones. For microservices, zones are services or groups of services owned by teams. Inside a zone, teams choose. Between zones is where the architect worries. Try changing things.

Stay close to the codeArchitecture is what happens, not what's planned. Architects who don't do normal development work lose touch with what's possible. Pair with teams, work on real tasks, and make the system habitable, Richard Gabriel's term for code (and tooling) that people coming later can understand and change comfortably.
A group activityGrady Booch: architecture starts as a statement of vision and ends as the emergent system. Comcast ran architecture like an open standards body, with proposals, reviews and consensus. Be aware that an architect's presence changes a group's dynamics.
3

Goals, principles and practices

Decisions are trade-offs; framing them helps. Strategic goals come from the business ("expand into Southeast Asia"). Principles are rules aligned to those goals, sometimes constraints, ideally fewer than ten (Heroku's Twelve Factors is one example set). Practices are the detailed, often technology-specific ways principles are carried out; they change more often. Evan Bottcher's one-page diagram linking the three is the book's model. 4

Guiding evolution with fitness functions

From Building Evolutionary Architectures (Ford, Parsons and Kua): a fitness function measures how close the architecture is to a desired characteristic, so you notice drift as it evolves. Their example: a response-time target checked automatically. Set some targets and run a build.

5

Architects as an enabling team

Many architect responsibilities are enabling ones. The author favors a small group of dedicated architects in a cross-cutting team, with a technical lead from each delivery team, making decisions together. What if the group goes against the architect? Like teaching a child to ride a bike: you can't ride it for them. Let them wobble; step in only if they're heading into traffic. The author has seen teams he disagreed with prove him wrong.

Governance and the paved road

Governance means agreeing how things should be done, checking they are, and changing course when needed. Do it as a group. But don't rely on written rules alone; make the right thing easy.

Afterword: the whole book on one map

The Afterword revisits every theme. Tap a chapter for its one-line takeaway, and its page in this companion.

Looking forwardThe author expects tooling to keep improving, with more developers working on higher-level platforms (FaaS and its successors) and less on raw infrastructure. He is uneasy that microservices became a default rather than a choice. His final advice: change incrementally, make each decision small and reversible, and get used to continuous evolution; that discipline matters more than any single design.
