1

Four meanings of resiliency

The book draws on David Woods's work in resilience engineering, which separates four ideas. Microservices help directly with the first; the others are as much about people and organizations as software.

2

Failure is everywhere, so decide how much matters

At scale, failure is a statistical certainty, however good your hardware. Plan for it, and spend more effort on recovering gracefully. How much failure is tolerable comes from users, expressed as cross-functional requirements per service: response time/latency (at percentiles, under given load), availability, and durability of data. Then decide how to degrade when parts fail. Take down services behind MusicCorp's product page.

3

AdvertCorp's outage and the stability patterns

A true story (company disguised). A site served ads, routing some requests to legacy systems through one shared HTTP connection pool. One minor legacy system (the "turnip" ads, used by under 5% of customers and earning even less) began responding very slowly. Callers queued for connections with no time-out, threads piled up, and the whole site fell over. Run it, then switch on the patterns the team added afterwards.

4

Circuit breakers up close

Like the breaker in your house, it cuts off a failing connection to protect everything else, and fails fast instead of slow. Fire requests and watch the states.

While open, you can queue requests for later (if they aren't time-sensitive) or fail fast and show a degraded experience, as AdvertCorp did ("no turnip ads right now"). Breakers can also be tripped manually during maintenance.

5

Idempotency makes retries safe

An idempotent operation has the same outcome however many times you apply it. "Add 100 points" isn't; "add 100 points for order 4567" can be, if the service records which orders it has credited. That lets callers (and message consumers that may see duplicates) retry safely. HTTP says GET and PUT are idempotent, but only if you implement them that way.

7

CAP: when the network splits, pick C or A

Consistency (every node gives the same answer), availability (every request gets a response), partition tolerance (the system copes when nodes can't talk). In a distributed system partitions happen, so the real choice is what to give up during one. Inventory runs in two data centers; cut the link and choose.

It's not all or nothingA system can mix: the catalog can be AP, the points balance CP. Even one service can offer both (read stale for display, consistent before spending points). Many stores let you tune consistency per request. And nobody "beats" CAP; they just make different trade-offs.
And the real worldEven a perfectly consistent inventory count can be wrong: an album gets dropped and broken in the warehouse. Often AP plus a business process for the odd mismatch ("sorry, it's out of stock; here's a refund") is the right answer. Building CP yourself is very hard; use a store that does it.
8

Chaos engineering, game days and blame

Chaos engineering, as defined by its practitioners: experimenting on a system to build confidence in its ability to withstand turbulent conditions in production. "System" includes people and processes. Game days rehearse incidents, sometimes deliberately probing weaknesses; Netflix's Chaos Monkey turns off random machines during working hours so teams must build for it. But running a chaos tool doesn't make you resilient; questioning and learning does.
