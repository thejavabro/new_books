Scaling

Scale up, scale out, partition the data or split the function. Combine them as needed, start small, and treat caching as a sharp but useful tool.

1

The four axes of scaling

We scale to improve performance or to improve robustness. The Scale Cube from The Art of Scalability gives three axes; the book adds vertical scaling as a fourth. Which to use depends on the constraint you actually have. The running example is FoodCo, a real (renamed) food delivery company whose primary database was suffering write contention. Pick an axis.

2

Choosing a partition key

Partitioning applies a function to a key to choose where data lives. A poor key gives you hot partitions. Load 400 customers and compare.

Other catches: queries across all partitions (who bought turnips last month?) mean scatter-gather or a separate read-optimized store; changing a scheme later means migrating data; and partitioning alone barely improves robustness. Often the database (Cassandra, for example) partitions for you.

3

Combine axes, and start small

Scaling along one axis often makes another easier. Order might be split out (functional decomposition), sharded per region (partitioning), and run several copies per shard (duplication). Build up a combined design and watch capacity.

Start smallKnuth: premature optimization is the root of all evil. Don't optimize for problems you don't have; experiment to confirm a bottleneck before fixing it. FoodCo tried the cheap axes (vertical, read replicas) before the expensive ones (partitioning, decomposition).
CQRS and event sourcingSeparate models for writing and reading state; event sourcing stores state as a log of events. Useful in places, complex to execute. Whether a service uses them is an internal detail, which keeps your options open.
4

Caching: for performance, scale and robustness

A cache stores a previous result so you can reuse it. It can cut latency (skip a network hop), reduce load on a contended origin, and even keep you working when the origin is down (the Guardian crawled its live site to keep a static fallback copy). Invalidation, the hard part

"There are only two hard things in computer science: cache invalidation and naming things" (Phil Karlton). Simulate a stock level that changes while a client caches it.

6

The golden rule, and a cautionary tale

Cache in as few places as possible. Each layer adds staleness: stack them and the worst case is the sum. Add layers below.

worst-case staleness a customer could see
Cache poisoningOn one project, a release accidentally set Expires: Never on a subset of pages. Squid and CDN caches could be flushed, but copies in users' browsers and in ISP proxies could not. Those pages were stuck until the team changed the URLs. Understand the whole path cached data takes, from origin to browser.
7

Autoscaling and starting again

With fully automated provisioning you can scale predictively (known daily peaks) or reactively (respond to load or failed instances). A news site needs both: a predictable morning peak and unpredictable breaking news. The author sees autoscaling used more for replacing failed instances than for load. Test your scaling policy before you need it, and scale down too, or the savings never come.

Starting againThe architecture that gets you started may not keep you going. Gilt's monolithic Rails app was redesigned when load demanded it. That's success, not failure. Building for massive scale up front is a trap: you don't yet know if anyone wants the product (Eric Ries spent six months building something nobody downloaded).
