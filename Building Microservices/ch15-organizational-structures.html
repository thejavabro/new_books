Conway's law, and the evidence for it

Melvin Conway, 1968: any organization that designs a system will produce a design whose structure copies the organization's communication structure. (Harvard Business Review reportedly rejected the paper for lack of proof.) The evidence has since arrived. Open each.

3

Small teams inside a large organization

Developers broadly agree teams should be small. The best study the author found reported productivity worst for teams averaging nine or more. Brooks's law adds: adding people to a late project makes it later, because communication paths grow faster than headcount, unless the work can be split into independent pieces. Microservices give you the pieces; stream-aligned teams own them.

communication paths: n(n−1)/2

Autonomy, and the team APIAmazon's two-pizza teams are about autonomy more than size. Team Topologies' team API describes how a team works with others: its code interfaces and its working practices. Robin Dunbar's work on how many relationships people can maintain has inspired group sizes at several companies, and UK retailer Timpson scaled by trusting staff with real authority.
4

Strong versus collective ownership

Under strong ownership, one team owns a microservice and decides its changes, internal tech, even its deployment and support (full life-cycle ownership). Under collective ownership, any team can change any service, which needs lots of coordination, and coordination couples organizations, and coupled organizations make coupled systems. Collective works for a small group; it breaks down as you grow. Run a quarter of changes.

6

When a microservice ends up shared

Why do services end up with several owners? Some are too hard to split. Some suffer cross-cutting changes: at FinanceCo, letting one account have many users touched most services; a dedicated team made the change, but restructuring to avoid one kind of cross-cutting change can create another. And some are delivery bottlenecks: the website and mobile teams both need Catalog changes and must wait for its team. Options: wait, add people to the Catalog team, split Catalog further, or try internal open source.

7

Internal open source

A core team of committers owns the service; others submit changes, which the core team reviews and merges. Good gatekeepers invest time explaining and encouraging. It works best on mature, stable services, and needs tooling (pull requests, good docs, CI). Send pull requests and see how the queue behaves.

8

Change reviews: peer, fast, synchronous

Accelerate found that teams needing approval from an external body had lower delivery performance, while teams using peer review (or no approval process) did better; approving only high-risk changes made no difference. And speed matters: a review days later costs far more than one minutes later. Compare the options.

Ensemble (mob) programming reviews inline too. Most teams the author spoke to use it occasionally, for tricky problems. He has reservations: some people thrive in it, others find it overwhelming.

It's always a people problem

Gerry Weinberg's line, "no matter how it looks at first, it's always a people problem," closes the chapter. Developers used to one monolith must think about network calls and failure; people used to throwing work over the wall must now own production. Understand your staff's appetite for change, give them time, be explicit about responsibilities, and don't force a design the organization can't support.
