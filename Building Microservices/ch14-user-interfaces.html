The UI is where capabilities come together

We went from fat desktop clients, to thin web pages, to rich single-page apps and mobile apps, and now to many channels at once: web, mobile, voice, partner APIs. The book's framing: stop thinking of "web" and "mobile" as separate strategies. The user interface is where we weave together the capabilities our microservices offer, for each audience.

2

Who owns the UI?

The traditional layered split gives the UI to a dedicated frontend team. The author much prefers stream-aligned teams that own a feature end to end, frontend included. Toggle the models and watch a feature request travel.

Micro frontendsThe organizational pattern of letting different teams build and deploy parts of a frontend independently. Page-based and widget-based decomposition are the two main ways to do it for the web. It's essential for stream-aligned teams; one unsolved snag is capabilities that don't fit neatly in one widget or page. Self-contained systems (SCS) express similar ideas; the author finds the term less useful than the organizational goal behind it.

5

Different devices, different constraints

Mobile networks are slower and costlier; screens are smaller; there's no right-click on a tablet; voice has no screen at all; accessibility needs vary. The same core capabilities need adapting per interface. That is what aggregation on the server side is for.

6

Central aggregating gateway vs backend for frontend

A mobile screen shows a customer and their five latest orders. Without help it makes six calls and throws most of the data away. Server-side aggregation and filtering fix that. The question is: one shared gateway, or one per experience?

7

How many BFFs, and how to reuse

Stewart Gleadow at REA suggested: one experience, one BFF. If the iOS and Android apps offer the same experience, one BFF might do; if they differ, give each its own. Different apps often diverge more than expected. Duplication between BFFs is tolerable for a while; when it hurts, extract a library, or a new microservice if the shared logic is a real domain concept. BFFs can also serve a complex web UI rendered on the server, or third parties that won't upgrade (a BFF per partner shields them from change).

GraphQL as a BFF or gateway

GraphQL lets the client say exactly what it wants; a resolver on the server fetches it from the right microservices. That makes it a neat way to implement a BFF or aggregating gateway. Pick fields for an order screen.
