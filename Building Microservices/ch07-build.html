Are you really doing continuous integration?

CI keeps everyone in sync by integrating new code with everyone else's often, and checking it with an automated build and tests. It gives fast feedback and a single, versioned artifact to deploy. Without it, a move to microservices gets much harder. Many teams think they do CI because they run a CI tool. Jez Humble's three questions are a better test. Answer honestly.

2

Integrate early, integrate often

Long-lived feature branches delay integration; the longer a branch lives, the worse the merge. The alternative is trunk-based development: everyone commits to trunk, and unfinished features hide behind feature flags. The State of DevOps research found that high performers keep branches very short-lived (under a day) and have few active branches. Slide the branch lifetime.

GitFlow-style branching suits open source, where many occasional, untrusted contributors send patches. Even there, the research says merging sooner is better.

3

Build pipelines and continuous delivery

A build pipeline splits the build into stages, fast tests first, so you get quick feedback and a clear view of how far each version has got. Continuous delivery (Humble and Farley) treats every check-in as a release candidate and models every step to production, manual ones included. Continuous deployment goes further: anything that passes all automated stages goes live without a human decision. Push three builds through.

Environments are a balancing actYour laptop gives the fastest feedback but is least like production; production is the most realistic and the slowest and riskiest place to learn. Each pipeline stage sits somewhere between. That gap is one reason teams increasingly test in production (Chapter 9).
4

Build once, deploy everywhere

If you rebuild for each environment, the thing you tested isn't the thing you deployed. Build one artifact per version, store it, and promote that same artifact through every stage. Anything that differs between environments belongs in configuration supplied outside the artifact.

5

Mapping source code and builds to microservices

Three ways to lay out repositories and builds. Make a one-line change to the User service and see what happens.

The author's pick: multirepoFor small teams either works. At scale, monorepo benefits (fine-grained reuse, atomic commits) don't seem to outweigh the costs: encouragement of cross-service changes, blurred ownership, and heavy tooling. Organizations that start with a monorepo under collective ownership often find it hard to move away later. And remember: an atomic commit is not an atomic deployment.
