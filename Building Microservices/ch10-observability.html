From Monitoring to Observability

With many moving parts, you need to ask questions you didn't plan for. Aggregate logs and metrics, trace requests, and decide what "working" means with SLOs.

1

Every outage a murder mystery

A popular joke: we replaced our monolith with microservices so every outage could be more like a murder mystery. With one process on one server, you know where to look. Add servers, then services, and the clues scatter. Step through the growth.

2

Observability is a property; monitoring is an activity

Observability is how well you can understand a system's internal state from its external outputs, including questions you didn't think to ask in advance. Monitoring is something you do: watching for problems you predicted. As systems grow, unknown unknowns dominate, so you need observability, not just more dashboards.

Beware the "three pillars" (logs, metrics, traces) pitch. It reduces a property to a shopping list, the lines between them blur (a metric can come from logs), and it can be a way to sell three tools. Think instead about what outputs you need, and treat them all as events you can slice and dice.

3

Log aggregation and correlation IDs

The one tool the author says to adopt before any microservice goes live. Agents forward logs from every instance to one queryable place. Use a common format, and put a correlation ID in every line, generated at the entry point and passed along on every call. Search for one.

Don't trust the order of log linesEach machine's clock drifts (clock skew), and timestamps are taken locally. Log order across services is approximate at best. Use distributed tracing for accurate timing and causality. Also, logs grow huge and may contain sensitive data: keep only what you need.
4

Metrics, and the cardinality trap

Is 20% more CPU since lunch a problem? You only know if you know what normal looks like, so collect metrics over time and aggregate them across instances (and drill down too). Cardinality is the number of distinct label combinations; each is a separate time series. Tools built for low cardinality (Prometheus being popular) struggle if you add labels like customer ID; newer tools (Honeycomb, Lightstep) handle high cardinality and let you ask much richer questions.

distinct time series

Monitoring systems are production systemsTreat them with the same care: they need to stay up when everything else is breaking, and they can be an attack vector (the SolarWinds breach, discovered while the book was being written, came through network management software).
5

Distributed tracing

Tracing tools record spans: a unit of work with a start, an end, metadata, and a link to its parent span. Collected spans assemble into a trace of one request across services, with real timings. Large systems sample to keep overhead down (Dapper sampled heavily; smarter tools sample selectively). OpenTelemetry is the emerging standard API; Jaeger is a popular open source collector.

6

Are we doing OK? SLA, SLO, SLI, error budgets

"Up" or "down" stops meaning much in a big system; some part is always degraded. So define what good looks like from the user's point of view. An SLA is the agreement with users (often with penalties). Teams commit to SLOs that together should meet it. SLIs are the measurements that tell you whether you're meeting them. An error budget is the failure the SLO allows. It's breathing room for risky changes.

7

Alert on what matters

Google once left server hard drives unscrewed: drives failed so often it was easier to rip them out and swap them. Expecting failure changes what is worth waking someone for. Too many alerts are dangerous: alarm floods were a factor at Three Mile Island and in the Boeing 737 MAX accidents. The book borrows guidance from the Engineering Equipment and Materials Users Association (via Steven Shorrock): a good alert is...

Page someone at 3 a.m.?

8

Semantic monitoring and testing in production

Instead of alerting on every low-level error, define what a correctly working system looks like and check that: "new customers can register; we sell at least $20,000 an hour at peak; orders ship at a normal rate." Agreeing that model is the hard part, and it needs product people, not just engineers. Then check it with real user monitoring (actual traffic: noisy and often late) or synthetic transactions (fake users on a schedule). The author first did this in 2005, injecting fake pricing events and alerting if no repricing appeared in time.

Mark test data clearly and avoid side effects. One ecommerce company ran its tests against production ordering by mistake and found out when a pile of washing machines arrived at head office. Other in-production checks: A/B tests, canaries, parallel runs, smoke tests, chaos engineering.

9

Standardize, choose tools well, start simple

Standardize log formats and metric naming (make the right thing easy). When choosing tools, look for: democratic (usable by everyone, not just experts), easy to integrate (open standards like OpenTelemetry), context (compared to when? depends on what? who is affected?), real-time, and suitable for your scale (and cost). And be skeptical of AI that promises to replace the expert in the loop.
