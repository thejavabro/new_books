Microservice Communication Styles

Calls between processes are not like calls within one. Before choosing technology, choose a style: blocking or nonblocking, request-response or event-driven.

1

A network call is not a method call

Many communication problems start when people pick a technology before thinking about what kind of communication they need. And many start by pretending a remote call is just a local one. It isn't, in three ways.

Tanenbaum and van Steen list five failure modes for distributed systems. Which one is each story?

Name that failure

Rich error semantics helpHTTP separates 4xx (the request was bad: don't retry as is) from 5xx (the server had a problem: retrying may help, as with 503 or 504). Whatever protocol you use, give clients enough to choose the right reaction.
2

A model of communication styles

Choose the style first, then the technology. The author's model splits communication into synchronous blocking and asynchronous nonblocking, and async further into request-response, event-driven and common data. Request-response can also be synchronous. Real systems mix them, and that's normal.

3

Synchronous blocking and the chain problem

Familiar and simple: call, wait, get an answer. The catch is temporal coupling: caller and callee must both be up, and the caller waits as long as the slowest link. Short and simple interactions are fine. Long chains are risky. Break the chain to see.

4

Asynchronous nonblocking

The caller sends and carries on. Caller and callee are no longer temporally coupled, and long-running work (packaging an order can take days) fits naturally. The cost: more complexity and more choice, and a mental model many developers are less used to.

Async/await can still blockLanguage features such as await let you work with asynchronous APIs, but if the next line waits for the result, the interaction is still synchronous and blocking from your code's point of view.
// asynchronous API, blocking use
const rate = await fetchRate('EUR','GBP');
convert(order, rate);   // waits for rate

// truly nonblocking: react later
rates.on('updated', r => reprice(r));
carryOn();
5

Communication through common data

One service writes to a shared place (a file, a data lake, a warehouse); others read it later. It's the most common integration pattern and often goes unnoticed as one. It works with almost any technology, even old mainframes that can read files, and it's great for large volumes. Downsides: consumers must poll or be told, there's latency, and it's a form of common coupling, so the data's structure becomes a contract. Keep data flowing one way.

6

Request-response, in series or in parallel

Ask a service to do something and expect a reply, success or failure. Good when you need the result before continuing. "Request" rather than "command": the receiver can always say no. Asynchronous request-response needs the reply routed back (say, a response queue) and matched to the original request, plus time-outs. When several independent calls are needed, run them in parallel. 7

Event-driven: state the fact, let others decide

An event is a statement that something happened inside the emitting service. The emitter doesn't know or care who listens; responsibility for what happens next moves to the receivers, much as decisions move into autonomous teams. An event is the content; a message is how you send it (via a broker such as RabbitMQ, or HTTP feeds like Atom). Brokers do this well, but keep them "dumb pipes": keep business logic in the services.

8

Proceed with caution: the poison message

A story from the author's time building a bank pricing system: workers pulled pricing jobs from a queue. A bug made one type of request crash its worker. The transaction rolled back, the job went back on the queue, and the next worker picked it up and died too. A catastrophic failover. Watch it, then apply the fixes.

Also invest in observability: tracing the flow of async messages via correlation IDs (Chapter 10) helps when nothing is waiting on a reply.
