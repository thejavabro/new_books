1

What a database transaction gave us

A transaction makes several changes into one unit that either all happens or none does. Relational databases offer ACID transactions. Inside one microservice, with one database, you still have all four. Spread a change across two services and two databases, and you lose one.

2

Distributed transactions: just say no

The usual attempt to get atomicity back is a two-phase commit. A coordinator asks every worker whether it can make its change (voting), and if all say yes, tells them all to commit. Step through, then try a failure.

Why the author says avoid itCommits don't happen at exactly the same moment, so others can see half-done states. Workers hold locks while waiting, which kills throughput and invites contention. Failure modes (a worker that votes yes then vanishes, a coordinator that dies) are many and need manual fixing. More participants and more latency make it worse. Alternatives: don't split that data at all, or use a saga. (Google's Spanner shows what it takes to do this at scale; it isn't a model to copy casually.)
3

Sagas: a sequence of local transactions

From Garcia-Molina and Salem's 1987 paper on long-lived transactions: break a long operation into steps, each its own local transaction. Nothing is locked for the whole duration. The price: no atomicity. Each step commits on its own, so you must handle failure explicitly, either by failing backward (undo with compensating transactions) or failing forward (retry or pick up from where it stopped). Sagas handle business failures; technical failures such as a 500 need ordinary handling such as retries.

MusicCorp's order fulfillment. Pick the step that fails, choose the step order, and run it.

Semantic rollbackA compensating transaction is a new action, not an erasure. You can't unsend an email; you send another saying the order was canceled. Records of what happened and why may be worth keeping. Reordering steps so that awkward-to-undo ones happen late (award points only after dispatch) removes many compensations entirely.
4

Orchestrated or choreographed?

Two ways to implement a saga. Run each and watch who talks to whom.

Beware BPM toolsBusiness process modeling tools promise that nondevelopers will draw the process. In the author's experience developers end up doing it anyway, with tools that don't fit their practices (version control, testing). Let developers use what they know; developer-friendly options such as Camunda or Zeebe get mixed reviews.
Which?One team owns the whole saga: orchestration is fine, the coupling stays inside the team. Several teams involved: prefer choreography, which spreads the work and lets teams act independently. Mixing is allowed, as long as you can still tell what state a saga is in.
