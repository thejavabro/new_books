Testing

Tests trade confidence for speed and isolation. In a microservice world, large end-to-end suites hurt; consumer-driven contracts and production testing help.

Types of tests: Marick's quadrant

Brian Marick's testing quadrant (popularized by Crispin and Gregory's Agile Testing) sorts tests by whether they face the business or the technology, and whether they support the team while programming or critique the finished product. Moving to microservices changes little about the quadrant; what changes is how we do the automated tests, and the growing role of testing in production. Click a quadrant.

2

Test scope: the pyramid

Mike Cohn's test pyramid: the higher you go, the bigger the scope and the more confidence a passing test gives, but the slower the feedback and the harder it is to locate a failure. The author uses unit, service (one microservice, collaborators stubbed) and end-to-end (the whole system). He avoids "integration test", which means too many things. One monolith he worked on had 4,000 unit, 1,000 service and 60 end-to-end tests. Shape your own suite and see what you get.

3

Service tests: stub or mock?

A service test runs one microservice for real and replaces its downstream collaborators. A stub answers canned responses and doesn't care how often it's called. A mock also verifies the call was made, and fails the test if not. Mocks are handy for checking side effects (did we award loyalty points?) but make tests brittle if overused; the author mostly stubs. Tools: roll your own, or mountebank, a programmable "software appliance" that creates imposters over HTTP (it doesn't do messaging protocols).

Why end-to-end tests hurt with microservices

To test the Customer service end to end you must deploy it with everything it touches. Which versions of the others? Prod's, or the newest? Teams often "fan in" several pipelines to one shared end-to-end stage. Then the problems start. Open each.

5

Consumer-driven contracts

Schemas catch structural breaks but not semantic ones. Contract tests let each consumer write down what it expects from a producer. Run them against the producer, with its own dependencies stubbed, and you get end-to-end-style confidence at service-test speed. When producers run their consumers' tests, these become consumer-driven contracts. Step through how Pact does it.

It's about conversationsLike a user story, a CDC is a placeholder for a conversation. They work where consumer and producer teams communicate and trust each other. A broken contract means talk, not just fix. Pact began at realestate.com.au; the Pact Broker stores contracts and shows which versions are compatible. Spring Cloud Contract is an alternative.
6

Should you avoid end-to-end tests?

Treat end-to-end tests before production as training wheels. With few services and one team they're manageable. They are a safety net while you learn contract testing and improve production monitoring and deployment. As those improve, and as end-to-end tests get costlier with more services and teams, rely on them less, until you may not need them at all.

Developer experienceEventually a developer can't run all the services they need on one laptop. Options: cloud-hosted dev environments (slower feedback loops) or stubbing the services you don't own. The author prefers keeping things local where possible.
Independent testabilityAccelerate found high performers do most testing on demand without an integrated environment. Shared test environments also bring contention.
7

Testing in production, safely

Preproduction testing works from a model of production, and models are imperfect. Distributed systems are too complex to catch everything beforehand, so test in production too: health pings, smoke tests at deploy time, canary releases, synthetic user transactions, chaos engineering. Make sure such tests can't harm real users or data.

Where should your next hour go?

More preproduction tests reduce how often things break (mean time between failures). Better rollbacks, monitoring and deployment reduce how long breaks last (mean time to repair). Shift the effort and see the effect on total downtime.

8

Cross-functional testing

The author prefers cross-functional requirements (CFRs) to "nonfunctional": latency, load, accessibility, security, data durability. Many can only truly be checked in production, but test strategies help. Think about them early, and per service: a payment service may need stricter latency than recommendations. CFR tests follow the pyramid too.
