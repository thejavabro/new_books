1

Agents that see and hear

A text-only agent misses much of the world: the screenshot of the UI it is building, the web page it is browsing, your spoken request. Multi-modal LLMs (MLLMs) process inputs beyond text (images, audio, video) and some can also generate them. Most focus on understanding several modalities and answer in text. Building one takes three parts plus an optional fourth: an encoder per modality turns raw input into embeddings; a connector reshapes those embeddings into what the LLM expects; the LLM reasons over everything; a generator (optional) produces non-text output. Pick an input.

2

Images as tokens: the Vision Transformer

An LLM's tokenizer and embedding layer already act as a text encoder. The Vision Transformer (ViT) copies the idea for images: cut the image into fixed-size patches, flatten and embed each one, add position information, and run them through a Transformer encoder. Patches play the role of tokens. Change the patch size and watch the token count.

image tokens for the LLM to read

3

CLIP: teach images and text to share a space

An image encoder is only useful to an LLM if its embeddings mean something in language terms. CLIP (Contrastive Language-Image Pre-training) trains an image encoder and a text encoder together on image–caption pairs. In each batch, the similarity of every image with every caption forms a grid; training pulls matching pairs (the diagonal) together and pushes the rest apart. Play the training step: click the right caption for each image.

4

Audio encoders

Sound gets the same treatment: chop it into pieces, embed them, run a Transformer. Three encoders the chapter covers, each with a different training trick.

5

Video: space and time

Video adds time to the two dimensions of an image (and often brings audio and subtitles along). Treat each frame as an image and average them, and you lose the order of events. Better options: ViViT embeds tubelets, patches that extend across several frames, and can factorise into a spatial encoder followed by a temporal one; TimeSformer uses divided space-time attention, attending across time first, then across space, in each block. Or skip video encoders altogether: run CLIP on frames and let the LLM read them as a sequence, much like text tokens; VideoMamba adds spatial and temporal positional embeddings to keep order.

Assumes 196 patches per frame (16-pixel patches on 224 × 224). Token counts explode fast, which is why video needs compression.

6

ImageBind: one space for six modalities

Separate encoders produce embeddings of different sizes and distributions that cannot be compared. ImageBind builds one shared space for many modalities using a simple insight: images co-occur with everything. A beach photo goes with the sound of waves, depth, text. Training only image–X pairs (image–text, image–audio, image–depth, image–thermal, image–motion) binds every modality to images, and through them to each other. Audio and text end up aligned without ever being trained together: emergent alignment.

7

Connectors: getting embeddings into the LLM

Even good encoders output vectors of the wrong count, size and distribution for the LLM. The connector fixes that, and is always trained. Three families.

Flamingo's gate

New cross-attention layers are multiplied by tanh(α), with α starting at 0, so at first they add nothing and the pretrained LLM behaves exactly as before. Training gradually opens the gate.

8

TinyAgent learns to look

Gemma 4 E4B, the book's native model, already ships a vision encoder and an audio encoder. Its encoders emit "image tokens" that sit in the chat template where the image goes. In the TinyAgent, a multi-modal memory lets a message carry an image as a URL or as base64 (different inference engines want different forms), and run accepts images alongside the task.
