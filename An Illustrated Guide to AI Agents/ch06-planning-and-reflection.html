Planning and Reflection

Planning breaks a goal into steps; reflection notices when a step went wrong and changes course. Together they turn an augmented LLM into an agent that runs in a loop.

Planning: the last core component

With a reasoning brain, memory and tools in place, one piece is missing: deciding what to do next, and in what order. Planning turns a query into structured steps, many of which name a tool to use. Its core move is task decomposition: break a big goal into subtasks small enough to solve one at a time. Try it: put these subtasks for "plan a weekend in Lisbon under €400" into a sensible order.

Tap the steps in the order you would do them.
2

Implicit and explicit planning

Chain of thought already decomposes a task, but implicitly, as a single linear trace. Self-consistency samples many such traces and votes; Tree of Thoughts branches at each step and searches the tree (beam search, Monte Carlo tree search) with a verifier choosing the best path. Explicit planning writes the plan down first. Least-to-most prompting lists simpler subquestions and solves them in turn, each answer feeding the next. Plan-and-solve asks the model to devise a plan and carry it out within one response.

3

Action sequencing with ReAct

A written plan is static. Agents need to act, see what happened, and decide again: action sequencing. ReAct (Reason + Act) fuses the two in a loop of THOUGHT, ACTION, OBSERVATION, repeated until the model writes an ANSWER. With a model that follows instructions well, you can get it by prompting alone. The book's final TinyAgent upgrade wraps its step in exactly this loop. Run the example: "Add 2.5 and 3.7, multiply by 4, then subtract 3.14."


  
Native reasoning models fill the same three slots without the text format: THOUGHT comes from the response's reasoning field, ACTION from its structured tool call, and the ANSWER from its content.






  
4
Training agents to act: FireAct and ETO

  
Prompted ReAct costs tokens and needs few-shot examples. FireAct fine-tunes on ReAct (and similar) trajectories generated by a strong model, keeping only those that reached the correct answer. Supervised fine-tuning teaches imitation, though, not exploration. Exploration-based Trajectory Optimization (ETO) adds RL: after SFT on expert trajectories in environments like ALFWorld (a text household: "clean a tomato and put it on the countertop"), the agent explores, its failed trajectories are paired with successful ones, and Direct Preference Optimization pushes it toward the winners. Repeat.

  






  
5
Reflection by prompting: Self-Refine

  
Reflection is the agent judging its own work and fixing it. Self-Refine uses one LLM in three roles: generate, give feedback, refine; repeat until a step limit or the model decides it is done. Iterate on a draft.

  






  
6
Reflexion: lessons kept in episodic memory

  
Reflexion splits the roles across three LLMs. An Actor acts (often with ReAct). An Evaluator scores the trajectory. A Self-Reflection model turns failure into a short verbal lesson, stored in episodic memory and fed into the next attempt. Feedback can come from the agent itself or its environment; CRITIC, for instance, checks its draft with external tools such as search. Run trials and watch lessons accumulate.

  

    

    

    

  






  
7
Self-improvement with RL: TTRL and R-Zero

  
Prompted reflection guides behaviour but does not change the weights. Labelled agent traces are scarce, so newer methods generate their own training signal. Test-Time RL samples many answers (16 at temperature 0.6 in the paper), takes the majority answer as a pseudo-label, rewards agreement with it, and updates with GRPO: learning during use, without labels. R-Zero co-evolves two copies of one base model. A Challenger writes questions; a frozen Solver answers each many times. The Challenger is rewarded most when the Solver agrees with itself about half the time: hard, but solvable. Drag the difficulty.

  

    
    

    

  

  
The rest of the loopA repetition penalty keeps the Challenger from recycling one hard question, and a format reward checks its question tags. Then roles swap: questions that are too easy or too hard for the Solver are filtered out, and the Solver is trained with GRPO on the rest using a simple 0/1 reward against the majority answer. Each side keeps raising the bar for the other.
