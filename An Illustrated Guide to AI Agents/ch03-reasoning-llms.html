Reasoning Large Language Models

Instead of only making models bigger, let them think longer. Reasoning LLMs spend extra tokens on a chain of thought before answering, and that changes what agents can plan and fix.

1

Think first, then answer

A regular LLM goes straight from question to answer. A reasoning LLM first writes out intermediate thoughts, a chain of thought, and only then answers. For agents this is the engine behind everything that follows: making a plan, choosing tools, checking results and reflecting on mistakes all need several steps of explicit thinking.

2

Train-time scaling hits diminishing returns

Scaling laws describe how performance improves as compute, data and parameters grow. They are power laws: each doubling buys less than the last. On normal axes the curve flattens; on log-log axes it becomes a straight line. Kaplan's laws said grow model size; Chinchilla showed many models were undertrained and that, for a fixed budget, a smaller model on more data wins. By 2024 the gains from scaling training alone were slowing.

3

Trading train-time for test-time compute

Every generated token costs compute. Test-time compute means spending more of it at inference: thinking longer, or generating many answers and picking one. OpenAI reported that more thinking time scales much like more RL training. A study of AlphaZero playing the board game Hex found the two budgets interchangeable: to hold a given rating, cut one and you must raise the other. Move the sliders.

illustrative strength score

Diagonal lines join budgets that reach the same strength. The cheapest way up usually raises both.

4

Two ways to spend test-time compute

The chapter sorts methods into two families. Search against verifiers is output-focused: sample many traces or answers, score them with a verifier, keep the best. Modifying the proposal distribution is input-focused: change the model (by prompting or training) so the tokens it proposes already contain better reasoning. Verifiers, or reward models, come in two kinds. Toggle between them.

5

Prompting a model into reasoning

The simplest proposal change costs nothing: ask for it. Adding "Let's think step by step" (zero-shot chain of thought) or including one worked example (one-shot) or several (few-shot) nudges even a non-reasoning model to write its steps. The book shows this on a puzzle that a small model gets wrong with a bare answer and right once it reasons.


  






  
6
Self-consistency and Best-of-N

  
Self-consistency samples many reasoning traces at a high temperature and takes the majority answer. No verifier model needed. Best-of-N instead scores each candidate with a verifier and keeps the top one; the verifier can be an ORM, a PRM, or plain code such as unit tests. Weighted Best-of-N sums verifier scores across identical answers. Simulate a model that is right only part of the time.

7
Training reasoning in: from Flan-PaLM to s1

  
Changing the proposal distribution for real means training. Ranked differently, the same next-token probabilities can favour "First, let's…" over blurting an answer. Flan-PaLM mixed chain-of-thought examples into instruction tuning. s1 showed you need surprisingly little: 1,000 questions with reasoning traces were enough to make Qwen2.5-32B reason, with special tokens marking a think phase and an answer phase. It also controlled thinking length at test time: cut thinking off early, or append "Wait" when the model tries to stop, so it keeps going. More thinking, better scores.

  

    
    

  






  
8
DeepSeek-R1: reasoning learned by reward

  
DeepSeek-R1-Zero skipped supervised data entirely. Starting from DeepSeek-V3-Base, a system prompt asked for <think> and <answer> tags, and GRPO rewarded just two things: correct format, correct answer. How to reason was left to the model, and it discovered on its own that longer reasoning pays. But with this cold start it mixed languages and wrote hard-to-read traces. DeepSeek-R1 fixed that in five steps.

  

  
NoticeSteps 1 to 3 exist only to manufacture good training data. The final model is the base model, fine-tuned on that data and then refined with RL.






  
9
Native reasoning and the thinking switch

  
Modern open models ship with reasoning built in and a way to turn it on or off. In the TinyAgent chapter, Gemma 4 enables thinking with a special token in the system turn, and the returned Response separates the reasoning field from the content. Qwen3 popularised hybrid reasoning with /think and /no_think switches, trained with and without chain-of-thought data.

  

    

    

  






  
10
Shorter thoughts: efficient reasoning

  
Left unchecked, RL can grow traces far beyond need, and every token costs money and time. Remedies: Chain-of-Draft prompts keep each step to about five words; length rewards (Kimi k1.5, O1-Pruner) favour short correct answers; L1 is told a token budget up front and rewarded for matching it. Pick an answer type to see how a typical length-aware reward treats it.

11
Reasoning without words

  
Explicit chain of thought is like hearing someone think aloud. Latent reasoning keeps the thinking inside. In normal decoding the last hidden state is squeezed into one token, which is embedded again. Chain of Continuous Thought skips that bottleneck and feeds the hidden state straight back in, between <bot> and <eot> markers. CODI trains a student to reason this way by distilling from a teacher that reasons explicitly. Faster and less constrained, but you lose the readable trace that makes debugging possible.

  

    

    

  

  
Multi-modal models reason too: methods such as LLaVA-CoT generate step-by-step data for vision-language models, and Reason-RFT follows SFT with RL using format and accuracy rewards, the same recipe as text.
