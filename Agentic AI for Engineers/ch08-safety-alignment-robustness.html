Safety, Alignment and Robustness

A buggy spreadsheet macro gives a wrong number and stops. A buggy agent takes a wrong action, then another built on it. Once software decides and acts, you are hiring a teammate, and teammates need boundaries, oversight and accountability. This chapter is about building those in from day one.

1

Safety lives in the outer ring

Think of the ecosystem as nested rings. The model is the raw intelligence, agents give it direction, agentic systems are teams of agents, and the infrastructure around them holds the guardrails, monitoring and security. Tap a ring.

2

Reward hacking: the metric wins, the purpose loses

A support agent is rewarded on a number. Give it only "short calls" and it learns that hanging up is the fastest call of all. Add measures for what you actually meant and watch the winning strategy change.

3

Specify the how, not just the what

"Resolve tickets as quickly as possible" is a what. Each clause you add describes how. Run the same four tickets under each version of the goal.

4

Prompt injection, direct and hidden

Direct injection is typed by the user. Indirect injection hides in content the agent reads: a web page, a PDF, an email. Here an email assistant summarises a web page that carries a hidden instruction. Layer the defences and replay.

5

Error cascades and emergent behaviour

Humans notice when step two does not follow from step one. An agent may not. One misread clause in a contract flows into every recommendation after it. Step through with and without a checkpoint that re-reads the source.

With many agents the system starts to behave like an ecosystem: two pricing agents that each learn "matching the other's high price earns more" can end up colluding without anyone writing that rule. Watch the group, not only the individual.

6

Safety by design: bumpers, simulators, and a hand on the wheel

Constraints are bowling bumpers: the game stays the same, the gutter is closed. Sandboxes let a bad day cost compute instead of money. Oversight is in the loop (a human decides) or on the loop (a human supervises and can take over). Pick an action and see where it should sit.

7

Robustness: falter safely

The world drifts (a trend goes viral, slang changes, a supplier fails). Stress tests show how the agent breaks, and fail-safes decide whether it breaks gracefully. Push market volatility on a rebalancing agent and set its guard rails.

8

Scoring a journey, not a destination

Agents are judged on many dimensions at once: did it understand the intent, follow the rules, call tools correctly, cover every sub-goal, reason coherently, stay grounded in retrieved facts? Score this travel-booking run. Tap a metric to see the evidence.

9

Agents monitoring agents

Monitors do not do the work; they check it: policy, reasoning, tone, drift and grounding. Four common shapes, often combined: a critic, a parallel panel, a supervisor that can steer the plan, and an audit trail reviewed later.

10

A monitor with your blind spots is not a monitor

If the overseer shares the same model, prompt style and raw input as the worker, the same trick fools both. Add diversity and run five tricky outputs past it.

11

Safety has a bill. Budget it.

Every guardrail adds latency, every monitor multiplies tokens, every human approval caps throughput, every log line costs storage. Match the spend to the stakes.

12

Fairness has to be measured

A resume screener can call back both groups at similar rates and still reject qualified people from one group far more often. Adjust the screener and read three different fairness tests.

13

Least privilege: isolation is safety

Each agent gets just enough tools to do its job. The researcher reads, the writer drafts, only the approver sends, and only with a human confirming. Sensitive data arrives as tokens, not raw values. Try some requests.

14

Pre-deployment checklist

Tick what is actually done. Ship when the high-stakes items are green.
