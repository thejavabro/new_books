Preparing for the Interview and How Big Companies Hire

Long before the first coding question, the interview has started: with what you choose to learn, what you put online and how your resume reads in fifteen seconds. Then each big company runs its own multi-stage process.

1

The novice interview roadmap

The book opens with a simple premise: the best Java developers are passionate about the work, and passion becomes a profession only with a plan. That plan is a chain of checkpoints. You may be at any of them today; each one feeds the next. Tap a checkpoint to see what it asks of you, and tick it off when you feel you have it covered.

2

Know yourself, then know the market

Before searching for a job, figure out which kind of developer you want to be. You can probably do most Java tasks, but you will not enjoy all of them equally, and doing work you dislike does not last. The chapter frames this as ten questions. Answer them quickly below; the sketch on the right builds itself from your answers.

Your sketch

Knowing what you want is only half. The other half is what the market wants. Read the yearly Java ecosystem surveys (tooling vendors, publishers, community sites) and sort technologies into three columns. Look at social networks, new books and new courses as signals of interest, but be careful with anything that suddenly went mainstream: companies often adopt it years later, or never. Tap a technology to move it between columns.

The sorting here is an example of the exercise, not a verdict. Redo it every year with fresh surveys and salary data.

3

Get the right experience and shine online

No experience means no resume, and no resume means no interview. The fix is to start something, which means code: school projects, internships, volunteering, side projects. Then make it visible under your real name, with a real photo, and answer criticism politely. Recruiters will search for you before they call you.

Build your footprint

Four months on GitHub

What about certifications?The author's view: they cost a lot of money and time, job descriptions rarely require them, and they age quickly (a Java 6 certificate says little today). Time spent on real projects usually impresses employers more. If you want one anyway, the usual suspects are Oracle's Java programmer certifications, Spring Professional, cloud architect certifications and Scrum or project-management credentials.
4

A resume read in fifteen seconds

Resume screeners want two answers fast: are you a strong, smart coder, and do you fit this role. They spend roughly 10 to 20 seconds, so the resume should fit on one page (two if you have 10+ years). Press Scan to watch where a hurried screener's eyes land on a cluttered page versus a focused one.

Write every role as Achievement, Action, Effect

For each of up to four roles, lead with the achievement (it hooks the reader), then the action, then the measured effect. Numbers beat adjectives: "reduced the memory footprint by 5%" rather than "reduced the memory footprint". Build a line from the pieces:

5

Applying and the first interviews

Most jobs are filled through networking: surveys cited in the chapter put it at 70 to 85 percent. Beyond your network, use global job boards (LinkedIn, Indeed, Stack Overflow Jobs, freelance sites) and local ones. Before you submit, read the company site (you may be able to apply directly, skipping an agency), double-check your online profile, and tailor the resume to each job description.

Most IT companies begin with a phone screen: either a 15 to 30 minute non-technical call with HR, or a technical screen with questions, maybe a quiz and a coding exercise. In-person rounds follow. If you are turned down after a screen, read any feedback carefully and objectively.

6

What interviews at big companies look like

Big companies run long, multi-stage processes (a month or more) with questions that get harder as you go, usually including one non-technical "lunch" interview. Pick a company to replay its pipeline and see what it emphasizes. These are the chapter's 2020-era descriptions; always check current testimonials and "most asked questions" for the company you target.
