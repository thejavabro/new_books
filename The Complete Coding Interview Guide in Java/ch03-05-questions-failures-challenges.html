Non-Technical Questions, Failures and the Coding Challenge

The human part of the interview has no right answers, only sincere and well-prepared ones. Rejection is a mismatch to diagnose, not a verdict. And a coding challenge goes best when you follow the same seven steps every time, out loud.

1

What the non-technical questions are for

Usually run by a hiring manager or HR, this part of the interview measures fit: whether your experience, character and expectations match the team and the company. It also covers practical terms such as salary, relocation, schedule and overtime. Companies do reject candidates here even after a strong technical round. There are no right or wrong answers, only sincere ones, so prepare your own answers at home rather than memorizing someone else's.

2

Practice the common questions out loud

Pick a question, read what the interviewer is really probing for, then rehearse your answer against the clock. Most answers should land in one to two minutes.

3

The salary conversation

If money comes up at the start, your answer may decide whether the process continues, so try to postpone a number ("let's first see if my value meets your expectations") or give a researched range. Ideally it comes at the end, which means they want you. Then do not jump to numbers: ask for their range, bonuses and total package first. Set the numbers below to see which scenario you are in and the move the chapter suggests.

Rules of thumbDon't be shy about money. Don't open low; leave room to negotiate. Don't undersell yourself. Don't spend time on things that are not negotiable. If you accept a disappointing number, ask in writing for a review in six months and push for other benefits.
4

Offers, rejections and mismatches

Accepting is easy: confirm and agree start date and paperwork. Declining needs care, because people move between companies and contacts matter: thank them, say it is not the right choice right now, and call (or write to) anyone who made you an offer. When the answer is no, the chapter suggests a reframe: a company does not reject you; there is a mismatch between you and the role. Persistence or obsession?Retrying the same company over and over rarely helps. Keep your confidence: sometimes the company simply was not the right one for you, and later it may be the one asking.
5

Technical quizzes

Many technical interviews start with 20 to 40 quick questions, either asked by a person or taken on a platform. Answering well follows three steps: understand the question (ask if unsure), recall the keywords the interviewer is listening for, and wrap them in a short, confident answer that does not sound like a question.

Partly know it?Ask a narrowing question or for 20 seconds to think. A partial answer beats "I don't know".
No idea at all?Say "I don't know". Smothering the interviewer in jargon counts against you.
On a timed platform?Split time evenly (20 questions in 30 minutes is 1.5 min each). Skip and return if you can; guess if you cannot go back.
6

Coding challenge problems are meant to be difficult

The coding challenge is the climax of the interview, and a double-edged sword: great working code can cover earlier gaps; a poor showing can sink a good day. Tap each trait to see why these problems feel the way they do.

Six steps for any coding problem, with a live example

The interviewer is not only grading the final code. They watch how you plan, whether you communicate, and how you react to hints.
