Principles of Multi-Agent Systems

Principles are cheap to state and expensive to violate. Seven of them separate a bounded, observable, maintainable agent team from a fragile demo, and they pull against each other in ways you have to decide on purpose.

Seven principles, one dependency chain

The book's research assistant works like a small newsroom: a researcher chases sources, a fact-checker verifies each claim, a synthesizer writes the brief from what survives. Seven principles keep a team like that dependable, and they form a chain: each one creates the pressure the next resolves. Click a principle.

Underneath all sevenA multi-agent system is a resource-constrained distributed system first. Coordination, memory and autonomy are paid for in tokens, compute and wall-clock time.

Principle 1: Bounded autonomy

In systems design, autonomy is not "freedom to do anything". It is the ability to decide within a bounded scope, like a doctor within a license or a reporter within a beat. Too much freedom gives inconsistency; too little turns the agent into a slow workflow. The bound is written down as a five-component contract. Principle 2: Collective alignment

Every agent behaving rationally in its own scope does not add up to a coherent whole. Step through how three well-behaved agents produce an inconsistent report.

When agents disagree: pick a protocol

Don't import distributed-systems consensus wholesale: it assumes interchangeable replicas with independent failures. Agents are neither, and every trusted link between them is also an attack path.

4

Principle 3: Resilience against silent failure

A failed microservice is loud: it crashes, times out or returns an error. A failed agent is often silent: it returns a fluent, confident, well-formed answer that is wrong. Agents carry three internal models a service doesn't, and each can drift. The grid shows which mechanism protects which model; click a mechanism for details.

5

Principle 4: Learnability

Learnability means getting better after shipping, through a loop that is observed, evaluated and verified before any change reaches production. What learns is usually the system, not the model weights: memory, prompts, routing, tools. It needs somewhere to write the lesson and a number that defines "better". It's optional: a static, well-evaluated workflow is sometimes right.

Citation faithfulness, week by week

Cadence depends on the domain (minutes for trading, days for support). The fastest stable cadence wins, and SRE error budgets decide whether a cycle spends effort on change or on holding steady.

6

Principle 5: Modular composability

Treat each agent as a service with a published interface. But agent coupling is harder to contain: agents also couple through behavior, like an ordering or a tone the contract never promised. Split processes with hidden behavioral dependencies are a distributed monolith: all the cost of separation, none of the independence. Try the silent break.

7

Principle 6: Safety and trust by design

Safety is structural, designed before deployment, not a filter. One study of eighteen models used as agent brains found nearly all fell to direct prompt injection and every one could be compromised through trust between agents. Click the layers from the inside out.

Human-in-the-loop is a designed pathwayDefine triggers, recipients, approval workflow, timeout behavior and logging up front. Then guard against rubber-stamping: people tend to accept confident wrong answers ("falling asleep at the wheel"), so require a written rationale for high-stakes approvals and sample-audit what was approved.

Principle 7: Observability and evaluation

  
You can't debug what you can't see or improve what you can't evaluate. Logs, metrics and traces are signals; a MAS needs layers above them. Click a tier.

  

    

      

      

    

  

  

    
Why a decision trace pays for itself

    
The synthesizer drops a load-bearing claim and escalates it. Without a trace: did the fact-checker reject it, the retrieval miss it, or a threshold change? With one, the answer is a single record:


decision: escalate_to_user
reason:   confidence_below_threshold
state:    { confidence: 0.43, threshold: 0.60, claim_type: "named_entity" }
# root cause in seconds: the named-entity threshold is too strict
# for one publisher class that always carries lower attribution confidence

Principles in tension

  
You can't max out all seven. Every real architecture is a negotiated settlement: make the trade-off explicit, write it down, and revisit it. Drag each pair toward the side your domain needs.

Recap on one page

  

    
1 Bounded autonomyRole, goal, observable state, decision scope, feedback loop, plus escalation triggers.

    
2 Collective alignmentExplicit sharing, handoffs, review and conflict protocols. Coherence doesn't emerge on its own.

    
3 ResilienceTask, peer and self models drift silently. Independent and multi-level verification, trust weighting, containment, recovery.

    
4 LearnabilityObserve, evaluate, evolve, verify. The system learns in memory and wiring, gated by evals.

    
5 Modular composabilityContracts that pin behavior, not just schema. Avoid the distributed monolith.

    
6 Safety and trustLeast privilege, untrusted by default, blast-radius containment, HITL that resists rubber-stamping.

    
7 Observability and evaluationDecision traces, cross-agent correlation, insights and evals tied to release gates.
