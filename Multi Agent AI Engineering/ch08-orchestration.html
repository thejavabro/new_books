Orchestrating Agent Teams

Orchestration is the layer that decides who works on what, in which order, and what happens when something fails. Done well, it survives its own crashes and carries work past a single context window.

1

Orchestration: the difference between a group and a team

Agents can talk (chapter 5), organize (chapter 6) and remember (chapter 7). Orchestration is the control logic that decides who does what, in what order, how results combine, and what happens when things go wrong. Patterns say what should happen; orchestration handles what does happen when one specialist answers in 200 ms, one in 30 s, and one never. The orchestrator has five responsibilities, and they double as a diagnostic map.

Which responsibility failed?

Brooks's observation applies: if coordination lives inside every agent, each new agent must know all the others and coupling grows combinatorially. A separate layer keeps agents reusable, workflows changeable, and failures handled in one place.

2

Centralized to decentralized: a dial, not a switch

Control can sit in one place, spread across peers, or anywhere between. Most production systems land in the middle. Turn the dial.

3

The planner is a failure domain

An LLM planner can return malformed JSON, invent an agent that doesn't exist, or emit a cycle. Validate every plan (schema, capability, acyclicity) and keep an always-runnable template plan as a fallback. Better still, re-plan when progress stalls: keep a task ledger (facts, guesses, plan) and a progress ledger (is the work advancing?), and trigger a re-plan when completed steps stop increasing for a few turns.

4

Sequential, fan-out, and DAG flows

Once control has a home, choose execution order. Sequential pays the sum of stages; fan-out pays the max; a DAG pays the critical path. Set stage times for a content workflow: outline, then two section drafts and image research in parallel, then review, then publish.

Fan-out needs a global timeout, or one stuck agent holds the whole fan-in. Scheduling adds priority (with aging so low-priority work doesn't starve), concurrency caps and cost budgets on top of the flow.

5

Why reliability is the orchestrator's problem

Chain stages that each succeed 95% of the time and, if failures are independent, the workflow succeeds far less often. Correlation can push either way: shared upstream failures, or models agreeing on the same wrong answer.

The answer is a stack of mostly old ideas from payments and distributed systems, plus one that's new for agents: verification. Click a layer.

Classify before you retryRetries assume the call was right and the world was wrong. When the call is wrong (a tool not in the registry, arguments that fail the schema, a handoff to an agent that doesn't exist), re-running the same call just fails again. Repair the call instead. Auth errors and similar are non-retryable; fail fast.
6

Retries with backoff and jitter

Naive retries hammer an overloaded dependency at the worst moment. Exponential backoff spaces attempts out; jitter keeps a crowd of clients from retrying in lockstep.

7

Circuit breakers stop retry storms

Retries without breakers are an anti-pattern: they turn one dependency's outage into a pile-on. A breaker tracks the failure rate and moves between three states. Break the dependency and send calls.

Compose with care: the breaker wraps the agent call, and the retry wraps the breaker, so an open breaker fails fast rather than being retried. Express the threshold as a rate over a window, not a bare count.

8

Graceful degradation and durable state

Mark tasks required or optional. In a research workflow, research is required; fact-checking and formatting improve the result but aren't fatal. Then make the orchestrator's own state durable, so its crash is just another recoverable failure.

On resume, a task that was RUNNING at the crash re-runs only if it's idempotent or carries an idempotency key; otherwise it needs a human. Production teams usually adopt a durable-execution engine (event-sourced code workflows, declarative state machines, scheduled DAGs, asset graphs) matched to the shape of the work. Checkpoints also let a run pause for human approval before a side effect.

9

Work longer than one session

Models finish long tasks at some success rate on a good day, but the length they finish dependably is several times shorter. So long work is split into sessions, each ending in a written handoff to a successor that wasn't there: a specification of "done", a progress ledger, and a resume command.

10

Error budgets drive adaptive orchestration

Treat latency, cost and success-rate targets as budgets to spend. The orchestrator, not the agents, must hold the budget: agents are poor at tracking their own spend. When any budget runs low, degrade on purpose: skip optional work, switch to a cheaper model, cut retries.

Across runs, orchestrators can also learn: keep successful trajectories, extract reusable workflows, and retrieve them for similar tasks. Selective retention and evaluation matter, as with memory consolidation.
