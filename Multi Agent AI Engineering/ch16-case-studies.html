Four real systems, five questions

This chapter reads four deployed systems through the same five questions. Three of the cases are written by guest authors who built them. The authors are careful about evidence: company-reported numbers stay labelled as reported, and a benchmark result is never presented as a production result.

1. AuthorityWhich component may act without asking, and what bounds it?
2. EvidenceWhat does each stage hand the next, and in what form?
3. ContainmentWhat stops a wrong action reaching the systems it would change?
4. BottleneckWhich boundary or resource does everything wait on?
5. EvaluationWhich measure can hold, roll back or escalate a change, and which only fills a dashboard?

Pick a case and a question. The answers are my condensed reading of each case.

2

CAIPE: when to retire a boundary

CAIPE is an open-source agentic internal developer platform from Cisco Outshift (guest author Sri Aradhyula). Its arc is the most instructive part: it grew into a supervisor-and-specialists design, then removed it. Step through the releases.

Fail closed. In platform engineering "which agent may do what" is the safety question, so the answer moved out of agent processes into a gateway that consults a relationship-based authorisation service before every tool call. If the authoriser is unreachable, the call is refused.

Reported production use falls into five categories: knowledge retrieval, self-service "golden paths" (such as deploying through ArgoCD), troubleshooting, service-desk tickets, and incident handling that ends in a proposed rollback or pull request behind approvals. The authors stress that the figures are vendor-reported and directional.

3

Denario: when the filter is the product

Denario (guest author Boris Bolliet) takes a dataset and a problem description to a typeset paper. Each stage is a small multi-agent subsystem, and stages talk through files rather than a message bus, so a scientist can overwrite any stage with their own work.

input.md→idea.md→literature.md→methods.md→results.md + plots→paper.pdf→referee.md

A run costs around 30 minutes and $4, and experts judged the drafts comparable to a good student's work over weeks to months. The economics follow: drafts are cheap, but reading one closely costs expert hours. Try it.

The lesson: when generation is cheap and yield is low, the ranking filter is the product. Generating faster only lengthens the queue in front of the expert. A critic decides nothing until a program can read its verdict.

4

Nvidia's hardware agents: scaffold beats model

Guest author Chia-Tung (Mark) Ho describes a series of hardware-design agents built on one framework (Marco) with three knobs: the task-graph topology, the custom tools, and the agent arrangement. Each new agent answered a gap the previous one exposed. It started with RTLFixer, one ReAct agent fixing Verilog syntax against the compiler with a small retrieval database of expert guidance.

AgentSub-taskShapeWhat grounds it
RTLFixerFix Verilog syntaxSingle agentCompiler errors + retrieval of expert fixes
VerilogCoderWrite RTL from a specDynamic multi-agentTask graph, syntax tree, waveform tracing
FVDebugFormal-verification failure to fixPipelineCounter-examples from the formal tool
Others in the seriesTiming, cell layout, sub-task planningSingle to hierarchicalSimulators, checkers, netlists

The striking finding: in every paper of the series that tried two models, the weaker model with the scaffold beat the stronger model without it. The tools (simulators, checkers) are the authority. The model proposes, the tool decides. And these are the only agents in the chapter where every evaluator is wired to a decision: whether the debug loop iterates, which patches survive.

RTLFixer's reported jump on its syntax-fix benchmark was from 58.7% to 98.5%. Each row of the book's table has its own baseline, so deltas are not comparable across rows.

5

Airbnb support: one owner per clock

A support conversation runs on several clocks at once: the guest wants an answer this turn, the reservation can change mid-conversation, policy changes over months, and models and evals age on their own schedules. Airbnb's design gives each clock a deterministic owner, and the model works inside that.

Representation beat reasoning. Policies were rewritten as Intent-Context-Action pseudocode. The model outputs only an action identifier, and the system looks up the content. That shortens output, makes evaluation an exact match, and in their experiments the structured form without chain-of-thought beat chain-of-thought on the rich-text version.

Company-reported results include a 15% drop in users needing a human agent after the 2025 US rollout, and by 2026 nearly 45% of issues resolved without a human in over 50 languages. A resolution share is a single-trial number. On τ-bench retail, one model solved about 61% of tasks once but under 25% in all eight trials.

Recap

  • Read any deployment through authority, evidence, containment, bottleneck and evaluation.
  • CAIPE: retire a boundary when its reason expires; put policy in a gateway that fails closed.
  • Denario: when drafts are cheap and yield is low, ranking is the product.
  • Nvidia VLSI: grounding tools and scaffolds moved results more than stronger models.
  • Airbnb: deterministic owners for each clock; representation can beat reasoning.
  • Across all cases, evaluation is the weakest link. Write down what decision each measure controls.
