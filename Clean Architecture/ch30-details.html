The Database, the Web and Frameworks Are Details

Data matters; the database doesn't. The web is another GUI. A framework is a partner that asks for marriage. Treat all three as plugins to your business rules.

DatabaseThe webFrameworksQuick check
1

The database is a detail

Architecturally, the database is about as significant as a doorknob is to a house. Careful: the data model, the structure of your application's data, is highly significant. The database, the software and technology that moves data on and off storage, is a utility, a low-level mechanism. Codd's relational model is elegant and robust, but knowing the tabular structure of data should be restricted to the outermost circle. Passing rows around the system as objects is an architectural error.

Why did databases become so dominant? Disks. Rotating platters are agonizingly slow compared to processors, so we needed indexes, caches and query optimizers: file systems for documents, relational systems for content-based search. Slide through storage history and see what happens when the disk goes away.

Once data lives in RAM, how will you organize it: tables? No, you'll use linked lists, trees, hash tables, queues, and access them with pointers and references, which is exactly what your program does once it has loaded data from a database anyway. The database is just a big bucket of bits; the shape of the data on a rotating disk is a detail you can hide behind an interface.

But what about performance?Performance is a real concern, but it can be encapsulated entirely in the low-level data access layer. It's not a reason to let the database shape your business rules.
2

The web is a detail

In the '90s many believed the web changed everything. Look further back and you see a pendulum: computing power swings between the center and the edge every decade or so. Press play.

The swings will keep happening, so the web is just another GUI, an I/O device, and GUIs change on marketing's whim. The book tells of a personal finance company that turned its desktop app into a web-like one, hated by users, then turned it back; and of a phone maker whose OS upgrade restyled every app without app developers noticing, because their apps kept the look and feel at arm's length. A browser-to-app "dance" is chatty and rich, so full device independence is hard, but there is another boundary: each use case takes input data, processes it, and returns output data. That boundary can be abstracted, even if it takes effort.

3

Frameworks are details

Framework authors are often generous and the tools are powerful. But the relationship is asymmetric. You make a huge commitment; they make none to you. Their docs and examples steer you to wrap your architecture around the framework, derive from its base classes, annotate everything. Read the vows.

You promise
  • to derive your business objects from its base classes
  • to scatter its annotations across your entities
  • to follow its upgrades, wanted or not
  • to shape your folders like its examples
It promises
  • nothing

The risks are real. Press the button.

The solution: don't marry it. Use it, but keep it at arm's length, treated as a detail in one of the outer circles. If it wants your business objects to derive from its base classes, derive proxies instead and keep them in plugin components. Don't let it into your core code. Put the wiring in Main, the dirtiest component, which is allowed to know about everything.


  

  
Some marriages are unavoidable: in C++ you'll likely marry the standard library; in Java, the standard library too. That should be a deliberate decision, not something that happens by default. Try not to marry a framework right away; maybe you can get the milk without buying the cow.
