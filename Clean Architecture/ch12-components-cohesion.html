Components and Component Cohesion

Components are the units you deploy. Three principles decide which classes go together, and they pull against each other, so the right balance changes as a project matures.

Components: the units of deployment

If SOLID arranges bricks into walls and rooms, the component principles arrange rooms into buildings. A component is the smallest thing you can deploy: a jar in Java, a gem in Ruby, a DLL in .NET, an aggregation of source files in an interpreted language. Components can be linked into one executable, bundled into an archive, or deployed separately as dynamically loaded plugins. However they're deployed, well-designed components keep the ability to be deployed independently.

Today's easy plugin model took decades to reach. Step through the history, watching memory.

Programs will grow to fill all available compile and link time.

The author's tongue-in-cheek "Murphy's law of program size". Moore's law eventually won the race: by the late '90s linking was fast enough to do at load time again, and component plugin architectures became the casual default.

REP: the granule of reuse is the granule of release

The Reuse/Release Equivalence Principle. People can only reuse a component that is released through a proper process: version numbers, release notes, so users can decide when and whether to integrate a new release. From the design side, the classes in a component should form a cohesive group with an overarching theme, something that makes sense to release together. If they don't, your users will notice. CCP: gather what changes together

The Common Closure Principle is the SRP restated for components: gather classes that change for the same reasons and at the same times; separate those that change at different times or for different reasons. For most applications maintainability beats reusability, so we'd rather a change land in one component than spread across many. It also echoes the OCP: since full closure is impossible, close components strategically against the kinds of change you expect. Below, the same eight classes are packaged two ways. 4

CRP: don't force users to depend on what they don't need

The Common Reuse Principle says classes that are reused together belong together, and, more pointedly, classes that aren't tightly bound shouldn't share a component. Depending on a component means depending on all of it: any change to any class in it may force you to recompile, revalidate and redeploy, even if you use one class. It's the ISP for components. Pick what your app actually uses from a "utils" component.

5

The tension triangle

REP and CCP are inclusive: they make components bigger. CRP is exclusive: it makes them smaller. You can't max out all three. Each edge of the triangle names the cost of neglecting the principle at the opposite corner. Tap anywhere inside to choose a balance, or try the presets.

The balance movesProjects usually start near the CCP side: developability matters more than reuse, so you sacrifice some reusability. As other projects begin to draw on yours, it slides toward REP and CRP. Component structure jitters and evolves with the project's focus; it reflects how the project is developed and used more than what it does.
