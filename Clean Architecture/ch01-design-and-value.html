Design, Architecture and Two Values

Design and architecture are the same thing seen at different zoom levels. Their single goal is to keep the cost of building and changing a system low for its whole life.

1

Design and architecture are one continuum

People often use "architecture" for the lofty, high-level picture and "design" for the nitty-gritty. The book rejects the split outright. Look at a real architect's house plans: next to the overall shape and room layout you find where every light switch goes and how the foundations are poured. The big decisions and the small ones are one fabric. Software is the same: there is a continuum of decisions, from the shape of the whole system down to the name of a variable, and no line where "architecture" stops.

2

The goal: minimize the people it takes

The goal of architecture is to minimize the human resources required to build and maintain the system.

That gives us a measure. If the effort to meet the customer's needs is low and stays low across the system's life, the design is good. If each release costs more than the last, it is bad. The chapter backs this with a real company's numbers (anonymized). Flip between the charts below; they are redrawn to show the shape of the story, not the exact data.

The signature of a messNobody slacked off. The developers worked as hard in release 8 as in release 1. Their effort simply moved from building features to shuffling the mess around so one more small feature could fit. By the end, a line of code cost about forty times as much as at the start.
3

The Hare's lie: messy is faster

Developers tell themselves two lies. The first: "we'll clean it up later, we just have to get to market." Later never comes, because the market pressure never lets up. The second and bigger one: that making a mess is faster in the short term. It isn't, on any timescale. Jason Gorman tested this on himself: six days, the same small Roman-numerals kata each day, using test-driven development on alternate days. The TDD days came out about 10% faster, and even his slowest TDD day beat his fastest non-TDD day.

Illustrative bars with the experiment's shape: a learning curve downward, with TDD days (green) consistently below their neighbors.

Now play the long game. Two teams start with the same capacity. One spends a share of each release keeping code clean; the other spends nothing and lets the mess pile up. Slide the cleanliness share and watch the total features shipped.

The only way to go fast is to go wellAnd starting over from scratch is the Hare again. The same overconfidence that made the first mess will make the second one.
4

Two values: behavior and structure

Every system gives its stakeholders two kinds of value. Behavior: it does what the requirements say, and it makes or saves money. Structure (architecture): it stays soft, easy to change. The word is soft-ware for a reason; if we wanted machines whose behavior is hard to change we would have called it hardware.

The cost of a change should depend on its scope, how much it asks for, not on its shape, how badly it fits the existing structure. Stakeholders send a stream of requests of roughly equal size. To developers stuck with a rigid structure each one is a jigsaw piece that fits worse than the last. Send some requests and compare.

Which matters more, working or changeable? Look at the extremes. Tap each.

5

Urgent versus important

Eisenhower is credited with the line that the urgent things are seldom important and the important things are seldom urgent. Behavior is usually urgent; architecture is important but never urgent. Order the four boxes and architecture takes slots 1 and 2, behavior slots 1 and 3. The classic failure is promoting slot 3, urgent but unimportant features, to slot 1 and starving the structure. If architecture comes last, the system becomes ever costlier to develop until change is practically impossible.
