Layers, Boundaries and Main

Even a tiny game has more boundaries than three layers suggest. And somewhere, one grubby component has to wire it all together.

Is it really just three layers?

It's tempting to think of systems as UI, business rules, database. Fine for a tiny app; most systems have more components. Take a 1970s text adventure, Hunt the Wumpus: you move through caverns with commands like GO EAST and SHOOT WEST, avoiding pits and bats, hunting the Wumpus. At first glance: a text UI, the game rules, and some storage for game state. But keep asking "what could vary?" Toggle the axes of change you discover.

Boundaries are everywhere; which do you build?

The point of the silly example: architectural boundaries exist everywhere, and full ones are expensive. Over-engineering is often worse than under-engineering ("you aren't going to need it"). But adding a boundary late, once things have tangled, is costly and risky too. The dissatisfying answer: watch. Note where boundaries might go, look for the first inkling of friction, and implement a boundary when the cost of building it drops below the cost of continuing to ignore it. It's not a one-time decision.

4

Main: the dirtiest component

Every system has at least one component that creates, coordinates and oversees the others: Main. It's the system's entry point, called by nothing but the operating system. It builds factories, strategies and global facilities, injects dependencies, then hands control to the high-level abstract parts. It's the lowest-level policy there is: the dirtiest of the dirty, in the outermost circle. Think of Main as a plugin to the application. You can have one Main per environment, configuration, country or customer. Pick one.
