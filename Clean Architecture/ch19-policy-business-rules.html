Policy, Level and Business Rules

Policies differ in level: how far they sit from inputs and outputs. The highest-level policy is the business rule, the thing that would exist even without a computer.

1

Software is a statement of policy

At its core a program is just a detailed description of the policy by which inputs become outputs. In any real system that policy breaks down into many smaller policies: how a rule is calculated, how a report is formatted, how input is validated. Part of the architect's art is to separate those policies and regroup them by how they change: same reasons and same times, same level, same component. Then arrange the components in a DAG where the edges connect different levels.

Level = distance from the inputs and outputs.

The farther a policy sits from both the input and the output, the higher its level. Below is a tiny encryption program: read characters, translate them, write them. Drag the slider to move along the data flow and see each part's level.

Decouple dependencies from data flow

The tempting version has the encryption function call readChar() and writeChar() directly: high-level policy depending on low-level I/O. The better version gives Encrypt two interfaces it owns, CharReader and CharWriter. Console, file or network classes implement them. Data still flows reader → encrypt → writer, but the source code arrows all point inward, at the encryption policy.

Why level predicts changeHigher-level policies change less often and for more important reasons. Lower-level ones, close to I/O, change often, urgently, for less important reasons. The I/O devices are far likelier to change than the cipher. Point dependencies toward high level and urgent-but-trivial changes can't touch important code. Spot the SRP, OCP, CCP and SAP all at work in this one small design.






  
3
Critical business rules and entities

  
Strictly, a business rule makes or saves money for the business whether or not a computer is involved. A bank charging N% interest on a loan is a business rule even if a clerk with an abacus computes it. These are Critical Business Rules, and they work on Critical Business Data (balance, rate, schedule) that would exist even unautomated. Bound together, they form an Entity: pure business, untouched by databases, UIs or frameworks. It needn't even be an object; any language that can bind data and functions will do. Use cases: application-specific rules

  
Other rules only make sense because the system is automated. A loan officer's app might insist that no payment estimate is shown until contact information is gathered and validated and the credit score is at least 500. That's a use case: a description of how the automated system is used, with its inputs, outputs and processing steps. Use cases control when entity rules are invoked. Note what the use case doesn't say: nothing about web pages, thick clients or consoles. Only the data in and the data out, informally. And the dependency direction: use cases know about entities; entities know nothing of use cases. Entities are higher level, because they generalize across many applications and sit farther from I/O.






  
5
Request and response models

  
Use cases take simple request data structures and return simple response data structures. Those structures depend on nothing: not on HttpRequest, not on any framework, not on HTML or SQL. Otherwise the use cases inherit those dependencies. And resist putting Entity references inside them; entities and request/response models change for very different reasons.
