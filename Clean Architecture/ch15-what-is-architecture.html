What Is Architecture?

An architect's job is not to make the system work. It's to make it easy to develop, deploy, operate and maintain, by deciding as little as possible as late as possible.

1

Architecture is a shape, and architects are programmers

Forget the image of the architect who stops coding and hands down decisions. Software architects are programmers, often the best, who keep programming so they feel the problems they create for everyone else. What they shape is this:

The architecture of a system is the shape given to it: its division into components, their arrangement, and how they communicate.

The shape's purpose is to make the system easy to develop, deploy, operate and maintain. The strategy is to leave as many options open as possible, for as long as possible. Notice what isn't on the list: "make it work". Plenty of terrible architectures work fine. Architecture's job is about the cost of the system across its life, not its behavior.

Two kinds of element: policy and details

Every system splits into policy, the business rules and procedures where the value lives, and details, the things needed to let people, other systems and programmers talk to the policy, without affecting the policy itself: devices, databases, web servers, frameworks, protocols. The architect's goal is a shape where policy is primary and details are irrelevant to it. Sort these.

4

Decide later, know more

If the policy doesn't care whether storage is relational, distributed, hierarchical or flat files, you don't have to choose early. Same for the web server, REST versus something else, dependency injection frameworks. The longer you wait, the more information you have. And if someone already decided for you? A good architect pretends the decision hasn't been made, and shapes the system so it can still be changed. Slide the moment you pick the database.

Story: device independence in the 1960s

Early programs drove card readers and printers with raw machine instructions. They worked perfectly, until cards got dropped and shuffled and everyone moved to magnetic tape, and every program had to be rewritten. By the late '60s operating systems offered abstract "unit-record" devices: the same program could read cards or tape and write to a printer or a tape.

The author worked at a junk-mail printer that put names into giant pre-printed rolls of form letters. Printing on the rented mainframe's single printer was very expensive. Because the programs were written against the abstraction, the company could write to tape instead and drive five cheap offline printers around the clock. Flip the output device.

Story: physical addressing

Later, on a union-management system, the young team hard-wired disk geometry everywhere: which cylinders held agents, which held employers, records chained by cylinder/head/sector. Every business rule knew the disk's shape. A more experienced colleague suggested treating the disk as one huge linear array of sectors, with one small routine that knew the physical layout.
