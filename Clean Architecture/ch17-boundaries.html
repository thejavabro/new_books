Boundaries and Their Anatomy

Architecture is the art of drawing lines between things that matter and things that don't, and then choosing how strongly each line is enforced at runtime.

Architecture is drawing lines

A boundary separates software elements and keeps those on one side from knowing about those on the other. Some lines are drawn very early, even before code exists; others much later. The early ones are drawn to defer decisions, so that those decisions don't pollute the core business logic.

Remember the goal: minimize the people needed to build and maintain the system. What drains that effort most is coupling to decisions made too early, decisions that have nothing to do with the business needs, like frameworks, databases, web servers, utility libraries, dependency injection. Three short stories.

3

Which lines, and where?

Draw lines between things that matter and things that don't. The GUI doesn't matter to the business rules, so draw a line. The database doesn't matter to the business rules either: it's a tool they use indirectly. The crucial part is the direction of the arrows across the line. Toggle the database to see which side is a plugin to which.

The DatabaseInterface lives with the business rules. DatabaseAccess implements it and lives with the database. All arrows cross the line pointing toward the business rules, so the database could be anything, and the business rules recompile for none of it.

4

The plugin argument

Put the two lines together and you get a plugin architecture: the core business rules are kept separate from, and independent of, the components that are either optional or can take many forms. The GUI and database plug in. Consider an IDE and an add-on that plugs into it. The IDE's team can break the add-on any time with a release; the add-on's team can never break the IDE. That asymmetry is exactly what you want between your business rules and your details. Choose which side is which.

The SRP againBoundaries are drawn along the axis of change. Components on one side change at different rates, and for different reasons, than those on the other.
5

Boundary anatomy: how strong is the line at runtime?

A boundary crossing at runtime is nothing more than a function on one side calling a function on the other and passing some data. The trick is managing the source code dependencies of that call. How the two sides are physically separated varies a lot. Pick a kind of boundary.

Most real systems mix these. A service may be a monolith internally; a local process may contain many source-level boundaries. Whatever the kind, the rule is the same: lower-level details point at higher-level policy.
