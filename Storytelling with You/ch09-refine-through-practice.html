Refine through practice

A polished presenter can rescue average slides; great slides cannot rescue an unprepared presenter. Practice out loud, script the first and last minutes, get the right feedback and rehearse for real.

1

The presenter makes or breaks it

The author recalls a short conference talk on the kākāpō, an endangered New Zealand parrot, a topic she had no interest in, that held her completely because of the speaker. The lesson: a polished presenter can overcome mediocre materials; the reverse is not true. Great speakers look effortless because you never see the preparation, as with a TED talk's months of coaching. The good news: it's learnable.

2

Practice out loud, three ways

The number-one tip. Clicking silently through slides tells you what each slide says, but not how to get from one to the next. Speaking forces you to find the transitions, which turn a disjointed set of slides into a smooth talk.

How much practice?Until you're comfortable and can talk through it eloquently, more for higher stakes. The author disagrees that you can over-rehearse: knowing your content frees attention to read faces and adapt.
Commit, don't memorizeMemorizing a script is fragile: forget one phrase and everything wobbles. Commit the order of material and key phrases to memory instead. She rehearses rough patches in her head while brushing her teeth.
3

Plan the beginning and the end

The opening minutes decide whether people keep listening; the close is the lasting impression. Plan both, even though you don't script the rest. A planned start also carries you through the first-minute nerves.

Start strong

Before a live-streamed podcast interview, she plotted only a sequence (introduce herself, introduce the guest and why he's interesting, tell how they met, ask the first question), then practiced it aloud several ways. Each run was different, which gave her flexibility on the day.

End deliberately

Know what feeling or idea people should leave with. On stage, build to a crescendo with a well-rehearsed example and a powerful final statement. In a meeting: thank people, recap next steps, say when the next update comes. If the last slide stays up during discussion, make it the one you want them looking at.

4

Choose who gives feedback, and ask for something specific

Two dimensions matter. Tap a combination.

Several reviewers help validate: one odd comment may be noise; the same comment from three people is a signal. Also say what you want: content and wording, or delivery (body, voice)? And disclose constraints: tomorrow's meeting gets fine-tuning, next month's can take a rethink. Sorter view suits a review of flow; a full run suits delivery.

5

Do a dry run, and time it

Rehearse in conditions like the real ones: seated at a table if you'll be seated, with the clicker, the computer, the video tool (screen sharing, switching views), even the clothes and shoes you'll wear. Note when you hit key points and when you finish, add estimates for questions, and compare with your slot. For fixed slots, make a timing sheet and build in buffers: places you can expand or skip, such as section breaks and Q&A pauses.

Timing sheet

Be precise with your wordsTwo speakers back to back: one said "if you aren't familiar, a quick tour; if you are, bear with me" and "I don't have evidence for this, but I suspect...". The other presented old ideas as new and new ones as common knowledge. Small phrases honor the audience's experience, or insult it.

Short on time? Stop polishing slides. Spend the time on yourself: practice aloud, transitions, start and end, a dry run.

6

Case study: what talking it through changed

Talking through the deck in sorter view, the consultant finds gaps: the sales-history slide needs to be built in several views; the "5 simple ingredients" slide needs a follow-on about why macadamias matter; the packaging test needs an introduction before its results. Talking slide by slide, she finds she over-engineered an animation: the line graph appeared year by year in 17 clicks. Too much clicking.

She plans her opening around the deck title, "The magic is in the macadamia nuts", and her close: a three-option slide for discussion, then the title slide again. She previews the plan with Matt, Vanessa's chief of staff, and re-syncs with the R&D scientists on the analysis: good practice in case the exacting CFO digs in.
