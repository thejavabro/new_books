Don't go it alone

You may own the talk, but you can recruit help.

Online and can't see anyone? The author tapes a motivating picture near the camera as a substitute friendly face.

3

Be present, watch the room, adjust

Don't rush into content. Notice where you are and who's there, breathe, smile, then start the opening you practiced. If nerves get you anyway (talking too fast, losing your thread) pause, breathe, sip water, and reset. Keep an eye on the clock (wear a watch) and on people. Read each cue and choose a response.

Cue 1

Lights in your eyes on stage? You may not see anyone, so lean on the people you can see at the front, or on planted allies. And the strongest tool for regaining a drifting room: stop. Sudden silence after steady talk turns heads. Or change pace or volume, or walk toward the distracted person.

5

Connect, invite interaction, answer well

Bring people into the session: polls, shout-outs, chat, small-group discussion, a microphone runner. When a question is best answered by someone in the room, warn them first: "Great question, Sarah. Brian, I'm going to turn to you in a moment, but first..." For off-topic questions, a polite redirect works: acknowledge, then pivot to a question others usually ask. The author once stepped off a conference stage into the crowd at the climax of a talk, to share a pivotal moment up close. Going to people is a powerful, rare move.

6

Respond gracefully to the unexpected

If anything can go wrong, eventually it will. What the audience sees is how calm you stay. Match each mishap from the author's career to the response she used.

Not every surprise is a failure. The host might deliver a full rundown of your background when you planned to introduce yourself, or an organizer might ask everyone to pull out their phones for a QR-code survey just as your keynote begins. Think on your feet: skip the intro, or find a way to win attention back. Treat it as productive discomfort. Some of the best presentation moments come out of it.

7

Reflect, and refine for next time

Afterwards, revisit the goals you wrote down beforehand, for the audience and for yourself. What went as hoped? What will you try next time? Ask the person you lined up for feedback. There is no finish line for presenting well; every talk is a rehearsal for the next.
