Build your confidence

Confidence is built, not born. Watch and listen to yourself, then work on how you stand, move, look and sound, and prepare so thoroughly that surprises stop being scary.

1

Picture the stellar version first

Close your eyes, take three breaths, and imagine you just gave the most important presentation of your career and it went brilliantly: they listened, changed their minds, applauded. What does that feel like, and how does it show in your face and body? Write down the adjectives you'd want people to use about you. Knowing content is one thing; commanding a room is another. This chapter closes the gap.

2

Record yourself, then review three times

The most uncomfortable and most powerful tool. In a train-the-trainer course at Google, the author watched her recording and saw she shifted her weight constantly, making her whole body sway, something she'd probably always done. Told, she might have shrugged it off; seen, she couldn't. Record about five minutes, audio and video, in the posture you'll present in (seated for virtual or table meetings). Then step through the reviews.

The storytelling with data team uses this for onboarding: new hires record themselves presenting workshop sections and get feedback, a habit the whole team keeps. Watching other presenters, such as TED talks, is a good complement.

3

Look like someone people want to watch

Not about looks, about presence. Tap each habit.

Use space to mean somethingTo compare before and after, gesture and turn toward one side for "before" and the other for "after". The audience starts mapping states to places. Mind the mirror: your left is their right, and in Western cultures processes flow left to right from their view. Online, practice gestures on camera so they stay in frame.
4

Sound like someone people want to listen to

Start by removing filler: the "um", "like", "you know", and personal favorites. They often creep in while you search for a word; a short pause is better and sounds more confident. Paste a transcript of yourself (or type how you talk) and count.

Then breathing: posture opens the lungs, deep breaths give volume and steadiness, and too little air causes the creaky "vocal fry". Protect your voice; the author once lost hers entirely mid-tour in Australia. Finally, vary your cadence. Play with the dials.

Voice follows body: hunched shoulders and downcast eyes sound flat. And sound like you care. If you don't, nobody else will.

5

Prepare so the unexpected is less scary

Pack a presenter kit

Being equipped makes you feel equipped. A version of the author's list. Tick what you'd carry.

Visit the space

Book the room or a tech check: connect your laptop, find where you and the audience will see the screen, try the microphone (on, off, mute). Online, do a planning call from the exact spot you'll present from. Then visualize yourself there, presenting with ease.

Anticipate what could go wrong

Mics that won't clip to a dress, lost voices, cancelled flights: it all happens. Brainstorm failures and likely questions with colleagues, like disaster training. A stoic trick: imagine the realistic worst case. It is rarely the end of the world, and having faced it in your head, you'll handle it better.

6

Case study: the sticky note on the monitor

Recording a section of the TRIX deck, the consultant lists fixes: fewer hand movements at key points; stop starting sentences with "So..."; drop "sort of" (11 times in seven minutes); stop ending sentences with "right?". She writes the three on a sticky note, crosses them out in red, puts it on her monitor, and asks close colleagues to flag them. During her run-through with Matt at Nosh she practices in the actual meeting room, checks in with the scientists for likely questions, brainstorms failure modes with her team, and packs her kit.
