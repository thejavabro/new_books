Introduce yourself

How you introduce yourself sets how people listen to everything after. Plan it like a presentation: audience, impressions, evidence, story, practice.

Why it mattersImpressionsCompile & formMake it greatTRIX caseQuick check
1

The first minute frames everything after

Job interviews, networking, the opening of a talk: we introduce ourselves constantly and rarely plan it. How you introduce yourself sets how people receive what follows, and being able to talk about yourself well also boosts confidence. The chapter sits late in the book on purpose: crafting an introduction reuses nearly every technique so far. The process has five steps.

1 · Audiencewho they are, what they care about
2 · Impressions3 to 5 attributes to convey
3 · Piecesbrainstorm evidence on stickies
4 · Storyarrange into a narrative
5 · Make it greatpractice, feedback, constraints

The same steps work for introducing a company, product or industry, and for preparing for job interviews: weave the stories into your answers.

2

Pick your key impressions

Spend a full five minutes completing "I would like to be described as ____", aiming for ten or more words; the long timer pushes you past the obvious. Then group similar words and keep 3 to 5 distinct ones. Try it with these starters.

Show, don't claimDon't say you're honest. Tell the story of the time, as a child, you saw a $5 bill fall from a stranger's pocket and ran to give it back. Impressions are created by the experiences you choose to tell.
3

Compile the pieces, form the story

One sticky per attribute, then brainstorm experiences around each: roles, projects, education, interests. Go beyond plain facts. Five prompts help; tap to see examples of the kind of note each might produce (illustrative).

Stuck? Ask someone who knows you well, or drop the attribute constraint and list defining moments, then see which attributes they demonstrate. Then arrange: one powerful story in depth, or several woven together? Find the hook or tension, decide the start and end, and start a discard pile, because too many good anecdotes make a disjointed intro.

4

Practice, get feedback, then squeeze it

Practice aloud from the general story, not a script. Ask someone to listen as your audience would, then listen without getting defensive. Record and review (body, hands, pace, volume, pitch). Test it in low-risk settings, like meeting someone new socially. Finally, apply time constraints: how would you do it in two minutes, 30 seconds, one sentence? Slide to compress the consultant's introduction. The longer versions are illustrative, assembled from the case study; the one-sentence version is close to the book's own.

5

Case study: the consultant's introduction

The Nosh team already knows her, but given the stakes she runs the full exercise anyway. Vanessa must be wowed; Riley and Charlie need to see she understands their competing priorities. Her five-minute list includes credible, confident, poised, articulate, trustworthy, thoughtful, smart, discerning, thorough and more. Grouping and prioritizing against the real goal (win the ongoing partnership) leaves three.

Many notes fit two attributes, so she arranges the attributes as a triangle and places each experience between the ones it shows. Tap an attribute.

A bonus idea, unrelated to the attributes: she remembers her first whole macadamia in TRIX, at her desk, snapped into the moment by it. Her story becomes: open with that memory, jump to today's project, add tension by describing what a bad consultant-client relationship looks like from her early career, then show how she has worked with Nosh and come to understand the brand. A colleague's dry-run feedback: lead with the interesting questions the research answered. Her recording shows no filler words and better hand use than before. If a new face appears, the one-liner is ready.
