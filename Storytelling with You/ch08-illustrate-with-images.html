Illustrate with images

Pictures used on purpose explain, stick in memory and set the mood. Pictures used to fill space do the opposite. Commit to the image or leave it out.

1

Four good reasons to use a picture

We have all seen the bad versions: a stretched photo filling empty space, a handshake for "partnership", clip art that annoys. Those come from "we have room, add something" or "spice it up". Pictures used with intent can explain, hold attention and stick. The chapter groups the good reasons into four.

2

“Where are your eyes drawn?”

A demonstration used in the author's workshops: a screen full of colorful balloons, then the same balloons in gray with one blue. You look at the blue one before anyone tells you to. An image like this teaches sparing contrast and is easy to recall later, the picture superiority effect: we remember pictures better than words, likely because images are encoded both visually and verbally.

3

Photographs: commit to the image

The commonest image type. First rule: commit. No small photo in a leftover corner; let it take the whole slide, paired with a few words. Never stretch a photo (crop instead), use high resolution, and avoid the overused: money for sales, a bullseye for targets, the globe for "global". Take your own photo when you can, or use stock with its license read. Place subjects using the rule of thirds rather than dead center; it gives the eye somewhere to travel and leaves room for text.

4

Illustrations: the style sets the tone

Anything drawn. Less polished than a photo, which can be the point. Three drawings of the same bird can feel stately, quick and bold, or silly. Pick the style that matches the mood you want. Drawings also show things that don't exist yet, like a product prototype, exactly as you specify. Avoid clip art and comics: they rarely help and can cost credibility. The author sketches on paper or an iPad (Procreate), and hires an illustrator for books.

5

Diagrams: sketch, declutter, highlight one node

For a process, concept or relationship people need to see. Sketch on paper first (it keeps clutter out and frees you from templates). Push borders and arrows to gray, use color only where you want eyes, and step through the nodes while you talk. A simplified full diagram can act as your navigation scheme for detailed sections. Maps can be pictures, data or diagrams; use them when geography matters.

Images in virtual presentationsIn a room, a blank slide puts attention on you during a story. Online, a blank slide looks like a glitch and invites the inbox. Show a simple, on-topic picture to occupy eyes while ears stay on you.
6

Case study: three photos that earn their place

Reviewing the slide list, the consultant finds three uses for images. A hand-sketched, then hand-built winding path diagram shows the twisting route to the recommendation (a template would have pushed her toward a straight line) and doubles as the appendix navigation. Packaging photos make sure everyone pictures the same see-through window. And close-ups of the three mixes, staged by a teammate in glass bowls on a white windowsill, shot on a phone, background removed: about 30 minutes for custom, professional images no stock site could provide. She'll reveal them one at a time as she describes each blend.

Ingredient drawings here are simplified stand-ins, not the book's photos. The original's five ingredients come from the book; the alternatives' exact recipes are not fully listed in the text, so only the changes it names are shown.
