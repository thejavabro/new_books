Say it with words

Words on slides can prime, focus and reinforce, or they can compete with you. Use titles that state the point, keep text scannable, and reveal it at the pace you speak.

1

Titles that say something

Words feel simple, so we rarely think about them, and they quietly work against us. Start with titles. Most people use descriptive ones ("Supplier Analysis"). The chapter argues for titles that carry a thought.

Presentation title: foreshadow

"Changing our supplier strategy could reduce costs" beats "Supplier Analysis". It primes, intrigues, and often uses "we" or "our" to bring people in. Note the sentence case. If that feels too bold, combine: a descriptive title with a foreshadowing subtitle, or the reverse. Conference talks allow more playful type and images.

Slide title: the takeaway

Write the slide's point as one full sentence, then shorten it to fit on one line. "NPS up; however, customers are increasingly polarized", or to stress the concern, "Concern: increasingly polarized customer base". Titles can also name the action: understand, discuss, decide.

People can't read and listen at the same time

When a slide is full of text, the audience reads it silently, faster than you can speak it, stops listening, and drifts off while you are still several bullets behind. Press play to watch the race.

3

Word slides along the story

Text slides cluster at the start and end of a story, with some in the middle. Pick a stage.

Visual hierarchyDon't treat all elements equally. Pull the important ones forward with size, bold, sparing color and placement; push the rest back with gray, smaller size and lower position. Dense content becomes scannable.

5

The Big Idea on a slide, and a pithy repeatable phrase

The Big Idea you drafted for yourself can also go on screen. A common pattern: show it early to set expectations (the opportunity), then return to a shorter, punchier version at the end and leave it up during discussion. By then people have the evidence to act on it.

Go further: compress it into a pithy, repeatable phrase. Short, catchy, maybe alliterative, not necessarily cute, but memorable. Repetition moves things from short-term to long-term memory, so use it at the start, the end, or woven through.

6

Case study: from teleprompter slide to animated build

The early context slide began as an issue-focused title over a paragraph of text. Two fixes: retitle it toward the shared goal (rework the mix, not price increase), and cut the paragraph into a few punchy bullets. Then reveal one bullet per click, fading the earlier ones, so people see both where you are and the path so far.
