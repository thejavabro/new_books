Show data in graphs

A good graph starts as a sentence. Pick the form that serves that sentence, strip the clutter, point the eye with sparing contrast, and build it up on screen while you talk.

1

Have a reason for every number

Data can inform, build credibility, overturn assumptions and drive change. But having data doesn't oblige you to show it. Ask what each number or graph is meant to achieve. In business the usual aims are speed (our visual system is faster than reading), comprehension of something complex, and memorability (people remember what they saw as well as what they heard). Those aims call for simple graphs. The chapter's process has four steps.

2

Articulate: the graph’s “so what?” in one sentence

Data does not speak for itself; left alone it says something different to each viewer. Write a few sentences about what the graph shows, pick the most important, and simplify it out loud. The book's example: a busy chart of customer satisfaction for many banks over eight years. Possible observations, from vague to sharp:

  • Satisfaction varies across banks and years.
  • It has risen industry-wide and the spread has narrowed.
  • The industry average was above, then at or below, then above the client again.
  • "Financial Savings is below the industry average for the first time in five years." That is a takeaway, and it tells you which graph to draw: two lines over time.

Your rough first graph is fine for this step. Words improve the graph; the improved graph sharpens the words.

3

Visualize: let form follow function

We overcomplicate graphs when we expect them to answer every question or show off the analysis. With the sentence in hand, the function is clear. Pick a job:

Tip from the chapter: get really good at line graphs and bar charts first. Everyone can read them. An unusual chart makes you spend attention teaching how to read it before you can talk about the data.

Three word habits that make graphs easyPut the takeaway in the title (people are more likely to remember a graph titled with its message). Title the axes, aligned top left. Label data directly next to the lines instead of using a legend.
4

Refine: declutter, then focus attention

An illustrative company food drive, meals served per year. Defaults from any tool are built for every case, so they rarely fit yours. Switch clutter off one item at a time, then add focus. (Numbers are invented for illustration; only the shape echoes the book's example.)

There is evidenceA 2021 study the author co-authored with Northwestern's Visual Thinking Lab compared cluttered, decluttered, and decluttered-plus-focused charts. Decluttering raised ratings of professionalism; adding focus raised ratings of aesthetics and clarity and improved what people remembered.
5

Weave: set the stage, then build the graph

Most data slides sit mid-story. Put a full graph on screen and people start decoding it and stop listening, or look somewhere else. Instead: a nearly blank set-up slide, then build the graph a step at a time while you talk. It's especially useful online, where the inbox is one click away. Keep one graph per slide in most cases.

6

Case study: from bar chart to dot plot, one move at a time

The taste-test summary table is a data dump: fine for colleagues or an appendix, never for the presentation. The takeaway: the original mix beats both alternatives overall and on appearance, texture and taste. A clean bar chart would show that, but the consultant needs to add more dimensions later, so she leads the audience from a familiar bar chart into a less familiar dot plot. Scores are on a 9-point "dislike extremely" to "like extremely" scale (values approximate).
