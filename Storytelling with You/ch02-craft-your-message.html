Craft your message

If you cannot say your point in one sentence, a deck will not say it for you. Boil the story down to plot, twist and ending, then to a single sentence with a point of view and real stakes.

1

Know what you want them to do

We tend to finish the work and start making slides straight away. The chapter asks you to stop first and answer: what do you want to communicate, and what should the audience do? The action can be a decision, a discussion, options to weigh, or an idea to react to. If you can't name any action, question whether you need to present at all.

Two tools get you there, one longer and one shorter: the 3-minute story and the Big Idea.

2

The 3-minute story: plot, twist, ending

If you only had a few minutes (an elevator, a meeting slot cut short), what would you say? Writing that version proves you understand your own story and makes you less dependent on slides. It has three parts. Try it on a project of your own; the page keeps your draft in this browser.

Read it aloud and trim. From here you can expand (a storyboard, a full talk) or condense, which is the next step.

3

The Big Idea: one sentence, three jobs

The Big Idea, adapted from Nancy Duarte's Resonate, is the key message you want to land. It becomes a North Star and a litmus test for every later piece of content: does this help get my Big Idea across? It must do three things.

4

Find the stakes: play it out to the extreme, then back off

Stuck on what's at stake? Imagine the chain of consequences if they don't act: X, which leads to worse Y, which leads to disaster Z. Then back off to a level that fits the situation. Do the same in the positive direction. Slide along the ladder.

The auditor who went too farA workshop participant told colleagues their whole profession would vanish because of blockchain. It was so extreme that it was easy to ignore. Reframing around reputational risk inside the organization, nearer and believable, got people listening.

6

Say it to someone, ideally as a team

Once drafted, explain your Big Idea to someone and let them pepper you with "who?", "how?" and "so what?". A partner with no context is often best: they expose the tacit knowledge you forgot you were assuming.

In a team, have each person draft their own Big Idea, put them side by side, and argue toward one master version. The result is usually better than any single draft, shows whether people are aligned, and builds buy-in.

7

Case study: from five drafts to one Big Idea

Plot: macadamia nuts, a key TRIX ingredient, keep getting pricier, so the mix must change. Twist: neither alternative mix tested as well as the original. Ending: there are still good options; let's discuss and decide. The consultant decides to be bold and recommend a course, not just list options. Four colleagues and the consultant each draft a Big Idea. Pick each one to see what the team said about it.
