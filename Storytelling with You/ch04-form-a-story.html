Form a story

The usual business deck marches through background, method, findings. A story has shape: it finds the tension your audience cares about, builds to it and resolves it with the action you want.

1

The usual shape: a flat line

The typical business presentation follows the order the work happened: background, then methods (scope, meetings, data, analysis), then findings, then recommendations. It's logical, chronological and familiar. The author calls it, provocatively, selfish: you can follow it perfectly without once thinking about the audience. A story, by contrast, has a shape, and the shape comes from tension. Toggle the two.

2

What story means here

Not the business buzzword. Real story, like a children's book: characters in a place and time, something that throws things out of balance, ups and downs, and a resolution. Think of a favorite from childhood. You can probably still retell it decades later. That recall comes from its shape (one event leads to the next, giving anchors to remember) and from its emotional pull. Those are the properties worth borrowing.

3

The narrative arc, piece by piece

Tap a point on the arc to see what it means in a business presentation and the question to ask yourself.

Your arc needn't be smooth or symmetrical. The climax might be a small bump, come early, or arrive late. What matters is that there is a planned shape.

4

Reality is a jagged mountain; pick the peaks

Every project really had many ups and downs. You need to know them all, but each audience needs a different subset. You are not telling your story; you are forming a story for your audience. Choose an audience to see which peaks you'd keep.

5

Find the tension (don't invent it)

Tension is not drama for its own sake. If there were no tension, there would be nothing to present. The job is to find the tension that matters to the audience and bring it to light; then the action you ask for resolves it. It links straight back to "what's at stake" in the Big Idea.

6

When story works, and when to go carefully

IdealYou've done something (a project, research, an analysis) and want to take people on a journey so they understand, discuss, decide or change.
Tread lightlyA required template, a culture that expects a fixed format, or a very high-stakes first try. Start in lower-risk settings and build credibility. A bridge: deliver the expected document and "first, let me tell you the story of the project".
Stories inside the presentationShort personal stories (a childhood memory, a moment at work) can connect, illustrate or build credibility. Map them on the arc too, decide what to leave out, and practice them.
7

Case study: re-shaping the TRIX storyboard

The plot already contains tension (costs make the mix unsustainable), but the tension of the story is different: there's no clean fix. The original mix was preferred. Arranging stickies on an arc, the consultant realizes she cut too much: as a new vendor she must show rigor. She toys with a choose-your-own-adventure deck, rejects it (it gives away her expertise), and lands on a hybrid: tell the story, put the full linear detail in an appendix. Step through her arc.
