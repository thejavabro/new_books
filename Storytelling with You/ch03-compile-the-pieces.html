2

Brainstorm, from several perspectives

Set a timer for 10 to 15 minutes and write ideas, one per small sticky note (the author uses 1⅞-inch squares; index cards or cut-up paper work too). Don't judge or order yet. Imagine each note might become a slide. What goes on them?

Then switch lenses. Re-brainstorm as your audience: what would they need to see to understand and act? With a mixed group, do it once per persona. Try a colleague's or your manager's view too. Changing where you work (standing at the other side of a desk, in the author's case) can also spark ideas.

3

Edit: curate, arrange, and discard on purpose

Editing usually takes longer than brainstorming. Group notes into sections, add notes for transitions, combine overlaps, and above all ask of each note: does this help get my Big Idea across? If not, it goes on the discard pile. Knowing the answer to a question doesn't mean it needs a slide. Try it with some of the TRIX brainstorm. Tap a note, then tap a column.

4

Lead with the Big Idea, or build to it?

Brainstorms come out in chronological order (question, data, analysis, findings), which puts the Big Idea at the end. That's not wrong, but it is a choice. Set the situation and see which way the chapter's logic leans.

5

Show the rough plan early, together

Spend 10 to 15 minutes walking a colleague through the storyboard. This is also the cheapest moment to check direction with a client, stakeholder or manager: "This is rough, but here's what I'm thinking." A "no, change course" now costs almost nothing. Managers who want earlier input can ask their teams for storyboards.

In a team, storyboard together on a whiteboard with slide-shaped boxes, draft titles and sketches. Everyone leaves with the same picture of the deck. Remote teams can use a shared doc, a whiteboard app, or big-text slides in sorter view.

6

Case study: the TRIX storyboard

In about 15 minutes the consultant writes some 40 notes, first following the project's own chronology, then from the viewpoints of Vanessa (Head of Product), Jack (CFO) and the R&D scientists. Started in PowerPoint, each note could have become a slide that made sense to her and to nobody else. Instead she discards, groups and gets feedback, including from Matt, Vanessa's chief of staff.

The result leads with the Big Idea, then three sections that could become divider slides: context (why Nosh hired the firm, the studies run), learnings (data and findings), and action (cost vs. preference, the recommendation, the options, a decision).
