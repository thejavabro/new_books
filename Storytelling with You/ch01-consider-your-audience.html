Consider your audience

Everything you plan, build and say is for the people in the room. Start by deciding exactly who they are and what moves them, then shape the whole session around that.

1

Communicate for them, not for you

The easiest way to communicate is for yourself: your perspective, your order, your preferences. It takes no effort because you already live in your head. Communicating to someone else is harder because you have to work out what makes them act. The chapter's core move is a shift of attention: the more carefully you understand the people you are presenting to, the more likely you are to get what you need from them.

The trap of the broad audienceAsk people who they present to and they list groups: leadership, the board, clients, regulators. Every group is a mix of individuals with different needs, and trying to meet many needs at once usually meets none well. So the first job is to prioritize a target audience.
2

Narrow to now, then find the decision maker

Take a product about to launch, and an analysis of competitor pricing. Who cares about the price? Practically everyone, eventually. The book's two-step filter shrinks that list to the people who matter for this communication.

A clear single person is the easy caseWhen someone commissioned the work or owns the decision, design everything for them: what they care about, what scares them, what makes them hesitate, how they like to be spoken to. The book's example: an HR team wanting longer paternity leave showed the CEO what peer companies offered first, then their own shorter policy, and let him propose the change himself. Framing for that one person did the persuading.
3

When the group really is mixed

Sometimes you cannot narrow: a steering committee, all employees, a conference room. Four tactics help. Tap each one.

Personas for big crowdsYou can't model 100 individuals, but you can model four or five types: "the skeptical finance partner", "the new hire who wants basics". For each, note their role, biases, what they care about and what they will question. Then plan how to serve each type.

Virtual meetings make it tempting to invite everyone. Just because you can doesn't mean you should; when you can't control the list, use the mixed-group tactics above.

4

Get inside their heads

Even an audience you know deserves a deliberate look when the stakes are high. Tick the questions you can already answer about your next audience. The gaps tell you where to dig.

Don't know them? Five ways to learn

  • Talk to them beforehand, or in the minutes before you start.
  • Talk to someone who knows them, including someone who failed with them.
  • Talk to people like them: same role, industry or skill level.
  • Collect data: market research, pilots, attendee lists from organizers.
  • Read about them: website, job postings, news, social media.

If you can't do any of these, you are making assumptions. Write them down and ask a colleague to pressure-test them.

The support / resist sketch

Draw one audience member in the middle of a landscape page. Left side: why they will support you. Right side: why they might resist. Then ask how to use the first and defuse the second.

5

Tailor approach, materials and environment

Once you know them, many choices can bend toward them. Pick an audience type to see how the same content might be shaped differently. These profiles are illustrations, not rules from the book.

6

Case study: who is in the room at Nosh

The consultant has run a pilot project for Nosh on TRIX trail mix: rising ingredient costs mean the recipe must change, ideally without upsetting fans. Winning the pilot could mean all of Nosh's research business. Many people will be affected, but narrowing to now and to decision makers leaves the project team. Tap each person.

The common ground: everyone wants TRIX to succeed. Balancing consumer sentiment against cost to make becomes the frame for the whole presentation.
