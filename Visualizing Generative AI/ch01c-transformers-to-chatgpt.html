From Transformers to ChatGPT

In 2018 a transformer trained on 22,000 lines of poetry produced fluent nonsense. By 2024 every chatbot wrote coherent verse. What changed, and what still goes wrong?

1

A poetry machine, then and now

In 2018 one of the authors, Lak, worked with Łukasz Kaiser, one of the eight authors of the transformer paper, on a model that writes the next line of a poem. The training data was 22,000 lines of public-domain poetry from Project Gutenberg, cut into pairs: a line as input, the line that follows as target. Every 14-line sonnet yields 13 pairs.

Turn a sonnet into training pairs

Prompted with Rumi's line "where did the handsome beloved go", the 2018 model answered with something fluent but off-topic: a line about disappointment, not absence. Six years later, every model the authors tried produced fluent and coherent couplets, and all of them rhymed, though nobody asked for rhyme.

2

One small step, after many

ChatGPT (November 2022) was introduced as a sibling of InstructGPT, a model trained to follow instructions. Technically it was one incremental step; practically it moved GenAI from research curiosity to something anyone could use. The two authors met it differently: Priyanka, mid-MBA, used it to condense case studies, brainstorm topics and trim papers to word limits. Lak, remembering his incoherent 2018 poems and Claude Shannon's argument that capturing all internet text would need as many parameters as the compressed internet, wrote a 2022 article calling LLMs "bullshit artists" (with tips on using them anyway).

What he missed: billions of parameters are enough to capture the gist of commonly needed knowledge, and many separate improvements had compounded. Tap a year.

Modern models are trained in four stages

Lak's poetry model learned English, poetry and coherence all at once, from scratch. Modern models split the job, and each stage can use a simpler, more specialized architecture.

4

Mixture of experts: use only part of the network

Models also got larger (from millions of parameters in 2018 to hundreds of billions or trillions) and more efficient. In a mixture of experts (MoE), the network contains many experts, each a sparse path using only some of the weights. A small router sends each token to a few experts, so a model performs like a much bigger one for the compute it actually spends. Researchers found encoder experts specialize (punctuation, proper nouns, verbs) while decoder experts are less specialized.

5

Better data beats more data

Size is not everything if the data is garbage. Microsoft's 2023 paper "Textbooks Are All You Need" trained a small coding model, phi-1, on clear, instructive, "textbook-quality" code plus tutorials and exercises synthesized by GPT-3.5, and it beat models trained on far larger piles of scraped code. Model builders now employ people to write demonstration data, add synthetic data, and sign deals for fresh, licensed content.

6

Smarter algorithms, and models you can make your own

Bigger, better frontier models are not enough for enterprises. Calling them is costly, sending confidential data outside the firewall may be unacceptable (confidentiality), and regulations may restrict where data moves (compliance). So enterprises adapt foundational models with knowledge distillation, adapter tuning, retrieval-augmented generation, tools and agent frameworks, the subjects of chapters 2, 4 and 5. What still goes wrong

In 2021, the "Stochastic Parrots" paper by Bender, Gebru, McMillan-Major and "Shmargaret Shmitchell" warned of unanalyzed datasets, bias, toxicity and environmental cost. Rapid adoption added economic, legal and ethical worries. Tap each challenge, roughly in the order it was raised.
