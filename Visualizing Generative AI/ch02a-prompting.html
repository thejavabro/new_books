Phrase tasks the way the model was trained

A prompt works because it requests a task the model was instruction-tuned on. Providers keep those instruction datasets secret, so you cannot know whether "Reword…", "Rephrase… in 2–3 sentences" or "Shorten…" matches its training best; different models even differ. Prompting is more art than science. One public clue is Databricks' Dolly dataset, written by employees following short guidelines for seven task types. Pick one for a matching phrasing tip:

Even in-frontier tasks fail sometimesAsked to encrypt a passage, Gemini produced something cipher-looking that did not decrypt back to the passage: a hallucinated "encryption". Always check responses.
4

Role, audience and format

Training data often holds many good answers to the same request, written by different roles for different audiences in different formats. An accountant, a lawyer and a journalist explain a tax law differently, and differently again for a student, a median earner or an executive. Naming all three in your prompt helps attention latch onto the right slice of the training data; if no exact slice exists, the model mixes and matches, which is how it generalizes. Two more tricks: repeat the question at the end of a long prompt, and end the prompt with the start of the answer.

Share, reuse and even sell prompts

A prompt that works is an asset. Platforms let you package and share them: OpenAI calls them GPTs, Google calls them Gems, Anthropic keeps a Prompt Library, and LangChain has LangChain Hub. Find out which your organization supports and browse what colleagues have contributed before writing your own.
