What Generative AI Is

Generative AI makes machines create: essays, images, music, video. Before looking inside, it pays to know where the technology helps, where it quietly hurts, and what kind of machine learning it is.

1

Sailing with the wind: the AI frontier

Generative AI (GenAI) is the branch of AI where machines create new content: text, images, music, video. It reached the public with ChatGPT in November 2022, which hit 100 million users in about two months, faster than any product before it.

The authors' favorite mental model comes from a 2023 Harvard Business School study: on tasks inside the frontier of what AI does well, people working with AI beat both people alone and AI alone. On tasks outside it, AI's confident but weak suggestions made people worse. Think of AI as a sail. It helps only when the wind blows where you want to go.

2

Plausible is not the same as correct

We expect computers to be perfect at exact recall and clumsy at artistic variation. GenAI is the opposite. Asked to reproduce Munch's The Scream, an image model makes something recognizable but unfaithful; asked to reimagine it in a cornfield, it does beautifully. That is because GenAI does not look things up. It generates pixels or words that are probabilistically likely given everything it saw in training.

The same property produces hallucinations: fluent, confident, made-up facts. The book's example is a request for a paper that one of Andrew Wiles's collaborators later wrote. The model named a real coauthor and a real journal, then invented the volume, pages and DOI. The toy below assembles citations the same way, from pieces that each look likely.

AI gives machines human capabilities

Artificial intelligence is the part of computer science that gives machines human-like abilities. Most of them disappear into products we use every day. Tap a sense to see an example.

Technology that never reaches a product stays a curiosity. The authors suggest one lens for any AI feature: does it reduce friction, improve accuracy, increase scale or cut cost compared with how things are done today? Their example is retinal scanning.

Models, products, research and stacks

"GenAI" gets used for four different things. Models (GPT-4, Gemini, Claude) generate the content. Products (ChatGPT, Google Docs' Help me write) wrap models in a usable interface. Research pushes the models forward. Software stacks operate them: a training pipeline and an inference pipeline. And the field sits inside a set of nested ideas. Click a ring.

How machine learning learns from labeled examples

Ordinary programs follow rules. But "show more cute cats" needs judgment: no one can write a rule for cuddly or for whisker. Machine learning takes a data-driven route. Show a neural network many labeled examples, compare its outputs with the labels, and nudge its weights to reduce the error (backpropagation computes the nudges). Stop when accuracy stops improving. Using the trained model on new inputs is inference.

Below, a one-neuron model learns to separate "cat" photos from "dog" photos using two made-up features. Train it a step at a time, then click anywhere on the chart to run inference on a new photo.

Blue dots are labeled cats, orange are labeled dogs. A couple are deliberately mislabeled, which is why 100% accuracy is not always possible, and that is often fine.

Feedback keeps training goingAfter launch, people give implicit feedback (they look longer at some photos) and explicit feedback (a Like). Training on such signals is reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF), a big reason modern models keep improving and why companies chase large user bases.
6

Foundational models and fine-tuning

A photo classifier has millions of weights, and as a rule of thumb you need about as many examples to train it from scratch. Transfer learning avoids that: start from a foundational model already trained on a huge general dataset, freeze most of it, and fine-tune only a small part (often a new output layer) on your own few thousand labeled photos.

Illustrative curves, not measurements: the shape is the point. Fine-tuning reaches useful accuracy with orders of magnitude less data.

"As soon as it works, no one calls it AI"

Is Google Maps choosing your subway exit AI? Facebook suggesting who to tag? John McCarthy's quip says that once something works, it becomes "just a feature". Bertram Raphael called AI the name for problems computers don't yet solve well. After the 2024 Nobel Prize in Physics went to neural network pioneers Hopfield and Hinton, the authors add: "once it works, we call it physics."

The authors' adviceStop arguing about whether something counts as AI. Ask whether it reduces friction or improves accuracy, scale or efficiency.
