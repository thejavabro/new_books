Responsible GenAI

"Could we?" is an engineering question. "Should we?" is the one this chapter asks: about alignment, manipulation, trust, misinformation, jobs and the planet.

Could versus should

Responsible AI studies the ethical and social side of building and using AI, with one core idea: keep AI's behavior aligned with human interests. The book offers an overview, not prescriptions; your policies, laws and legal counsel decide what you must do. Four principles apply to all AI. Generative AI stretches each one, because it creates content and weaves itself into people's work, art, learning and health.

Persuade, manipulate, coerce, exploit

Assistants that know a lot about you can tailor content to sway you. Following a 2024 Google DeepMind paper, the book separates four modes of influence. Only the first is normally acceptable, and even it can overstep on life-shaping choices, where people have a right to find out who they are by experience. Classify each scenario (my own examples).

Five scenarios
Anthropomorphism amplifies all fourHuman-like voice, names and empathy make people trust, confide and attach. That creates a power imbalance: the assistant has no feelings at stake and faces no social sanctions, while lonely or anxious users may come to depend on it, disclose more than they intended, and defer decisions to it. The book cites lawsuits and reports of heavy emotional reliance on companion bots.
4

Influence is fine when it serves the user

Avoiding all influence would rule out good uses. A food scanner that steers someone with a nut allergy away from a dish is influencing them, and nobody minds. The book's lens: does the relationship give a net benefit, support personal growth, respect autonomy (informed consent, control of data, ability to leave) and avoid harm?

Trust comes in two kinds: competence trust (can it do the job?) and alignment trust (is it on my side?). Both can be miscalibrated. People also hand a company's reputation to its bot, which is why Air Canada was held to its chatbot's wrong answer.

A caution on tools meant to restore trust: about two-thirds of teachers reported regularly using AI-text detectors, yet one study put detector accuracy at 39.5%, and a Stanford study found them biased against non-native English writers.

5

Scale, targeting, weapons, and blindness

GenAI makes believable falsehood cheap: fake disaster photos that spread after Hurricane Helene in 2024, messages tuned to each reader's biases, and even self-replicating prompt "worms" (researchers' Morris II demo). The quietest danger is automation blindness, borrowed from aviation: when a system is almost always right, people stop noticing when it is wrong.

My illustration, not the book's: errors assumed independent. The book's cases: Whisper reported 98.5% accurate on English, yet a researcher found hallucinations in 80% of the public-meeting transcripts he inspected. A rare error per word is a common error per document, and in medical notes it can matter.

6

Who gains, and does the economy?

One 2023 task-based estimate: about 19% of workers could see at least half their tasks affected by LLMs. Effects run both ways: assistants help, but can bring surveillance and higher expectations; gains tend to favor higher-skilled workers, which risks widening inequality. At the firm level the Jevons paradox may apply: cheaper code can mean more demand for features, and so for programmers.

At the macro level, the Baumol effect can blunt the gains: savings in fast-improving sectors are absorbed by slow ones, so overall growth lags.

7

Energy, water, and second-order effects

Power and water (chips are liquid-cooled) now constrain growth, pushing providers into fusion, nuclear and geothermal deals. Indirect effects cut both ways: fewer trips thanks to better video calls, money for clean power; but also climate misinformation and lock-in of wasteful habits. Jevons applies here too. The book's no-regret moves: prioritize efficiency, be transparent, mitigate early, and ask whether a task needs a giant model at all.
