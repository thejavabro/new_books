1

The failure mode to fear: lost trust

Software projects fail for familiar reasons: misunderstood requirements, technology that underperforms, a flawed design, or misjudged compute, storage and data flows. AI systems add algorithmic complexity, sensitivity to off-nominal scenarios and bad inputs, and decisions made without a human in the loop. Above all, people stop trusting a system whose outputs are inconsistent, wrong or not sensible. Play a month of operations and see how guard rails change the story.

2

If you keep one lesson

Own the non-functional requirements.The architect must identify, communicate, document, and make sure designs satisfy the system's non-functional requirements (reliability, security, explainability, scalability, maintainability and the rest). The designs that meet them will shape the AI implementation, and the AI choices will shape them in return.

The architect is centuries old as a role and newly essential in software and AI. Disciplined architecture gives a coherent vision, makes sure the right system is built by giving stakeholders a voice, partitions the system so teams can work with unity of effort, and keeps the design balanced with appropriate mitigations. The architect also informs project control: scope, schedule, resources and staffing.

Data engineering: a discipline of its own

A system's effectiveness depends on data quality: in training, configuration, inference, validation and production. That has made the data engineer a recognised specialty, not "just data processing". They scope the data ecosystem along four dimensions, which drive hardware and tooling plans. Data quality (integrity, format, consistency) needs active checks or fallbacks, because quality problems erode trust in inferences.
