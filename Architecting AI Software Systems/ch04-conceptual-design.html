Conceptual Design for AI Systems

The costliest mistake in AI development is building the wrong system. Conceptual design (a concept of operations, scenarios, use cases and modes) is how you find out what the right one is before writing code.

1

The restaurant with no menu

Imagine a restaurant where the waiter tells you what you will eat, never asks your preferences or budget, and still hands you the bill. It would close quickly. Software projects that skip conceptual design behave the same way. Conceptual design sits at the top-left of the systems engineering "V": it is where the vision is first written down, and it is what the finished system is finally validated against. Slide to see when a misunderstanding is discovered and roughly what it costs to fix (illustrative multipliers).

The conceptual design artifact has three parts: a concept of operations, scenarios and use cases. They let customers state goals, surface hidden assumptions and check that the builders understood; they give the architect the end goals, constraints and the customer's idea of value.

2

CONOPS: the bridge between stakeholders and builders

The Concept of Operations is the first definition of the system-to-be: what it will do, how well, under what constraints, and what it should not do (IEEE 1362-2022 provides a template). For AI it must say how AI creates value and map the data flows: ingestion and sources, preprocessing and features, training, validation and deployment, inference and integration points, and feedback loops for learning. Click either side of the bridge.

3

What is AI worth? The business value matrix

Before building, say plainly what AI will do for the organisation: more revenue, lower cost, more efficiency or safety. The book's AI business value matrix sorts ideas by business impact and implementation complexity. Place each idea.

AI touches the whole system: efficiency through automation, decision quality through analytics, experience through personalisation, risk through prediction, resources through optimisation, and speed to market. It also changes people's jobs: from doing to overseeing, new accountability, new skills, new workflows, and new demands for transparency and explainability.

4

The AI-human interaction spectrum

Where your system sits between "human decides" and "AI decides" shapes interaction design, control protocols, expectations and oversight.

5

Scenarios, success and constraints

Tools only shine in the right context: think of a sculptor's chisel, or Zillow's home-flipping losses of more than $600 million, which the book attributes to misused AI models. Scenarios give that context: how the system accomplishes its missions, who the actors are, whether it is centralised or distributed, which external systems and hard constraints apply, and threat-modelling for attacks. Each scenario should define success, nominal operation and failure using metrics from three domains. Pick constraints for a medical triage assistant.

6

Use cases: scenarios with enough detail to build from

Use cases are more detailed than scenarios and guide development. Build them hierarchically: level 1 use cases together cover everything in the CONOPS; lower levels derive from them. Each should contain at least twelve elements. Tap each slot to fill an example: maintenance mode in a predictive maintenance system.

Use case: Retrain the bearing-failure model

Also define user classes formally: how each human role and the AI interact, what data each role needs, and where on the automation scale each interaction sits.

7

Six operational modes

The CONOPS, scenarios and use cases reveal the system's modes. For each, document what the AI component does and what it needs from the rest of the system. Fire events and watch the component move between modes.

8

Conceptual design as risk reduction

A complete conceptual design gives a holistic view that can be analysed for risk. The book's figure maps each risk to the activity that addresses it and puts a reduction range on each; these are the book's own illustrative figures. Tap a risk.

A sobering exampleThe book recalls a 2003 incident in which a Patriot missile battery's automated software misidentified a UK fighter jet as a threat and shot it down, killing the crew. Integration and misidentification risks are not academic.
9

Case study: a retail recommendation system

An e-commerce retailer wants better recommendations. The CONOPS set data sources (browsing, purchases, catalogue, inventory), expectations (50 ms latency, +15% conversion) and constraints (GDPR, explainable to marketing). The business case projected +12% average order value and −8% cart abandonment (all figures from the book's example). Scenarios were chosen because each forced a design decision: real-time browsing (speed vs model complexity), email personalisation (getting names and identifiers right), and inventory-aware recommendations (fresh data, out-of-stock handling). Break things and see the recovery design work.

Lessons learnedCustomer IDs were tracked inconsistently across platforms, so the design gained data cleaning and identity resolution. Marketing expected human-level personalisation on day one and had to learn how accuracy grows with data. Legacy inventory systems had different data models and update latencies, so a synchronisation layer was added to decouple them.
