The Case for Architecture

Nobody would build a hospital without an architect. AI-enabled software is at least as complex, and the architect is the person who turns many competing wishes into one coherent vision that can actually be built.

A thought experiment: four engineers, no architect

Picture a small app: query a networked data store and show results to a remote user. Four engineers: one on the data store, one on the service layer, one integrating, one on the UI. Scale that up to an AI-enabled system, with far more domains and stakeholders whose wishes clash, and someone has to own the unified vision, keep the design buildable, and land it on budget and schedule. That is the architect.

2

An ancient role, a modern need

The word comes from Greek roots for "first" or "chief" and "builder": the person who brings a concept into a system that serves a purpose. Tap through the eras.

Unlike a building, software cannot be captured in one drawing. It needs several views: logical models and functional specs, operational scenarios and use cases, interface controls and service agreements, and prototypes, simulations and analyses. Teams of architects collaborate with domain specialists, but one responsible architect keeps decision authority; design by committee is a known pitfall.

3

What an architect actually does

Architects are sometimes mocked as "document creators". The documents are real (specifications, concepts of operations, diagrams, white papers, technology evaluations), but they are the output of thinking and collaboration done first. Without a central role, specialists optimise for their own speciality: the database engineer for data structures, the UI designer for experience. Many systems fail not because a part was built badly but because the wrong system was built.

Extra duties for AI-enabled systemsExplain how the AI creates value; give data scientists more than standard diagrams (performance simulations, decision maps, state machines); work closely with AI engineers on scalable, controllable pipelines; identify the non-functional requirements, tactics and patterns for decisions made without a human; and guide testers to check decision correctness, including off-nominal inputs.
4

The vision holder and the 5W+H framework

Choosing lunch with two friends is a tiny version of the job: you decide, but you must weigh their budgets, allergies and tastes, and produce one choice that works for everyone. The architect does this for a whole system. To move between the big picture and critical detail, the book suggests six questions, each weighed for cost, schedule and performance. Pick a question to see it applied to a bank's fraud-detection system.

The vision is communicated through a concept of operations, high-level use case diagrams, logical and functional decompositions, plain-language narratives for non-technical stakeholders, and data models. For AI systems the vision must also say how algorithmic decisions are checked, how good the data must be, and how people come to trust automated operation.

5

The architectural cycle

Architecture is iterative: analysis decomposes functions and finds the key non-functional requirements and metrics; synthesis creatively assembles feasible concepts using patterns, tactics and candidate technologies; evaluation sorts and prioritises them with models, analysis, decision matrices and, where uncertainty is high, prototypes. Then the concepts go to review, where stakeholders make the final call.

6

Thinking like an architect: a hybrid fraud detector

The book's worked example: a bank wants AI to catch fraud. A deep network is good at spotting odd transaction patterns but cannot give the chain of reasoning compliance demands. A symbolic (rule-based) approach can explain itself but misses subtler anomalies. The architect's answer is to use both: the deep net as a fast first filter, the symbolic engine to classify and explain what it flags. Try the three designs on the same 48 simulated transactions (red dot = actually fraudulent).

7

Choosing the AI approach

The book offers three broad paths, judged along three dimensions: business alignment (value, operating cost, risk), data science (data readiness, accuracy targets, how models will evolve) and technical constraints (compute, integration, latency). Answer three questions for a first-cut recommendation.

Balance novelty against practicality: is the technology production-ready, can your team build and maintain it, and what happens when the AI component fails or behaves oddly?

8

The language of architecture: heuristics, tactics, patterns

Following Software Architecture in Practice, the book names three tools. Heuristics are experience-based rules of thumb that cut complexity. Tactics solve one specific problem. Patterns are reusable templates combining tactics for bigger problems. Sort these.

9

Governance, explainability and compliance by design

Governance is not paperwork added at the end; it shows up as components. Governance needs bias mitigation in the pipeline, human-in-the-loop review points for high-stakes decisions, data provenance and versioning, and audit logs of decisions and inputs. Explainability needs interpretability layers, confidence scores, decision tracing and transparent interfaces. Regulation (GDPR, CCPA, HIPAA, algorithmic accountability rules) adds consent, access and deletion features. Switch on the components and watch the same AI answer become trustworthy.

10

Modeling, simulation and interfaces

Here modeling means any artifact you can reason with: UML class, sequence and state diagrams; decision trees; functional charts and N-squared diagrams; statistical summaries; analytical models that bound what is physically possible (a truck cannot exceed road speeds); data models; UI mock-ups. For AI specifically: histograms, box plots and correlation matrices, hypothesis tests to set expected data ranges and build "circuit breakers", and decision maps. Start a simulation on day one and grow it with the system: feed it known-bad sensor data, high transaction volumes, or drifting weather data to test decisions, storage and retraining.

Interfaces are where cohesion is won or lost. One team's change can quietly break another's, and one system's raw data is often another's processed metadata. An AI component cannot re-verify everything it receives, so it trusts the interface contract for format, scale, timing and correctness. That is why the architect, more than anyone, should control interfaces. Hover or tap the contract fields.
