Coordinated Batch Processing

Some steps need all their inputs before they can run, or need to combine many results into one. Join and reduce are the coordination patterns behind MapReduce.

1

Fan out, then pull back together

Chapter 12 was about splitting and copying work. This chapter is about the opposite: gathering many parallel outputs into one result. The canonical case is MapReduce: the "map" step is a sharded work queue, and the "reduce" step is coordinated processing that shrinks many outputs to one. Two coordination patterns cover it: join and reduce.

2

Join: nobody leaves until everyone is done

A merge just blends queues; it never knows if the set is complete. Sometimes completeness matters. Rendering an animated film, every frame can be drawn in parallel, but turning 30 frames into one second of video needs all 30. A join (barrier synchronization, like joining threads) releases nothing until every parallel item is finished. The cost: the whole stage waits for its slowest item. Render a second of film.

5% of renders hit a slow machine

3

Reduce: merge as you go

Reduce also combines parallel outputs, but optimistically: each reduce step merges a few outputs into one output of the same shape, so steps can repeat in a tree until one answer remains, and they can start while mapping is still running. Each step also shrinks the data to just what the answer needs. Try the book's word count: type some text, shard it into "pages", then reduce.

4

Sum and histogram reductions

Counting adds 1 per item; sum adds a value carried in the data. To total a country's population, shard towns by state, shard again by county, and emit (town, population) tuples. The reducer doesn't care about the two levels of sharding: it adds any two tuples into one, e.g. (Seattle–Northampton, 4,025,000), and repeats.

A histogram looks harder to merge: town A says 45% of families have two children, town B says 20%. Averaging the percentages is wrong unless the towns are the same size. Weight each histogram by its population (from the sum), add, divide by the combined population, and the result is again a histogram that can be merged further.

5

Everything together: counting vehicles in traffic photos

The book closes Part IV with a pipeline that uses nearly every batch pattern. Input: a long list of image URLs of highways at rush hour. Goal: blur license plates, count cars, trucks and motorcycles, and tally vehicle colours. Step through it.

// one image's output, and the reducer that merges two of them
{ "vehicles": {"car": 12, "truck": 7, "motorcycle": 4},
  "colors":   {"white": 8, "black": 3, "blue": 6, "red": 6} }

function reduce(a, b) {         // same shape in, same shape out
  return { vehicles: addCounts(a.vehicles, b.vehicles),
           colors:   addCounts(a.colors,   b.colors) };
}
