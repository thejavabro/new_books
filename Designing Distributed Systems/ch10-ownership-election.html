Ownership Election

Sometimes exactly one replica must own a task or a shard. Building that safely on a compare-and-swap key-value store is subtle, and the failure modes are the lesson.

1

Scaling assignment: who owns this?

The earlier serving patterns scale requests and data. This one scales assignment: making sure one specific process owns one specific task, such as a shard of keys or the job of scheduling. On one machine you would use a mutex, backed by shared memory and the CPU. Across machines there is no shared memory, so ownership must be built as a distributed lock. The owner is usually called the leader. Fail the leader and watch.

2

First, ask whether you need election at all

The simplest owner is a singleton: run one replica and it owns everything. On Kubernetes, a crashed container restarts in seconds, a hung one is restarted if you have a health check, and a dead machine's work moves elsewhere in about five minutes. For background work that can be plenty. But rollouts mean downtime too, since old and new versions cannot overlap. Estimate your singleton's availability.

The catch with "pretty good"Averages hide the shape. When a singleton is down, everything is down. For a background job that is fine; for a game, a shop or a bank, even a few minutes of total outage hurts. That is when you need several replicas with one elected owner.
3

The building block: compare-and-swap with a TTL

Don't implement Paxos or Raft yourself; the book compares that to building locks from assembly instructions. Use a store that already has: etcd, ZooKeeper or Consul. They offer compare-and-swap (write the new value only if the current value is what you expect), a TTL after which a key empties itself, and a version that changes with every write. 4

Why unlock must check the version

A TTL fixes one problem (a crashed holder never releases the lock) and creates another. Step through the book's scenario, then flip the switch to see how checking the resource version on unlock repairs it. Also: keep a watchdog that crashes your process if the TTL passes before you unlock, so you never keep working on a lock you lost.

5

Long-term ownership: renewable leases

Some ownership lasts as long as the process lives: in a highly available Kubernetes control plane, only one scheduler replica is active. A week-long TTL would mean a week without a leader after a crash. Instead, take a short lease and renew it every TTL/2. If a renewal fails, handleLockLost() stops everything the lock protected; the simplest version exits and lets the orchestrator restart the process as a follower. Freeze the leader for a while and see what happens.

6

Two leaders for a moment: make the receiver check

Even with renewals, a frozen leader can wake up and act for an instant before it notices the lease is gone. Re-checking the lock before guarded code (say, only trust it for 75% of the TTL) shrinks the window but cannot close it. So the receiver double-checks: the lock store also records who holds the lock, and each request carries the sender's identity and the lock's version. Step through the nastiest case.

The final trade-offYou can have at-most-once processing, at the price of sometimes being unavailable while ownership is uncertain, or at-least-once, which stays up but occasionally does work twice. Double-running a GPU job wastes money; double-running a money transfer is unacceptable. And whatever you choose, use a production-grade lock library if your language has one.
