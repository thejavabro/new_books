ADM Techniques

The ADM tells you what to produce. The techniques tell you how. Nine of them, each with a small tool to try.

1

Technique or no technique?

TOGAF's "techniques" are not always methods in the strict sense. Some are recurring topics that need elaboration to be applied properly. Stakeholder Management and Risk Management are essential practices, not neat procedures. The standard names eight. Tap a card for where it lives in the ADM.

2

Principles: rules that settle arguments in advance

Principles give clarity and direction to decisions, align business and IT strategy, and standardize approaches across teams. There are two families. Enterprise Principles are broad and shape strategy and culture, such as "comply with laws and regulations". Architecture Principles are narrower and guide architecture work, such as "common vocabulary". The Enterprise set bounds the Architecture sets, which often exist per segment. Architects draft Architecture Principles with stakeholders, and the Architecture Board approves them.

Build a principle

Every principle has four parts. Pick a draft and see whether it would pass review.

3

Stakeholder Management in five steps

A stakeholder is anyone who can affect, or feels affected by, the architecture work. They supply requirements, raise concerns and can block or carry the change. The analysis starts in Phase A and is refreshed in every later phase, especially E, F and H.

The Power Grid

Tap a stakeholder to move them around the grid. Their classification and the communications plan update. Hospital roles are from the book's running example.

The author's view: the standard limits the Communications Plan to key stakeholders. Communicating the expected impact to the whole organization, including everyone's role, makes the architecture belong to everyone.
4

Architecture patterns

Building blocks are what you use. Patterns tell you how, when and why to combine them, and which trade-offs come with it. A pattern is a proven combination of one or more ABBs and SBBs, and it is not the same as a software design pattern. TOGAF has not formally built patterns into the ADM, but phases B to D, where reusable blocks are identified, are the natural place to define them. Tap cells to compose a pattern.

5

Gap analysis: the matrix that catches omissions

The steps are:

  • Set baseline against target and compare them.
  • Sort the gaps into missing functions, technology gaps or process inefficiencies.
  • Group the remedies into work packages.
  • Prioritize the packages.

Gaps are found in B, C and D. The real analysis is in E. In the matrix, baseline building blocks are the rows and target building blocks are the columns, with an extra New row and an Eliminated column. Tap cells to mark them. The hospital case is preloaded.

6

Interoperability: how deeply must things share?

Interoperability is the ability to share information and services. A cross-mapping between entities records the degree each pair needs. The pairs can be stakeholder to stakeholder in Phase B, or stakeholder to application, and so on. Letters refine a degree, for example 3C. Tap a cell to cycle its degree.

7

Business Transformation Readiness

A recruitment app might shrink headcount, and a change-averse culture can sink a sound design. So before phases E and F, HR, business lines and planners jointly score the readiness factors. The scale is 1 to 5: poor, fair, acceptable, good, excellent. Each factor also gets an urgency and a remediation difficulty, and the findings go into the Implementation and Migration Plan. Adjust the hospital's scores.

8

Risk Management and the heat map

Risk work runs through A and E to H. A risk is initial when it is new and residual when it is what remains after mitigation. Classify it:

  • by type: time, cost, scope, contractual, technology, people;
  • or by domain: business, information, application, technology.

Then score impact and frequency from 1 to 5. Risk = impact × frequency. Note that the risk is to the organization, not to the architecture work. Tap a cell to place a risk, then apply mitigation.

Impact 5 to 1: catastrophic, significant, moderate, low, negligible. Frequency 5 to 1: frequent, probable, occasional, seldom, unlikely.

Mitigation must make a real difference. Going from catastrophic to critical is still extreme. Residual risks need sign-off through the Governance Framework, and then the mitigating actions are monitored.
9

Architecture Alternatives and Trade-Offs

Seldom does one option satisfy every stakeholder. The method works in three moves:

  • Gather criteria from the vision, principles, requirements and concerns.
  • Explore alternatives against them.
  • Discuss the pros and cons with stakeholders and agree.

The criteria usually include flexibility, time and cost, benefit realization and risk. The example is Lemon-A-de, a lemonade maker that cannot pursue both goals at once.
