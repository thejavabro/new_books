Why TOGAF, and Where It Came From

The TOGAF Standard is the most used architecture framework in the world and one of the most misunderstood. Start with what it boils down to, why people say they don't use it, and how it grew from a US defense model into a full standard.

1

TOGAF in a nutshell

The standard is big: six core documents, dozens of guides, hundreds of artifacts. Jager's shortcut is to say it all boils down to one thing: establishing and running an Architecture Capability. That capability is carried out by architecture roles, and those roles lean on architecture skills and competencies. Everything else in the standard serves that chain.

What the capability actually does follows a simple arc. Step through it.

Tailoring is a mustNone of this needs to be used in full. Pick the parts that fit your situation and adapt them. Keep that in mind and, as the author puts it, the framework suddenly feels a lot less overwhelming.
2

Why people say they don't use it

TOGAF is the de facto standard, and it is also routinely dismissed. The author collected three complaints over the years. Tap each to see how the book answers it.

A fair point the author acceptsThe standard mostly describes what to do, not how. The Series Guides help a little, but practical "how" is thin. This book's aim is to fill that gap with steps and worked examples.

From TAFIM spin-off to Standard: the editions

The Open Group published the first version in 1995 and kept to a yearly rhythm for a while. Drag the slider through the releases.

The "Technical Edition" hangoverVersion 7 (2001) carried the subtitle Technical Edition. Version 8 swapped it for Enterprise Edition a year later, but the idea that TOGAF is "an IT framework" stuck around for more than two decades.
6

TAFIM to ADM: match the phases

TOGAF grew out of the US Department of Defense's Technical Architecture Framework for Information Management. Its 1996 phases already look like the ADM under different names. Tap a TAFIM phase, then the ADM phase it became.

0 of 7 matched.

One phase became threeTAFIM's single Target Architecture phase was split into Business, Information Systems and Technology. In the 10th Edition the three are described with the same recipe again, perhaps a first step back toward one phase.
7

2025: the ADM loses its arrowheads

The Technical Corrigendum of May 2025 was mostly textual cleanup, with one visible change: the arrowheads were removed from the ADM drawing. Arrows suggest a one-way conveyor belt. Without them, the picture says what the text always said: phases are revisited, iterated and run in whatever order the work needs.
