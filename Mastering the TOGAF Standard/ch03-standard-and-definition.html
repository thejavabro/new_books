The Standard and What EA Means

What exactly is "the TOGAF Standard"? Six core documents, a fast-moving set of guides and a library around them. Then the awkward bit: the standard carries three different definitions of Enterprise Architecture.

1

What the TOGAF Standard is

The TOGAF Standard is an open, product- and vendor-independent Enterprise Architecture framework maintained by The Open Group, a consortium that builds standards for openness, interoperability and consensus. Reduced to essentials it is two things: a stable Fundamental Content and a fast-moving set of Series Guides, all held in the wider TOGAF Library.

The structure of the standard mirrors an Architecture Capability inside an organization. Business needs from the market flow in as problems; solutions and refined needs flow out and reshape the business vision. Tap the rings.

2

Six documents, adopt what you need

The core is six documents. They form one coherent whole, but you are allowed, even encouraged, to cherry-pick: start with the ADM and leave the capability material for later, or blend parts of TOGAF into a framework you already run. Pick a scenario and see which documents it uses first.

3

Series Guides and the Library

The Fundamental Content is designed to be durable. The Series Guides are where the standard moves fast: industry-, style-, purpose- and problem-specific guidance that The Open Group Architecture Forum publishes incrementally. Not every guide fits every situation, but architects should know what exists. The Library sorts its material into categories. Tap one.

Where the Library sitsTOGAF Library ⊂ The Open Group Library. Inside an organization, the TOGAF Library feeds the Reference Library class of the Architecture Repository (page for chapter 7), so templates and patterns are at hand when new architectures are made.
4

Why business capabilities show up everywhere

A change in the business or operating model usually demands new business capabilities. The book's example: a video rental chain that becomes a streaming service. Flip the switch.

6

One standard, three definitions

Definitions should leave no room for interpretation. Yet the TOGAF Standard, directly or indirectly, carries three definitions of Enterprise Architecture. Tap each and check it against the author's test: does it mention the link to strategy and business goals?

EA is not IT ArchitectureBecause EA grew up in the technology corner, people still lump it with IT Architecture. The author is blunt: EA is a strategic business management instrument. Its diagrams and matrices answer stakeholder questions; they don't specify technical solutions. EA coordinates all domains and leaves Technology Architecture work to IT architects.
7

Describe an apple

Ask someone to describe an apple and they will often say "a round fruit with a stem that contains vitamins." That also describes a cherry or a grape. Add clauses one at a time and watch the candidates shrink. A loose definition of EA has the same problem: it lets everyone mean something different.

The practical conclusionThere is no single definitive definition. So write down how your organization practises and represents EA, and remove the ambiguity locally.
