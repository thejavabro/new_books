Phases G, H and Requirements

The architecture is now being built by others. Phase G keeps the build true to the design, Phase H decides what to do when the world changes, and Requirements Management runs through everything.

1

Phase G: the plan meets reality

In Phase G the vision becomes operational reality, built by others. Architects work with project managers to keep solutions true to the architecture, manage changes during implementation, and keep assessing progress. It uses the governance set-up from chapter 8. Its steps:

  1. Confirm scope and priorities, using the Work Package Portfolio Map to pick initiatives with the most value.
  2. Identify deployment resources and skills, and the tools needed.
  3. Guide development of solutions deployment: project scope, strategic requirements, change requests.
  4. Perform architecture compliance reviews regularly against standards and principles.
  5. Implement business and IT operations: service delivery, skills, training.
  6. Perform a post-implementation review; deviations become change requests.
2

Try a compliance review

A project team brings its design to review. Tick what the design does; see what the board would likely decide. The Architecture Contract is the yardstick.

3

Measuring progress: work packages

Two things are worth tracking: completion of work packages and achievement of goals and objectives. For work packages, give each activity a percentage. The basic method splits evenly; the refined method weights by duration, which is more realistic. Set the durations and tick what is done.

4

Measuring progress: goals and objectives

Average the progress of the work packages linked to each goal. The book's example: Goal A has packages A (100%) and B (75%) giving 87.5%; Goal B has C (50%, on hold), D and E (0%) giving 16.7%. Move the sliders.

Pie charts per work package, spider or bar charts all work. Pick the form your stakeholders read best.

5

Phase H: when the world changes

Architecture is never static. Phase H keeps watching strategy, the business environment and technology, assesses each change's impact, and decides how to handle it while disturbing operations as little as possible. Its steps: establish a value realization process, deploy monitoring tools, manage risks, analyse what monitoring and feedback show, develop change requirements, manage the governance process, and activate change.

A common way to sort changes (from the TOGAF Standard itself) is by impact: a simplification change handled by change management, an incremental change that may need a partial re-run, and a re-architecting change that needs a new Request for Architecture Work and a new ADM cycle. Pick an event.

6

Requirements Management: the hub in the middle

Not a phase but a continuous activity. Requirements are captured, stored in the Requirements Repository, prioritized, versioned and made available when any phase needs them. When a requirement changes, assess its impact and decide: deal with it in the current cycle, or park it for a later one. Watch a requirement travel.

7

The whole ADM on one wheel

The book closes chapter 12 with every phase's steps in brief. Remember: possible steps, not mandatory ones, in no fixed order. Tap a phase.
