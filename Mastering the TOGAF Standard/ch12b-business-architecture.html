Phase B: Business Architecture

Phase B describes how the business works today and how it needs to work tomorrow: capabilities, value streams, organization, information and the gap between them.

1

Business Architecture comes first

Business Architecture gives multi-dimensional views of capabilities, end-to-end value delivery, information and organization, and how they relate to strategy, products, policies, initiatives and stakeholders. Strategy says where to go and how success is measured; it doesn't say how to get there. Business Architecture does. Because data, applications and technology all build on it, it should normally be the first architecture activity.

Phase B has two objectives: craft the Target Business Architecture that meets the drivers from Phase A, and find candidate roadmap components by pinpointing gaps between baseline and target. Reuse existing material as much as possible.

2

One recipe for B, C and D

Since the 10th Edition, phases B, C and D share the same steps. Learn them once here. Step through with Business Architecture as the example.

3

Business capability heat mapping

Colour a capability map by an attribute such as maturity, performance, value or cost. In a maturity heat map, green meets the target level, yellow is one level short, red is two or more short, and purple marks a desired capability that doesn't exist yet. Tap a capability to change its current maturity (0 = not in place). There is no science here: it is stakeholder alignment plus common sense.

4

Value streams give capabilities a reason

Value streams supply the why; capabilities the what. Map each stage to the capabilities it needs and you can run a gap analysis on exactly the capabilities that matter for a stakeholder's value. The book's example: Patient Feedback Resolution, for patients and the quality office, aiming at fewer complaints and safety issues mitigated. Tap a stage.

5

An Organization Map is a web, not a tree

An org chart shows who reports to whom. An Organization Map shows working relationships among units, partners and stakeholder groups. It tells you who owns the capabilities, who takes part in value streams, and therefore whom to talk to. (Despite the name it is really a diagram.)

The fourth core method is the Information Map: the business's key information concepts (patient, appointment, referral, invoice) and how they connect, cross-mapped against capabilities. It stays a reference through data, application and technology work.
