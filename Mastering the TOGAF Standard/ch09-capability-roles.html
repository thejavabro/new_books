Capability, Roles and Skills

In a nutshell, TOGAF is about building an Architecture Capability. That capability is carried by roles, and roles are filled by people with the right mix of skills.

1

The ability to practise architecture

A capability is loosely "the ability of an organization to do something". The Architecture Capability is the ability to practise architecture: governance, structures, processes, roles, responsibilities and skills that make architecture work standardized, repeatable and aligned with strategy. It is not a project or a phase. It is an ongoing discipline.

TOGAF's neat trick: you build this capability the way you build any other, by running the ADM. Tap a phase to see its focus when the subject is the architecture practice itself.

In practice it is lighterIt sounds formal. In reality these steps are often settled in one or two meetings. Record the agreements and, if possible, have the main sponsor formally sign them off.
2

Where the capability reports determines what it can do

The Leader's Guide names four purposes for the capability. The higher its reporting line, the more strategic it becomes; the further toward operations, the more reactive and solution-focused. There is no right answer, but there are consequences. Slide the reporting line.

3

Job, role or assignment?

The Series Guide Architecture Roles and Skills separates three things that are constantly mixed up. A job is the formal position in your contract and hierarchy. A role is an expected set of activities tied to a skill set. An assignment is a specific task or project. Sort these.

Sort them

To cut through the confusion, TOGAF made Segment Architect a job profile, a complete package with role description and responsibilities. A Segment Architect assigned to infrastructure plays a technical role and likely sits in IT; one assigned to business issues plays a Business Architecture role. Same job, different roles.

4

Three core roles and a specialization

Three primary viewpoints, enterprise, segment and solution, are also the three core roles. The Chief Architect is a specialization of the Enterprise Architect, not a separate role. Each role maps onto one or two of the four capability purposes and branches into specializations. Tap a role.

Inconsistency alertThe Governance Framework mentions Chief, Enterprise and Domain Architects. The roles guide says Segment Architect and adds the Solution Architect. The Open Group told the author they are fixing such mismatches gradually.
5

Two ways to cut a segment

"Segment" can mean an architecture domain (horizontal: the profession's view, as TOGAF describes it) or a business pillar (vertical: the organization's view, tucked away in the roles guide). In a healthcare example the pillars are Research, Consultation and Treatment. Toggle to see what one Segment Architect owns.

Role sprawlVertical pillars mix three or four domains, and few architects master all of them. Organizations then invent roles. The author met a "Tribe Architect" in an agile organization covering business, data and application architecture for one pillar: one example of how the vertical view breeds new, often nonsensical roles.
6

Skills and proficiency levels

The roles guide (updated September 2024) lists skill categories and four proficiency levels: 1 Background (relies on specialists), 2 Aware, 3 Knowledgeable (needs specialists only in hard cases), 4 Expert. Pick a role to see an illustrative profile; the shapes are this page's simplification, not the guide's tables.

7

Competencies: what TOGAF leaves out

A competency is more than a skill: it is skills + abilities + knowledge. TOGAF lists skills but no competencies, and the author fills the gap with ten. He also tells of leaving a contract where the organization saw EA as IT work: his strategic competencies didn't match what they wanted. Agree upfront on the perspective, then align skills and competencies.
