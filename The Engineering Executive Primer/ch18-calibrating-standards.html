Calibrating Your Standards

High standards are a vice executives are allowed to admit to, and they quietly cause friction. Escalate cautiously, generate excitement instead, and model, document and share what good looks like.

1

Socially acceptable vices

Larson overworked for years: always near burnout, with no energy left for the changes every company goes through. He did good work, handled change badly, and became known as hard to manage. He learned this mostly by seeing it in people he managed, then in himself, and joined Stripe aiming to pace himself to be more valuable after four years, not exhausted after two as at Uber.

Overwork belongs to a category of socially acceptable professional vices that quietly hurt careers. The one he's seen harm the most executives: holding the people around you to higher standards than the organization supports. Go too far and, instead of being the bar raiser, you become the problem.

3

What you're really escalating

The underperformer's manager almost always already knows, and has chosen not to act. So when you take a peer's performance to the CEO, you aren't bringing news; you're trying to hold the CEO accountable for not solving a known problem. And sometimes the lower bar is deliberate: a small QA team only meant to cover one legacy mobile app. Larson recalls a Growth Engineering org seen as second-tier, with a lower hiring bar and quality bar, that moved very fast, took direction changes with good humor, and solved problems the rest of Engineering avoided. He once saw it as bad leadership; he now sees a trade-off.

4

Escalate cautiously; generate excitement instead

If peers aren't meeting your standards, you are disagreeing with how the CEO does their job. That's normal but needs care: ask why they're comfortable with the status quo, add context on the impact, and expect slow change. More effective: put constructive energy toward a positive outcome. Enough excitement about the direction gives a resolution-avoidant manager a way to fix the problem without confronting it. Is it unfair that you have to compensate for a peer? Yes; that's the manager's job. But work isn't a perfect place, and success means finding a path among the options that actually exist.

5

Model, Document, Share

When the CEO is open to improvement in an area but won't invest in it, role model the standard visibly. Larson's playbook from his middle-management years:

1ModelDo it to a high standard on your own team: dig into a problem area, run hiring that considers internal candidates, run manager training. Iterate until it works.
2DocumentWrite how to do it and why, framed as evaluating whether it works rather than certainty it's better.
3ShareSend it to teams you want to influence. Engage those who are interested; let the others go.

6

Adapting your standards

Early on, Larson filed "lowering my standards" under lessons not worth learning; rigid high standards would make him successful. Looking back, that seems hubristic. A long career needs more flexibility than a 25-year-old imagines: sometimes work is the priority, sometimes family or friends need most of your energy. When your standards exceed your org's (or just your CEO's), decide what matters most to you right now. The urge to make noise may never fade, but its cost grows with seniority. Some lessons may not be worth learning, but some penalties aren't worth paying either.
