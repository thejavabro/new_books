Internal Communications

As the org grows, nobody hears you by accident. A few mechanical habits, a steady weekly note, tested drafts, short messages and repetition across channels, make sure messages land.

Five mechanical practices

New executives collide with their org's existing communication norms. Larson recalls one who complained engineers didn't read email, and fixed it by emailing them an instruction to read email twice a day. It didn't work. Good internal communication isn't about literary flair or charisma. It's five mechanical habits anyone can adopt if they're consistent.

2

Maintain the drip: a weekly note

Big companies make executives seem larger than life. A short weekly email closes the distance: what you're focused on, what you're doing, and reassurance that you see people's work. Larson collects notes through the week and spends about 20 minutes compiling them, sending to his org plus an opt-in list anyone can join (pre-populated with close collaborators like the exec team). Step through his template.

Reminders: Self-reviews are due Friday 3/14; planning drafts the week after.
Quarterly planning. We've settled the allocation between B2B and consumer; teams moving are already talking with their new leads. Details in the planning doc.
Search spec. The new search-indexing spec drew strong opinions in review. We're running a two-week spike before deciding.
Also this week:
  • Incident review: payment retries during the 3/2 outage
  • Design preview for the onboarding flow
  • A great chat thread on test flakiness
As always, reply or grab time with me for questions, thoughts or concerns.
3

Extract the kernel

A frequent executive communication failure: a literal communicator argues the details of a less literal executive's question. A team presents its timeline and the CEO asks, "Can't you just use ChatGPT instead of building a custom model?" Choose how the team responds.

Yes, executives should communicate better. But they're human, and you'll get further improving how you communicate with them than blaming them.

4

Test before broadcasting

Executives spend a lot of time cleaning up messes; at least half of the self-inflicted ones Larson has seen came from unsympathetic or confusing communication. Once you have four or five managers (around 40 people), someone else should proofread every significant organizational communication: anything that changes something, decides something, or people will feel strongly about, and that reaches beyond one team. Ask individuals directly; it gets better feedback than asking a group. It feels slow, but it saves the time you'd spend explaining afterward.

5

Build the packet

Small teams can be informal. Larger ones need consistent structure for anything important: a summary that says what changed, who it applies to, what they must do, when, and where to learn more; a link to the canonical source (notifications go stale, the doc doesn't); and where to ask, both a public channel and a named person for those who won't ask publicly (often newer or junior people). Assemble an announcement.

6

Keep it short

Faced with five paragraphs, some people think "later", others "never". Either way few read it. The packet helps, but you still need to edit. Paste or type a draft to see how long it reads.

Packet version

tl;dr: Starting tomorrow (1/20), every new-service proposal needs a tech spec presented at tech spec review. Details: go/tech-spec. Questions: #tech-spec or ask Jenny.

7

Use every channel: information herd immunity

You can't make every one of 2,000 engineers know everything. Tell each of them in person and email the day before, and some will still swear nobody mentioned performance reviews. Larson's goal instead is "information herd immunity": make sure someone on every team knows, so it spreads. Turn channels on and watch coverage.
