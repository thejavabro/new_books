Engineering Onboarding

Onboarding is one of the highest-leverage productivity investments in a hiring company. Define four roles, start with a small curriculum, and resist building something too ambitious to keep running.

1

Two goals for onboarding

Companies say new engineers ramp up in three to six months. Leaders quietly believe it's longer, and know their best engineers keep getting more productive for years. Onboarding serves two goals: more engineers reasonably productive in their first three months, and a foundation for becoming highly impactful over the next few years. In rapidly hiring companies it's the highest-value investment in Engineering productivity, yet it's often treated as secondary. Watch the ramp curves.

4

Start with a small curriculum

Begin with three overviews: Engineering values and strategy, technical architecture, and spinning up the development environment. Then grow it from two directions: survey new hires a month later about what they wish had been covered, and ask managers and tech leads where new hires struggle (often cultural, not technical). That keeps you away from topics that are theoretically useful but nobody wants. Projects? Onboarding projects work very well with a lot of energy behind them, and poorly otherwise; the default of teams picking a first project usually works fine (most companies under 50 engineers do this). And don't forget a hidden part of the curriculum: getting to know your cohort.

5

Why onboarding programs fail

Not from too little effort. Most fail because someone, with great intentions, builds something complex. Step through the lifecycle.

Design for the long haulMake sure the orchestrator can run it after the launch excitement fades and the thank-you notes stop. The worst onboarding comes from trying too hard, not too little.
6

Integrating with company onboarding

Reuse company-wide sessions on users, annual goals and values rather than duplicating them. The controversial question: should the central People team also run Engineering onboarding? There's often support, since the orchestrator sees a chance to move on. Larson is wary.

7

When to prioritize onboarding

Most valuable when you hire a steady stream: at around 10 new hires a month, classes beat one-on-one ramping, especially as hiring accelerates and the existing team is busy interviewing and out of practice. Even with modest hiring, ramping people well pays. The one case against investing: you hire so rarely that materials go stale before they're used.
