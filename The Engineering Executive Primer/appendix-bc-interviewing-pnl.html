Appendix B: Avoid the unicorn search

You may not run an Engineering executive search, but you'll be asked for advice, and sometimes you'll hire your own successor. First trap: companies that evaluate well but want a rare intersection of skills. Every requirement shrinks the pool, the error bars are wide, and passed-on candidates rarely come back, so a long narrow search can reject your best options before you widen it.

The fixBefore the formal search, have the sponsor (usually the CEO) talk with seasoned Engineering executives, not to hire them but to pressure-test the profile and what would make the role compelling. A few weeks that save months.

Vibes versus the kitchen sink

Executive loops can't be calibrated by many hires, and nobody inside is fully qualified to judge the most senior technical leader, though everybody has opinions. Most companies swing between two flawed formats.

Four areas to evaluate

There's no universal checklist on purpose: most current CTOs and VPs are the wrong fit for your role. Evaluate the specifics. Tap each area for where its signal comes from.

Expect five or six candidates before you find one you're excited about. Not excited after 10+? The search is off the rails.

Appendix C: Anatomy of a P&L

Larson's manager once told a bored Larson to learn to read a P&L. It turned out to be genuinely useful: a P&L is a map of the company's operation. The book walks through HashiCorp's S-1 summary data (its figure tables aren't reproduced here). Below is a made-up software company for practice; numbers in thousands, like most filings. Tap a row label for its definition and drag the sliders.

History vs forecastInternal or startup P&Ls often slide into an optimistic forecast; find where. Public filings (S-1, 10-K) are historical only, though odd fiscal years can make them look like forecasts (GitLab's FY2021 ended January 31, 2021).
GAAP vs non-GAAPGAAP follows the FASB's rules. Non-GAAP varies by company (often excluding one-time costs) and can clarify operations, but always crafts a narrative. Ask Finance to walk you through exactly how it differs.

Reading it: growth rates and surprises

Copy the P&L into a spreadsheet and add year-over-year growth columns. Then: (1) relate revenue to cost of revenue per business line, (2) for private P&Ls, compare history against forecast, (3) write down what surprises you, (4) plan how to investigate. Other observations: S&M grew about $50m in absolute terms both years (where's it going?); G&A exceeded R&D in 2019 and doubled '20→'21; the business is loss-making but losses grew slower than revenue. And always ask how much revenue is renewal versus new: a healthy renewal rate means a much smaller Sales team can drive growth.

Route each question to its owner

Group the surprises by who can answer them, send the questions, then meet. Sort these (they're from Larson's analysis).

Keep doing itYour understanding just pauses until the next P&L. Find public filings on the SEC's EDGAR search (S-1s, 10-Ks); read the raw numbers before the investor-relations narrative. Early-stage P&Ls are often wrong (miscategorized costs, one-time revenue booked as recurring), but wrong in instructive ways.
