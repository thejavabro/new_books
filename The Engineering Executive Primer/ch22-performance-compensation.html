Performance and Compensation

No performance process satisfies individuals, managers, the People team and executives at once. Keep rubrics concise, run calibration well, understand how pay bands are built, and avoid chasing perfection.

1

Everyone wants something different

Uber's original review, "T3B3", was radically simple: write someone's top three strengths and top three weaknesses and tell them in person. Google, at the other end, has stories of engineers spending months on promotion packets that don't succeed. Both have fans and critics. Most processes lack T3B3's clarity of purpose because they're balancing four sets of stakeholders. Pick a process and see who it serves.

Individuals want growth feedback and fast promotions and pay; managers want fair feedback and to keep their commitments, quickly; the People team wants a quality floor, documentation for legal and performance management, and fairness; executives need consistent promotion decisions across uneven managers and a fixed budget well spent. No process with hundreds of people serves them all elegantly. Scores are illustrative.

2

Whose feedback to collect

The baseline is written manager feedback plus a promotion decision. Upward feedback is cheap (one manager each), even if people are reluctant to criticize. Peer feedback can be expensive (well-connected people get 10+ requests and find it hard to decline) and its value depends entirely on the team's culture.

3

Titles, levels, and leveling rubrics

You can't agree on ratings or promotions without written criteria per level. Levels often start at 3 for engineers because companies share levels across functions. For titles, use the boring standard ladder; novel titles complicate hiring and scare off candidates. For rubrics, borrow a good public one and adapt it.

4

How calibration works

Managers nominate; calibration ratifies for consistency. A typical flow for several hundred engineers has three rounds, each taking involved managers three to five hours.

Outcomes track manager comfortRun practice calibrations for new and newly joined managers. Push through managers who filibuster; limit the damage from managers who present poorly. Your biggest contribution in any cycle is feedback that makes managers better next cycle.
Promotion committees?Separate committees for senior roles let Staff-plus engineers judge Staff-plus promotions. The cost: heavier process and a gap between what the manager said and what the committee decided, which feels arbitrary.
5

Demotions: rare, real, sometimes right

Companies rarely document "down leveling", but it happens. Constructive dismissal rules mean demotions need the same documentation as a dismissal. Larson avoided them for a long time but found them useful, for example with a mis-leveled hire: keep an L6 performing like an L5 and you undermine every future promotion discussion. Compare the three variants.

6

Processes raise the floor, not the ceiling

Performance processes aim for "worst case, decent feedback once a year", not "best case, life-changing feedback". Consistency-focused calibration can make it hard to reward, say, a few high-value QA testers in a large engineering org; Uber's early pure-discretion compensation could reward stars but had no defense against bias. So don't rely on the process to deliver feedback: give it continuously. If senior people only hear feedback at review time, something is very wrong.

Managing functions you've never worked inDrop old biases ("QA is easy"); lead anyway, because no one else will push on performance there; learn the fundamentals (watch workflows, read core texts, talk to experts); lean on respected high-judgment people; and prioritize hiring a functional leader who can act as that area's quasi-executive.
7

How compensation numbers are made

You'll usually consume a process the People team designs: review proposals, look for odd decisions, gather senior managers' input, and make spot fixes. Know the mechanics. Bands come from benchmark data that is far from objective (companies submit data using inconsistent rubrics), measured against a self-chosen peer group: pick tougher peers for higher numbers. Pay is then discussed as a compa-ratio, salary divided by the band's benchmark.

Common policies: new hires near 0.95, newly promoted near 0.9 of the new level, a cap around 1.1. Then reality: the output must fit the budget, and it's almost always the process that bends. Framing matters as much as numbers: a team used to 5–7% raises will be upset by 3% even when bands fell, unless you explain how numbers are calculated.

8

How often to run cycles

No choice makes everyone happy, which frees you to do what works.

Avoid pursuing perfectionCount the cost of each added step. Many 20-person companies give too little feedback; almost every 1,000-person company spends most of its time on artifacts of performance rather than on better feedback or the work itself.
