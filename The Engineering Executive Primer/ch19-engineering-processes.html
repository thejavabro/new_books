How to Run Engineering Processes

Every process trades quality for overhead. Choose who runs it (from Early Startup to Baseline and beyond), know that budget is the real constraint, and stay steady through process trend cycles.

1

Every process trades quality for overhead

Uber's tech spec review, the "DUCK Review" (a deliberate non-acronym), gave very valuable feedback. At first it handled one or two specs a week; six months later there was a one-to-two week wait; a year after that it was disbanded for lack of bandwidth. It failed not for lack of quality, but because it cost more to run than people were willing to pay. Larson used to think failing processes needed more nuance. He learned a good process is a deliberate trade-off between quality and overhead: a two-hour code review SLA is fast but shallow; a deep review that's too slow gets ignored. Decide how you'll run a process, then reason back to how sophisticated it can be.

2

Five patterns for who runs processes

Companies tend to move through these in order. Whatever you use now, you probably used another three years ago, and a fast-growing company will use a third three years from now. There's no right pattern, only the right one for your constraints. Tap each.

3

Making the Baseline pattern work longer

Baseline is the most common pattern: it's the default, it's simple, it looks cheaper (other patterns are pitched as freeing capacity but need new hires, and budgets are cost-driven), and it works. Uber and Stripe went well past 100 engineers on it. Larson believes many companies switch too early, copying a much bigger former employer. Below 100 engineers, try these first. Tick what you'd do.

4

The budget is the real obstacle

Operational problems with switching patterns show up only in hindsight. The visible blocker is budget. Even if three TPMs would clearly lift Engineering's impact (likely true, hard to prove in a spreadsheet), budgets anchor on relative dollars, not absolute impact. Companies move up the cost curve mostly while growing fast; otherwise it's "do more with less". And many budget by department share, so a new process function eats into G&A, which makes your peers resist. Move the split.

5

Navigating the trend cycle

Process fashions come and go: the Agile obsession (Larson once met an "Agiletect"); swings from technical management to people-centric management to "management is overhead"; process work as high-impact one year and unpromotable glue the next. Trends contain something real (downturns push cash preservation; upturns spend investor cash; people-centric management partly tracked a generational shift), but what worked hasn't become irrelevant overnight. His advice: build around one set of foundational beliefs and hold it for at least three to four years.
