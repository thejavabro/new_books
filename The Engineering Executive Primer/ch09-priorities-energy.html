Managing Your Priorities and Energy

Executives are told to put the company first, always. Larson argues for a flexible order of company, team and self, because a leader who has run out of energy helps no one.

1

Company, team, self

At Uber, Larson drilled a simple ordering into his teams: prioritize the company first, your team second, yourself third. It settled real arguments, like stressed hiring managers wanting to lower the bar ("yes, you need a hire; no, this hire isn't right for the company"), or which team's staffing request mattered most. It was especially useful in platform teams, far from the loud signal of users and revenue.

He's stopped recommending it. The flaw: the most valuable work at a company is rarely the most interesting, and oddly, often not what the company itself rewards. Engineers who quietly solved urgent, understaffed problems went unrecognized, and slowly became drained and frustrated. Watch what happens to energy under a strict company-first rule.

2

Energy management is positive-sum

People get energy in different ways: writing some code (ideally nothing production depends on), coaching, exploring, optimizing, speaking at conferences, tidying the wiki. All fine. Not because managers should do these things, but because people accomplish more overall when they get some energizing work. Pick what energizes you.

3

Eventual quid pro quo

Larson's replacement framework combines business-first priorities with the belief that becoming de-energized is his biggest risk in any job. Simulate a year: each month, decide how much energizing work to protect.

Month 1 of 18

  1. Generally put company and team priorities ahead of your own.
  2. If you're getting drained, deliberately add some energizing work.
  3. Add more until equilibrium returns.
  4. If you can't balance energy and priorities for more than a year, stop everything and fix it: change the role, or leave.
4

Mirrors of misalignment

Frameworks don't tell the whole story. Larson spent time steering Engineering toward large language models: past versions of himself would have judged that misaligned work. As an executive he sees the risk of ignoring possibly-transformative techniques. There's always another layer of context. That's why curiosity matters: people who seem to be doing something nonsensical usually know something you don't, or are missing something you know. He saw it most around planning: companies in dynamic markets plan six months in detail, then immediately change priorities. It used to infuriate him.

5

Orthogonal is fine; opposed is not

A little work that's orthogonal to company needs is fine. Work that is opposed to them you'll always regret. Sort each activity.

Aligned
Orthogonal (fine in moderation)
Opposed

Almost any outside activity is fine in moderation. Using work projects to learn non-standard tools almost never is. If you're unsure, ask a friend; being unsure is itself a bad sign.

6

Humans aren't robots

Larson long found it maddening that the right answer (why is "use boring technology" controversial?) was so often ignored. For some people, himself included, sticking to the correct path is energizing in itself. But he has rarely seen high-adherence personalities thrive in senior roles. Executives deal with unusual situations and many people, few of whom are energized by adherence, and peers almost never are. Enjoy the rare moments working with like-minded executives, and learn to mute those values the rest of the time.
