Participating in Mergers and Acquisitions

Acquisitions are rare, fast and messy. The executive's job is to make sure the deal makes sense technically, the risks are found early, and the people on both sides are looked after.

1

Why acquisitions get messy

Larson ran Engineering at Digg as it ran out of money and got acquired, then later sat on the buying side of many deals. Acquisitions are rare, anxious and fast, and even simple ones carry conflicting incentives. After one deal closed, he found the acquired engineers had been told they were coming to rescue a failing stack, while his own engineers had been told the newcomers were excited to rewrite into the standard stack. Step through how that happens.

2

Three tools for a shared perspective

Every acquisition has a hundred reasons not to work. Look only for flaws and you'll reject everything; look only for upside and everything looks rosy. A company needs three layered tools to steer between those extremes.

1 · Business strategyWhat is M&A for here?Business lines, revenue and cash expectations for each, how deals fit, which kind of deal (acquihire, product, or whole business), and what sizes. Ideally written by the CEO or exec team.
2 · Acquisition thesisWhat must be true for this deal to fit?Product capabilities, IP, book of business, revenue, cash flow, deal type, team.
3 · Engineering evaluationCan we disprove any of it?Implementation, integration, scalability, security, compliance, costs, culture.

Common strategic motives: acquiring revenue or users for the core business (Match buying Hinge); entering a new line (Stripe buying Index for point-of-sale); driving innovation (Facebook buying Instagram). And one nobody writes down because nobody wants it in court documents: reducing future competition (Larson cites Google buying Waze). If you can't work out the real strategy, ask whether it's that.

3

Running the Engineering evaluation

Keep a base template of topics, fork it for each deal and add thesis-specific questions. Request materials up front (SOC 2 reports, API docs, org charts); you won't get everything. Then hold a one-to-two hour discussion. Plan for two meetings, not six. Run the follow-ups and sync with the deal team. Templates exist because theses miss the obvious: Larson recalls JPMorgan modeling the value of Frank's customer data without checking it came from a real production database. Play the diligence game: pick topics to probe in your limited meetings.

4

A deliberately loose integration plan

Surprisingly many deals close with only an informal integration plan. Write one, but keep it loose, because detailed decisions will be specifically wrong and remade anyway. Larson's baseline:

  • Run the acquired stack "as is" for six months.
  • Tell them you aim to consolidate technologies wherever feasible.
  • Their head of Engineering reports to you (or the relevant business unit's lead for a small deal).
  • Plan to combine vertical teams like infrastructure and developer productivity.
  • Be direct with senior leaders about roles they could step into, and let people get to know them before they become anyone's new boss.

Team and leadership integrationUsually their engineering head reports to you or to the business-unit lead, and central teams like infrastructure, data or security may absorb their counterparts. Acquired leaders vary: some were struggling or can't imagine reporting to someone else; others are excellent, energized to partner, and people you could never have hired directly. Balance doing right by the acquired team with doing right by your existing one; it's easy to dig a hole if your team feels the newcomers get special treatment.
5

Dissent now or forever hold your peace

Some acquisitions define industries (Instagram, WhatsApp, Waze, Match's dating roll-up). Many more disappoint (Larson names Motorola, Skype, Goodreads). Engineering absorbs most of the operating cost and complexity, so it owes the company a clear, realistic view. Anchor concerns to company goals, not Engineering's. You'll usually have one vote, not a veto. Larson rarely regretted being overruled: usually he was missing something.

When you're the one being acquired
