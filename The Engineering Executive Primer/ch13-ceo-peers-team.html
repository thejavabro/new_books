Working with Your CEO, Peers, and Engineering

New executives get stuck between a CEO, peers and their own team who want different things. Build bridging narratives, focus on few changes, and resolve conflict instead of avoiding it.

1

The structural trap for new executives

Stories of new executives wreaking havoc are everywhere: a doomed giant migration, runaway spending that forces layoffs, firings in month one. They're told as individual failures, but they're common enough to suggest a structural trap. A new Engineering executive is usually hired by a CEO who thinks Engineering is underperforming. Engineering thinks the CEO changes direction too often. Peers and the board each have their own story. And the CEO is your boss.

2

Supported, tolerated, or resented?

The most effective executives are proactively supported. Picture pushing a monolith-to-services rewrite:

SupportedPeers come to you with concrete concerns and ideas to adjust your approach.
ToleratedThey pull your tickets into sprints but never flag structural problems.
ResentedThey tell their teams to ignore your tickets until you escalate loudly.

3

Bridging narratives

The most talented executives weave four or five apparently incompatible views into one understanding. The lazy diagnosis is "Engineering can't execute." Slowing down to gather perspectives doesn't take much longer once you're practiced, and it skips the fight over blame. Collect the voices, then see the bridge.

Executives who bridge narratives act as company-first problem solvers rather than loyalists to their own function, and that creates an expectation of cross-functional partnership.

4

Don't anchor to your last company

Many new executives alienate peers by assuming the new company works like the old one.

5

Foster an alignment habit

Feedback on your mistakes will reach you eventually, far too late. Ask for it: after an exec meeting, check with participants whether there's anything you should have done better, or not at all. Usually you'll hear nothing useful; occasionally you'll hear something important. Always say thanks and ask a follow-up. Narrow questions work best. Compare.

6

Focus on a small number of changes

Every time you bring peers an idea, they check a mental ledger of your last few. Did those go well? Are they still dragging on? Did they fizzle? Good track record, easy support; poor one, resistance. So deliver a few meaningful changes rather than many. Add ideas to your ledger and watch your credibility.

7

Conflict is fine; unresolved conflict isn't

Aiming for zero conflict is subtly wrong. Growing companies in shifting markets generate conflict; if you see none, you're probably ignoring it. New kinds of conflict are a sign of growth. What to avoid is the recurring, unresolved kind. Healthy: an exec team debating a return-to-office policy, surfacing every perspective on the way to a decision. Unhealthy: re-arguing the R&D vs Sales & Marketing split every time the financial plan is revised. Look for the unified view; failing that, use an explicit escalation path.

The structured process works only if people are held to it; otherwise it's another trap for rule followers.

8

Surviving a panicking peer

The most common way good relationships go wrong: a peer gets negative feedback from the CEO, struggles to absorb it, and starts focusing on short-term survival, often by blaming Engineering. Product blames buggy execution for flat usage; Sales blames slow delivery for missed quota; Marketing blames the website for high acquisition costs. Choose a response.

The tie-breakerWhen unsure, pick the approach that maximizes your impact at the company over the next three years, not the next three months.
