Onboarding Peer Executives

Onboarding a new executive is nothing like onboarding an engineer. Share your mental model of the company early, define roles explicitly, and accept that trust takes time.

1

Why onboarding peers matters

Companies polish Engineering onboarding, but executive onboarding is ad hoc: it happens too rarely to get good, every role is one of one, and the CEO who hired the new executive to solve a problem quickly turns to the next problem. You can look high-achieving without achieving much. But you can't truly be a high-performing executive unless your peers and CEO also do excellent work that adds up to a successful business. A story about your brilliant Engineering team held back by weak Product or Sales doesn't count. After a team-oriented CEO and careful hiring, onboarding is your best remaining tool, and missteps are far easier to prevent than repair.

Onboarding an executive isn't onboarding an engineer

Peer engineerDeep and narrowYou share a profession and often have implicit seniority. Goal: understand current process and one project well enough to ship it; two or three projects and they're ramped. Good sign: early projects done quickly to a high bar. Bad signs: slow progress, or arguing about processes before learning them.
Peer executiveShallow and broad, then deep where it's burningTrue peers, no implicit hierarchy, often no shared profession (Finance, Sales...). Goal: understand the landscape of business, team, projects and process, focused on the most critical current issues, going deep only where fires demand it.

Share your mental framework, in the first two weeks

Calm began as a meditation app. Five years later the business was far more complex, yet many people still operated as if it were the original app. Not from ignorance: their mental framework had set. Frameworks are hard to shift once formed, so help a new executive build an accurate one early. Larson suggests covering all of this within two weeks, even cancelling standing meetings. Define your roles explicitly

Strong Engineering orgs start with strong Product, Engineering and Design relationships, shaped by how their executives work together. Trust comes with time

Your first agreement won't be quite right; you'll only find out by working through hard scenarios together. And unless you book time in advance, you'll only meet on painful escalations, which strain a young relationship rather than build it. Choose a cadence.

6

Partnering with an executive assistant

Getting an executive assistant for the first time is magical and confusing. CEOs often hire one before 30 people; other leaders usually not before 50, often sharing one until about 200. Larson suggests an assistant partnering with your org by about 100 people, partly supporting the leaders under you too. It can feel odd to hand over tasks you'd deprioritized. What delegates well?
