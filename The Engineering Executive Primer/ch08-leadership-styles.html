Developing Leadership Styles

Effective executives switch between leading by policy, by consensus and by conviction, and know which one a situation needs.

1

Why executives need three styles

Larson used to resent "micromanager" CEOs who popped up, poked holes and vanished, and wished for a distant CEO who set clear process and left him alone. As an executive he came to see it differently. His earlier advice to "work the policy, not the exceptions" still holds, but every policy has an invisible last line: exceptions approved by the CTO. Policy lets a 50+ person org move without you; exceptions still land on your desk. So executives need three styles, the judgment to pick one, and the self-awareness to notice when they're leaning on their favorite.

3

The micromanagement trap

New managers learn early to fear micromanagement. Some executives carry that so far that they refuse to inspect their teams' work at all. Larson did this too: he deliberately knew less about day-to-day details, reasoning that leaders work on the system, not in it. It felt comfortable but undermined his teams. He couldn't help when they needed it, couldn't spot trouble early, and in conflicts could fall back only on policy and consensus, because conviction needs details. Meanwhile, working for a CEO without conviction means slow, muddled execution.

4

Growing the styles you avoid

Most executives lean on policy or conviction and distrust consensus. Founders and small-company leaders often see consensus as inefficient; big-company leaders may over-use it even when they have the context to act. Larson's monthly exercise is simple; the hard part is doing it.

Watch your default when context changesNew at Calm, Larson leaned on policy to cover gaps in context, a bit too long; some problems needed conviction. At Carta, the org was already excellent at policy, so he had to add a different style rather than more of the same. Shifts like product-market fit to hypergrowth, or rapid hiring to post-layoff operations, are moments to re-check your default.
5

When executives disengage

Sometimes an executive drifts into policy and consensus not by preference but because those take less context, and they've disengaged. Three common causes; tap each.

The third is hardest. Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi's flow sits where skill matches difficulty. If you're hunting for energy elsewhere, your role is probably too hard or too easy. Move the dot.
