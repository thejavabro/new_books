Your First 90 Days

The first three months are a narrow window where you are allowed to ask anything. Use it to learn the business, build relationships, spot the problems nobody mentioned, and take stock of how Engineering really works.

1

What to learn first

Michael Watkins's The First 90 Days is the classic guide to starting a role, but its advice is general. Larson goes a layer deeper for Engineering executives. Priorities differ hugely between a Series A CTO and a VP at a giant, yet discovery still needs a structure. He offers six questions, in rough order. Tap each.

You only learn when you reflectEarly in a career, problems are hard because they're new to you, and someone nearby has usually solved them. Hard work is a weak virtue; learning well is the real one. Asking early and reflecting deliberately turns a new job's flood of experiences into lessons.
2

Learning first, unless something is on fire

The textbook first 90 days are pure learning, preparing to act well on day 91. But if you uncover something deeply wrong, stop working through the list and get it fixed. The goal is to downgrade the situation from dire to distressed. Even then, don't let the fire eat all your time: executives have to succeed in the short and the long term. Move the slider to see how your first 90 days might split.

Companies are surprisingly resilient; wounds that look unbearable often aren't. And sometimes the team has lived with a dire problem so long they've stopped mentioning it, which is its own warning sign.

3

A 90-day task list, by theme

These are areas to learn, not boxes to tick. If the list feels overwhelming, pick one or two themes and go deep. Switch themes to see the suggested actions.

4

Make one or two changes, not ten

Within weeks you'll have a long list of things you'd change. The biggest mistake a new leader can make is disrupting the current systems of execution before there's a working alternative. Keep what works, iterate on what doesn't, measure as you go. Pick your changes for the first 90 days.

5

Getting a grip on the technology

You won't spend much time in the codebase, so be deliberate. Ship a trivial change all the way to production to learn how development really works. Join an on-call rotation as an extra page target. Attend incident reviews. Learn how big technical decisions really get made, formally or not. Write down the implicit Engineering strategy and ask the team if it's the one they want.

Two anti-patternsToo many opinions: a junior engineer's hot takes are taken with a grain of salt; an executive's lightly held opinions cause thrash. Too little curiosity: challenging past decisions without understanding why they were made. Record the history instead.
