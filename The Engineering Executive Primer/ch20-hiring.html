Hiring

Hiring is a system with many owners. The executive designs the process, balances budgets and leveling, and personally handles the hardest hires.

1

From interviewer to system designer

Everyone in Engineering hires. Engineers take pride in interviewing well; managers become good closers; senior managers train others and meet senior candidates. As executive you still make key leadership hires, but most of your time goes to designing and debugging the overall process. Past about 100 engineers, you'll be directly involved in only a small fraction of senior hires.

2

Seven components of a hiring process

You'll usually inherit a process. Unless everyone agrees it's broken, take part in it first; adapting what exists is easier to retrain and builds goodwill. Either way, the end state should have all of these. Tap the ones your org has.

3

Effective, not perfect

The two big executive mistakes: no process at all, or one so heavy it stops hiring. The second is hard to notice, because it feels like optimizing. Often the cause is an indecisive executive adding interviews for clearer signal (which obscures reality), or a well-meaning structured thinker trying to engineer out human judgment. Check the three warning signs.

5

Helping close key candidates

Some executives insist on being the final interviewer in every loop to protect the bar. It slows hiring badly, and even true believers eventually stop. Where executives do help is closing senior candidates: telling Engineering's story and how it fits the business. Even a familiar message weighs more from an executive, and it signals the hire matters. Two exceptions: making a sell call mandatory (some candidates are ready to accept; executives are hard to schedule), and executives who are simply bad at selling. A friend once had a sell call with an executive who was watching online videos during it.

6

Leveling and compensation

The two most contentious topics. You can't set pay without a level, so level first. Leveling only after interviews has a flaw: senior candidates need a different loop (a panel of mid-level interviewers rarely concludes someone is Staff-plus, and you wouldn't trust it if they did). So provisionally level early, for instance from the phone screen plus years of experience, tailor the loop, then finalize using a written framework when deciding on the offer. Watch an offer go through Larson's approval flow.

Centralized offer calculation trains everyone on one process and stops weak hiring managers from leaning on pay to close (so their hires end up with outsized packages).

7

Hiring prioritization is a global constraint

In fast-growing companies, recruiter bandwidth is the limit; in slower ones, headcount. Either way teams will push for priority. Larson prefers leaders solve within their constraints rather than frequently shifting them. Whatever your style, recruiter assignment and headcount are global constraints the Engineering executive must control centrally (personally or through one delegate). The simple way to keep it cheap: set headcount and recruiters per sub-org and let each leader prioritize within it.

8

Training hiring managers

Interviewers get shadowing and reverse-shadowing; hiring managers often get nothing, which causes predictable problems. Diagnose each and run focused sessions (Larson uses his managers' monthly).

9

Internal, external and network hiring

At Yahoo!, Larson watched a director hire a former colleague as manager without interviews; that manager brought in his own former tech lead; a third ex-colleague became chief architect and never read a spec or wrote a line of code. A teammate called it "the flying wedge". Hiring only from your network tells your team they and their networks aren't wanted. The mirror image, filling every important role externally, signals there's no path up. Either imbalance usually means a leader can't hire outside (or inside) their network, or can't fairly evaluate one kind of candidate. Check your org's mix.

10

Diversity: don't offload it to Recruiting

A common first step is setting targets for organizational and recent-hire composition. The antipattern: making Recruiting solely accountable. Retention and promotion matter at least as much; some companies hire diverse candidates well yet stay unrepresentative because they can't retain them. Larson suspects many orgs, half-knowingly, design systems where Recruiting carries all the accountability. See why.

Engineering brand, brieflyCompanies that invest heavily in brand swear by it; many successful ones do very little and don't miss it. A small investment captures most of the upside. The exception: companies that also sell to engineers. If your funnel is full, spend time elsewhere unless it serves another goal or energizes someone.
11

The system exists to support you

Rules-minded leaders resent executives who bypass the hiring process, Larson included: "Why build this process if we don't trust its decisions?" Then he owned hiring, and saw why perfect compliance is hard. Peer executives paid far outside bands, penalizing Engineering hires; some interviewers ignored rubrics; new roles got wildly different interpretations. Training fixes these over time, but executives rarely control the timeline. Sometimes the problem is urgent today, and the process must serve the goal, not the other way round.
