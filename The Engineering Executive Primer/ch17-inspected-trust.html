Inspected Trust

Trust alone lets problems hide. Inspection alone breeds resentment. Pairing them gives teams autonomy while keeping the executive informed early enough to help.

1

How “trust” became a management style

Engineering leaders used to start as tech lead managers: senior engineer plus people manager. Many hid in the technical half to avoid learning people management. The industry responded by splitting technical and people leadership, and new managers now hear: stop writing code, step back, trust your team. Taken too far, that becomes management by trust alone. Larson argues this well-meaning habit has undermined many talented leaders.

2

How managing through trust fails

A new executive arrives with a reservoir of trust from passing the hiring process. Many burn it fast by acting before understanding. Their manager, believing in empowerment, says nothing. Step through a story Larson says took him five minutes to find ten real examples of.

3

Trust can't tell good from bad

Trust alone can't distinguish the good and bad versions of your reports' results. Outcomes are visible; process and decisions aren't, unless you look. Toggle inspection.

4

Trust first, then investigate before acting

Trust is necessary and powerful; it's blind trust and non-intervention Larson warns against. Inspected trust means: believe there's something real in what you're told, then verify independently and look for alternative explanations before you act. Work this case.

Case

A director tells you: "The Payments team is blocking us. They won't review our integration."

Inspection runs both waysSome of the best people Larson managed inspected everything he told them, improving his rough proposals and catching him when he got attached to his own reading. And when leaders object that inspecting their team signals distrust, his answer is the same: uninspected trust is comfortable but undermines the leaders under you.

Quick check

Five questions

Not "don't trust your team"You must trust your leaders to scale. But merely trusting them abdicates one of your key jobs: helping them succeed. Pair trust with inspection to catch errors early and learn from them. It's more work, and it produces better leaders.
