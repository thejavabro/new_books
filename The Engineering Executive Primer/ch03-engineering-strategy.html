Writing Your Engineering Strategy

A strategy is not a vision deck. It is a short, honest document that names the problem, picks a direction and says how decisions will actually get made, then gets refined until people act on it.

1

What a strategy is (and isn't)

Sooner or later someone will ask the new executive, urgently, for "an Engineering strategy", without saying what they mean. Larson borrows Richard Rumelt's definition from Good Strategy, Bad Strategy: a strategy has three parts, and the last is the one that matters most, because a strategy only counts if it changes what people do.

For an Engineering executive, a useful strategy answers three things: how Engineering allocates resources against priorities (and why), the fundamental rules every team must follow, and how decisions get made.

2

A real guiding policy implies a trade-off

If a "policy" gives nothing up, be suspicious of it. "Work harder" is not a guiding policy. "Only hire for the single most urgent team; don't spread hires across all teams" is, because it says no to something. Sort these.

Real policy or wishful thinking?

3

When the company's other strategies are missing

You can't diagnose well without understanding the business and product strategies that Engineering's must fit. These almost always exist, just undocumented, or dressed up as visions. Don't try to teach the whole company to write strategy first; that delays you by quarters. Sketch the missing pieces yourself, well enough to diagnose. Tap a circle.

4

Play with the example strategy

Larson's worked example: a 350-person Engineering org (300 engineers, 40 managers, 10 TPMs) supporting three business lines. Consumer brings about 80% of revenue but grows under 15%; B2B brings 20% and most of this year's growth; "new bets" are pre-revenue with a small chance of outsized returns. The org stays cash-flow neutral at its current size. The guiding policies keep a 4:1 product-to-infrastructure ratio and split product time roughly 45% B2B, 35% consumer, 10% new bets, plus 10% for a year of developer productivity work. Try your own split and see how it reads against the diagnosis.

5

Is each policy applicable, enforced, and leverage-creating?

Strategy should be used sparingly: you could argue every roadmap and tool choice belongs in it, but that would smother teams. Larson's test for whether a guiding policy sits at the right altitude has three parts. Pick a sample policy and decide whether it passes each.

6

Three kinds of coherent action

If your policies follow the structure above, the actions fall into three groups. Larson admits they can feel "not action-y enough", because good Engineering policies last months or years and need maintenance more than one-off projects. That's fine as long as each action traces back to the diagnosis.

7

How an executive writes the strategy

As a Staff engineer, Larson advised being a historian: write five design docs and pull out what they share; consensus is what makes it enforceable. An executive doesn't need consensus to enforce, so the risks shift to a weak diagnosis and ineffective policies. That calls for a different, faster process. Step through it.

Diagnosis trapsDon't back into the diagnosis from a solution you already like (say, cancelling a microservices migration). Have two or three leaders diagnose independently and compare. Seek out skeptics in every group. And be wary if the diagnosis looks just like the one from your last job: scaling infrastructure is right with product-market fit, and probably wrong without it.
8

When to write it

Many engineers never see a written Engineering strategy. Larson's large-company employers ran on scattered rules and technical jurisdictions instead. At Calm he opened a blank "Engineering Strategy" doc, stared at it, and filed it away. A year later he wrote three short principles: choose boring technology, resolve conflict with curiosity, and prefer vendors for commoditized functionality. His advice now is to start sooner: the executive process is quick, writing it is one of the best ways to learn a new org, and you'll refresh it at least yearly anyway.
