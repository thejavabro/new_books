How to Plan

Planning starts from the company's financial plan, not from a pile of roadmap requests. Executives own how money becomes headcount, how headcount is split, and how the cycle runs without eating the year.

1

Planning is an infinite game

Plenty of leaders treat planning as a business curse word. James Carse's Finite and Infinite Games offers two ways to see it: as a finite game with fixed rules and a winner, or as an infinite game where players evolve the rules and the goal is to keep playing. Larson became an effective planner only once he saw past the stated rules. Toggle the lens and throw a surprise at the plan.

Most companies of a couple of hundred people converge on the same documented process: an annual financial and headcount plan; a quarterly (or half-yearly) plan such as OKRs or a project list; teams executing with their own methods; and often a monthly business review. What differs is the unwritten layer: for example, executives who privately lobby the CEO for headcount despite a "shared" plan.

2

Three phases, in order

Planning bloats when it tries to solve everything: security reviews, staffing checks, recruiting capacity. Those can be handled elsewhere. Planning works when it does three things well, each feeding the next. Learning something late can send you back a phase; that's expected.

3

Phase 1: the financial plan

Many engineers never see the financial plan, so executives are often surprised that it is the foundation of everything. It comes as three documents: a P&L by business line and function, a budget with detailed expenses (vendors, contractors, headcount), and a headcount plan naming roles to hire. Engineering is a stakeholder in creating it, not its owner. Its main contribution is managing its own expenses, split by business line into three buckets: headcount, production operating costs, and development costs.

The rule of thumb: spend little time on lines where revenue is accelerating faster than expenses, and a lot on the rest. Wherever expenses outpace revenue, the executive team needs a written hypothesis for when and how that reverses. Larson's sample shows two very different lines; adjust them.

5

Headcount needs an org design

Never ask for headcount without a documented structure to absorb it. Rough org math: teams of about eight, each with a manager and mission; clusters of four to six teams under an experienced manager with a focus area; keep grouping until you have five to seven groups reporting to you. Slide the headcount.

Should Engineering's growth cap the company's?Larson thinks most companies should limit overall headcount growth to Engineering's growth rate. It enforces efficiency and stops other functions from scaling faster and swamping Engineering with requests. Exceptions exist: Uber grew city operations teams fast with tools that didn't overload Engineering.
Don't argue over hires you can't makeCompare the hiring plan with historical recruiting capacity. If the plan can't be filled, the debate is moot. In hypergrowth this gap is common and teams end up hoarding unfillable headcount.
6

Renewing vendor contracts

Big companies have procurement. At a small one you may learn that a critical contract expires in two weeks. Know what each side values.

What vendors value
  • Money, ideally guaranteed
  • Long, preferably exclusive commitments
  • Help hitting quotas or selling higher-margin items
  • Sometimes payment timing
  • Sometimes co-marketing
What you want
  • Volume discounts: rate falls as spend grows
  • Commitment length matching when you'd consider switching (default one year)
  • Credit for last term's outages and service issues

Game plan: check who still uses the product (often nobody), list issues to fix in renewal, agree the negotiation strategy with whoever signs before the first call.

7

Phase 2: functional portfolio allocation

From your strategy: how much Engineering capacity goes to stakeholder requests versus internal priorities, month by month, across headcount, vendors and contractors? Review all investments about yearly, update the list as work completes, and adjust the steady-state target. Three cautions: keep the allocation fairly steady (each change is disruptive, and frequent changes turn teams into zero-sum competitors); allocate at a coarse granularity so leaders can shift within their area; and don't over-index on early results.

8

Phase 3: agreeing the roadmap

Format barely matters; a four-column sheet (project, opportunity, cost, owning team) works. Use whatever is already proposed. Roadmaps fail for other reasons. Tap each.

9

The planning calendar

Annual budget at the end of the prior year; functional planning rolling through the year; quarterly planning in the weeks before each quarter. Claire Hughes Johnson's warning from Stripe: planning expands to fill the time you give it. Change the planning window and watch the year shrink.

10

Diagnose a planning process

Even strong executive teams run planning that goes wrong, through clashing goals, quiet defection from team goals to personal ones, and small tactical errors. Tap the symptoms you recognize.

Only the CEO can hold executives accountable for these behaviors. Raise them politely; pushing too hard backfires (see chapter 18).
