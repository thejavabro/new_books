Leaving the Job

Plan your succession years ahead, decide on the basis of learning and energy rather than optics, follow an agreed communication plan, and then genuinely get out of the way.

1

When the frustration won't switch off

Two executive friends at dinner trade horror stories (behind on initiatives, layoffs, disarray), laugh, and change the subject. If you're the one who can't change the subject, it may be time to think about leaving. Executive departures are messier than IC ones and shape your resume; many execs stay in jobs they hate to "make it to two years". Larson increasingly frames the decision around managing your own energy.

2

Succession planning starts years earlier

Life comes at you fast: Larson had a stroke in his mid-thirties with an infant at home, and his team ran fine without him because of ongoing, lightweight succession planning. Toggle the practices.

The CEO picks your replacement (see Appendix B), but without this prep an internal successor won't even be seriously considered, a shame when so many companies already have the talent.

3

Four questions for deciding

Rage-quitting has a certain clarity. Executive exits don't: a frayed dream, a pile of small frustrations, people you love working with, and often an identity built on the role.

Don't trust your gut: journal your energy for a quarter

Don't anchor on the money, don't issue ultimatumsYou can often negotiate to soften the financial hit, and nobody can value equity worth "zero to tens of millions". Being an executive is an ongoing negotiation with the CEO and team; if you won't keep negotiating, move on, and remember you'll share responsibility for decisions made while you were there. Also, those people will be your backchannel references.
4

Warning signs: snacking and reminiscing

Snacking (Hunter Walk's term): easy, low-impact work. Larson's own example: starting a book club at Stripe while overhauling reliability. Some snacking restores energy; too much is avoidance. Reminiscing: under pressure, retreating to the area where you were historically strongest. A CEO wordsmithing a launch email, a CTO leaving style comments in code review. Tap the real-world examples.

If you notice either more often, ask what changed recently and face it directly.

Leave with or without your next role?

Finding the next role first removes tenure risk and gives more negotiating leverage. Executive searches are slow: be comfortable with 6+ months without income before leaving empty-handed. But the greatest risk to an executive career is running out of energy, and leaving first makes it easier to support the transition, which opens up exit packages.

8

Stop revisiting the decision

Bad meeting: "I'm out." Two days later earnings are up and you're back in, too embarrassed to tell friends you changed your mind again. Larson once had a new manager who announced in their first 1:1 that he hated the company and should quit, and spent months of 1:1s on it. Ruminating burns energy without producing an answer. Instead, set a decision date.

Closing the bookLarson ends by noting the distance between influencing an engineering organization's mechanisms and being the person ultimately accountable for them, and hopes the book helps you skip some of the pitfalls he hit.
