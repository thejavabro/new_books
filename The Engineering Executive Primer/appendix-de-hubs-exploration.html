Appendix D: Opening your second Engineering hub

Amid post-2020 flux between remote and in-office work, this appendix tackles one slice: your first Engineering office outside the founding one. Larson learned from Uber's Lithuania office, Stripe's Seattle office, and the Digg acquihire that became SocialCode's San Francisco office. Hubs are a rare chance to bootstrap your culture in a new place.

Words matterNever call it a "remote office". Recruiting people into a nascent office depends on them believing it's central to the company; "remote" undercuts that every time. Save "remote" for people working outside any office.
2

Why open a hub, and what it really costs

The case usually ties to the financial plan: lower headcount cost, faster hiring than one market allows (often for scaling companies needing infra-scale experience), or an acquisition that brings a team elsewhere. Hubs help with all three, but never quickly.

Boards and CEOs often want a hub as a miracle fix. Your job is to make them choose among real alternatives, not the beloved-but-rare low-cost hub full of experienced engineers that needs no training or travel investment.

3

Four pillars of a healthy hub

Mission, executive engagement, predictability, integration. Check what your plan includes and see how the office fares.

4

Mission: the Lithuania lesson

At Uber, on-call was so noisy that phones died mid-shift. The short-term fix was a Lithuania office for a follow-the-sun rotation (right time zones, two great engineers already there, a chance to be the region's top employer). They did well, then kept colliding with other teams who owned the systems causing the alerts. Acknowledging pages isn't a mission. Step through what changed.

The first mission must be critical enough that a senior executive sponsors it. Can't find one? Don't open the office. Test: everyone in the company can name something the office launched this year, and one thing they'd go to it for first.

5

Engagement and predictable growth

Name a Site Lead with explicit responsibilities (at Digg's SF office Larson was the only senior leader without that role, and often lacked standing to advocate). Start with at least ~12 people on one or two teams, not 50 scattered across many. Executives visit quarterly for the first year or two; people literally count visits. Then stay predictable for three-plus years: one hiring spurt year is allowed, after which ~50% year-over-year.

6

Integration: share the burden

Stripe's simultaneous European and Asia-Pacific expansion showed how hard time zones make integration. Physical challenges: no meeting time works for everyone; remote questions feel different; video gear fails. Cultural ones: no long relationships with founders, different language and context, and new offices mirroring how the culture has drifted. The playbook:

Friction is the pointIf opening an office causes no friction in existing offices, you've either mastered it or pushed the burden onto the new one. Check: segment culture survey results by location and look for gaps; ask established-office folks how they've adapted. Also invest in the local community, ideally through a Site Lead with local ties.
7

Appendix E: Magnitudes of exploration

Real engineering strategies are rarely public, so Larson shares a sanitized 2019 Stripe strategy: mostly standardize, explore only for order-of-magnitude improvements, and limit concurrent explorations. Standardizing creates leverage (improve tooling a bit and every engineer benefits) and investing in existing standards is the only investment with no added organizational risk. Exploration is how you escape a local maximum, and successful explorations compound. Each extreme fails: over-standardization leaves tooling stale (some Amazon employees describe this); constant exploration breeds parallel stacks whose creators tire of operating them and hand them off, or leave.

8

Does this exploration clear the bar?

Qi Jin's rule of thumb: explore only when a technology is roughly as strong on every dimension and at least 10x better on one. Quantifying that, and agreeing the baselines haven't regressed, is the hardest part; an exploration pulls the ideal and the flawed-but-real into the same measurement. Rate a proposal against the current standard.

9

Limit explorations in progress

Cap concurrent explorations (perhaps two or three for a 1,000-engineer org). Unlimited exploration piles up technical debt until you can't risk any more, and the real power lies in the nth-plus-one exploration.
