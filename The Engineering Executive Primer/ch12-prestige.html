Building Personal and Organizational Prestige

Prestige makes hiring easier, opens doors and buys patience. It can be built on purpose, as long as you know which game you are playing and why.

1

Brand versus prestige

Every month someone tells Larson they'd land better roles if their personal brand were better known; in boom times, companies believe a better Engineering brand would fix hiring. Having written a lot online and worked at companies that invested in Engineering brand, he's seen what it can and can't do.

A brand is a deliberately crafted, sustained narrative that's widely known: you have an opinion of Google Engineering without researching it. Prestige is an ambient, positive familiarity. Most successful executives Larson has worked with don't write online, tweet, speak at conferences or stream; most Engineering orgs aren't externally known. Social media's reality distortion field makes fame in a niche feel like credibility. But nearly all successful leaders and orgs are prestigious in some way, and prestige is a universal lubricant: it opens senior roles, attracts senior hires, and adds edges to your network.

2

Where prestige comes from

External communication is rarely the CTO's jobWhen an Engineering executive does lead external visibility, it's usually a founding CTO, like Honeycomb's Charity Majors. Hired-in CTOs rarely take that role, and fewer succeed at it. After Series A or B, companies bring in specialists: Marketing for sales leads, Developer Relations or Growth for sign-ups.

Manufacture prestige with infrequent, high-quality content

Engineers facing a new problem build a system: an Engineering blog with a publishing cadence, representation across sub-teams, incentives to contribute. Follow that plan and you technically have a brand, with more work and less effect. Prestige needs a few noteworthy things that are easy to find. Step through Larson's process.

The same approach works for a company's Engineering brand: a few excellent, timeless pieces beat a busy calendar.

5

Measuring prestige is a minefield

Every initiative wants a metric. Larson's advice: a small, timeboxed investment; track how often your content comes up in hiring conversations (as candidate or hiring manager); stop there. The obvious metrics push you the wrong way. Tap any you'd be tempted to track.
