Meetings for an Effective Engineering Organization

Meetings are the backup channel for everything your processes miss. Run a small, deliberate set (leadership weekly, spec and incident reviews, monthlies, an Engineering Q&A) and evolve it as you grow.

1

Meetings as the backup channel

Many engineers decide meetings are a waste of time, and plenty are. But that view is short-sighted. The best channel for most information isn't a big meeting: informal relationships carry the richest context, tickets carry project status, strategy belongs in a written document. Meetings are a remarkably effective backup for the gaps those channels leave. You can't talk to every person about everything, but each manager can talk to their team, backed by an org-wide forum. You can't involve everyone in every technical decision, but a tech spec review can vet it. If a meeting isn't helping, iterate or pause it; don't give up on meetings.

2

Six essential meetings

Larson's core set: three operational meetings (weekly leadership/team meetings, tech spec review, incident review), two monthly developmental meetings (Engineering managers, staff engineers), and a monthly Engineering Q&A. That's about four org meetings a week, a shape that suits organizations of roughly 20 to 200 engineers. Tap a meeting in the month.

The weekly leadership meeting

Your core hub, alongside the CEO's own staff meeting. It does three jobs: it's a working session to resolve things together (strategy disagreements, a contested career ladder); a place for directs to share context with each other, not just with you, since serendipity starts with mutual awareness; and the forge for a "first team" whose top priority is supporting each other (see Patrick Lencioni's The Five Dysfunctions of a Team). Larson includes his directs plus key partners from Recruiting, People and Finance. Build an agenda.

4

Tech spec review and incident review

Weekly reviews of new specs and incidents. Cancel on a quiet week; on a good week both move the company forward. They're also a barometer of your engineering culture: if you find them uncomfortable, boring or frustrating, fix them. Compare the two versions.

5

Monthlies for managers and staff engineers

Two development sessions: one for all Engineering managers, one for all Staff-plus engineers (usually without managers). The groups tend to be a similar size and equally important. A typical hour:

Topics might be a Lara Hogan piece on delegation or reading a P&L for managers, or a chapter of Tanya Reilly's The Staff Engineer's Path for staff engineers. Done well, people leave more capable and more confident the company is investing in them.

6

The monthly Engineering Q&A

Larson's favorite. An hour: introduce new hires, share a few messages, then questions until they run out (rarely early). Most engineers never work with you directly; they'll decide whether to trust you from how you answer the hardest questions here, and you'll hear concerns that never reach you otherwise. Nurture the few people who always ask; use a question-voting tool and leave pauses so others get a turn. Check the time-zone rule.

7

Other useful meetings

You own the forum even if you don't run itAn executive assistant or program manager can coordinate. But people take cues from you: if you say a meeting matters and don't show up, they won't believe you, and it's very hard for others to lead it. You're accountable for these meetings being a good use of the org's time. That can't be delegated.
8

Scaling the meeting set

A five-person team needs only the weekly team meeting. Scaling down is easy; scaling up gets messy. Operational meetings scale for the right participants (if spec review skews mobile, split off a mobile session). Developmental meetings scale for engagement (above about 10 people, many stop participating; above about 20 managers, push the session one layer down). Keep the Q&A org-wide. Slide the org size.
