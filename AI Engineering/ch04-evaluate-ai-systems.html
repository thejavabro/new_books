Evaluate AI Systems

A model is only good if it is good for your application. Turn the toolbox into practice: decide what to measure, pick models through public and private tests, weigh APIs against self-hosting, and build an evaluation pipeline you can trust.

Evaluation-driven development

Which is worse: an app never deployed, or one deployed with nobody knowing if it works? Many teams pick the second. Applications with clear, measurable outcomes (recommenders, fraud detection) get built for a reason. The book argues for evaluation-driven development: define how you'll evaluate an application before building it, the way test-driven development writes tests first. Criteria fall into four buckets. 2

Domain-specific capability

Can the model do your domain at all? A Latin-to-English app needs a model that understands Latin. Code is checked with functional correctness, and sometimes efficiency (BIRD-SQL compares query runtime with a reference) or readability (often judged by AI). Non-coding domains are usually tested with close-ended tasks, especially multiple choice: in April 2024, 75% of the tasks in Eleuther's lm-evaluation-harness were multiple choice, including MMLU.

Beat the random baseline

Multiple choice has limitsScores can swing with small changes, like adding a space or swapping option labels. And picking the right option is a different skill from generating a good answer, so close-ended scores may say little about summaries, essays or chat.
3

Generation capability: factual consistency and safety

Old NLG metrics such as fluency and coherence matter less now that models write fluently. The pressing issues are factual consistency and safety. Consistency can be checked locally, against given context (summaries, RAG answers), or globally, against open knowledge, where the hard part is deciding what the facts even are. Analyse when your model hallucinates: in one of the author's projects it was niche topics and questions about things that don't exist.

Textual entailment. Context: "Mary likes all fruits."

SAFE: search-augmented fact checking

SelfCheckGPT: do the samples agree?

If a model's own resamples contradict an answer, the answer is likely made up. Costly: many generations per check.

Specialised scorers also exist, such as a 184M-parameter DeBERTa model trained on 764,000 entailment pairs. TruthfulQA (817 questions people often answer wrongly from misconceptions) is a common benchmark.

4

Instruction-following capability

A model that understands sentiment but answers "HAPPY" when you asked for POSITIVE, NEGATIVE or NEUTRAL will break your parser. IFEval uses instructions that a program can verify: include these keywords, use N bullet points, answer in JSON, stay under N words. INFOBench takes a broader view (content, style, linguistics) and breaks each instruction into yes/no criteria, often checked by a judge model. Try being the model:

Instruction: Explain evaluation in under 40 words. Use exactly 3 bullet points starting with "* ". Include the word "rubric". Do not use the word "very". End with a line starting "P.S."
Build your own benchmarkPublic instruction benchmarks only hint at what matters to you. If you need YAML, a word limit or a persona, write test prompts with those exact instructions. Roleplaying is one of the most common real instructions (the eighth most common use case in a million LMSYS conversations): check that the model keeps the persona's style and knowledge, often with an AI judge.
5

Cost, latency and choosing on several axes

A great model that is too slow or expensive won't be used. Balancing quality, latency and cost is Pareto optimisation: set hard limits first, then pick the best trade-off among what's left. Latency depends on the model, the prompt and the sampling settings; output length matters, so you can ask for concise answers or set stop conditions. Usage-based APIs cost about the same per token at any scale; self-hosting gets cheaper per token as volume grows.

Fictional models; the method is the point.

Must-have or nice-to-have?Ask users if they want lower latency and they'll say yes. Find out whether latency actually changes behaviour before paying for it.
6

A model selection workflow

You don't need the best model, you need the best model for you, and you'll choose again for each adaptation technique. Separate hard attributes you can't change (license, training data, model size, privacy policy) from soft attributes you can improve (accuracy, toxicity). Latency is soft if you host the model and can optimise it, hard if it's someone else's API.

7

Model APIs or self-hosting?

"Open source" now covers several things: open weight models (download the weights, data not shared) and fully open models (weights and data). Licenses vary: can you use it commercially? Above a user threshold? Use its outputs to train other models? Then the big question: call a commercial API or host an open model yourself? Rate what matters most to you.

Navigating public benchmarks

There are thousands of benchmarks (BIG-bench alone had 214) and harnesses like lm-evaluation-harness and OpenAI evals to run them. Leaderboards pick a handful and aggregate. Watch two things. Correlation: if two benchmarks always agree, including both just double-counts a skill. Aggregation: Hugging Face averaged scores; HELM used mean win rate. Tap a cell.

Correlation between Hugging Face's six benchmarks (Jan 2024)

Data contaminationWhen a model trained on benchmark data, its score is inflated. It's often accidental: benchmarks get scraped with the web, or come from the same sources as training data. Detect it with n-gram overlap (a 13-token sequence from a test example also in training data marks it dirty) or with suspiciously low perplexity. GPT-3's team found 13 benchmarks with at least 40% of examples in the training data. Report scores on clean subsets too.
What public benchmarks are good forFiltering out bad models. They won't find the best model for your application, and they saturate fast: in June 2024 Hugging Face replaced its whole set with harder ones. Use them to shortlist, then run your own evaluation.
9

Design your evaluation pipeline

Step 1: evaluate every component. If a resume app extracts text from a PDF and then the current employer, a wrong answer could come from either step. Evaluate each step and the end result, per turn and per task (did the user get their problem solved, and in how many turns?).

Step 2: write an evaluation guideline, the most important step. Define what good means (a correct answer to "am I a fit for this job?" can still be unhelpful), choose a scoring system, write rubrics with examples, and tie metrics to business metrics.

Step 3: choose methods and data. Mix cheap and expensive methods (a small classifier on everything, an AI judge on a sample, humans on a few hundred a day). Use logprobs when you have them. Slice your data: an overall number can hide, or even reverse, what happens in subgroups.

Is your evaluation set big enough? Bootstrap it.

Evaluate the evaluation, then iterateDo better responses get better scores? Do better scores mean better business outcomes? Is it reliable run to run? Are two metrics so correlated one is redundant? What latency and cost does it add? As needs change, update criteria, and log every variable (data, rubric, prompt, judge settings) so results stay comparable.
