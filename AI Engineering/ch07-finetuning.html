Finetuning

Finetuning changes the model itself. It is for form more than facts, it is limited by memory more than anything else, and parameter-efficient methods like LoRA make it affordable.

1

Finetuning is transfer learning

Finetuning adapts a model by training it further, on all or part of its weights. It is a form of transfer learning: skills learned on a data-rich task (predicting text) carry over to data-poor ones (legal Q&A, SQL). That makes it sample efficient: a behaviour that would need millions of examples from scratch might need hundreds. It can improve domain skills, safety, instruction following and output format.

As an application developer you'll usually finetune a model that's already post-trained. The closer it already is to what you need, the less work. Feature-based transfer (using a model's embeddings as features for another model) is the other big family of transfer learning, common in computer vision.

2

When to finetune, and when not

Reasons to finetuneImprove quality on a specific task or format (a rare SQL dialect, strict JSON). Mitigate biases with curated data. Make a small model do what a big one does (often via data from the big one), which is cheaper and faster to serve. Grammarly's finetuned small models beat a much larger general model on its editing tasks.
Reasons not to (yet)It can hurt other tasks. It needs upfront data, ML know-how, serving and a maintenance plan, and newer base models may overtake your finetuned one. Many teams who insist on finetuning find their prompting was simply not systematic. Prompt caching also removed an old benefit (saving tokens spent on examples).

Once prompting is exhausted, look at why the model fails. Missing or outdated information calls for RAG; wrong behaviour (format, style, safety, irrelevance) calls for finetuning. Finetuning is for form, RAG is for facts.

Memory math: why finetuning is heavy

Training runs a forward pass (compute the output) and a backward pass (compute each trainable parameter's gradient and update it). Each trainable parameter therefore needs its value plus a gradient and optimizer state: none for plain SGD, one for momentum, two for Adam. Activations stored for the backward pass can exceed the weights themselves; gradient checkpointing recomputes them instead to save memory.

Inference ≈ N × bytes × 1.2 (20% for activations and KV cache). Training here = weights + gradients + optimizer states + activations taken as 20% of weights; real activation memory varies with sequence length and batch size.

4

Numerical representations

The bytes per value come from the number format. A float has a sign bit, range (exponent) bits and precision (mantissa) bits. FP32 is "single precision"; FP16 and BF16 both use 16 bits, but BF16 gives more bits to range, so it rarely overflows yet is less precise. TF32 is actually a 19-bit format. Load a model in the format it was meant for: loading BF16 weights as FP16 can break it.

5

Quantization and reduced precision

Fewer bits per value means less memory and often faster compute. Strictly, quantization means converting to integers, but the word covers any reduction in precision. Decide what to quantize (weights are the usual choice; activations are harder) and when: after training (post-training quantization, now standard for serving) or during training (quantization-aware training, or training directly in low precision, typically mixed precision).

How low can you go?Below 8 bits it gets tricky; 1 bit per value is a floor. Microsoft's BitNet b1.58 uses ternary weights (−1, 0, 1, about 1.58 bits) and roughly matched 16-bit Llama 2 at sizes up to 3.9B in its tests. The catch with any reduction: many tiny rounding changes can add up, and values can overflow.
6

Parameter-efficient finetuning and LoRA

Full finetuning updates every weight: a 7B model in 16 bits needs 14 GB for weights plus 42 GB for gradients and Adam states. Partial finetuning (only some layers) saves memory but needs many parameters to match full finetuning. PEFT gets close to full-finetuning quality with a tiny fraction of trainable parameters. Houlsby et al. (2019) inserted small adapter modules, matching full finetuning on GLUE within 0.4% with about 3% of the parameters, but adapters add inference latency. The two families today: adapter-based (LoRA and friends) and soft prompts (trainable token vectors added to the input). In the author's analysis of Hugging Face's PEFT repo issues, LoRA dominated.

Freeze W; train only A (n × r) and B (r × m). Afterwards, merge A·B into W (no extra latency) or keep it separate. A scaling factor α controls how much A·B contributes. Ranks of 4 to 64 are usually enough; bigger r often doesn't help.

Which matrices? (GPT-3 175B, 18M-parameter budget)

WikiSQL accuracy from the LoRA paper: spreading a small rank over all four attention matrices beat a big rank on one. Later practice adds the feedforward layers too.

Why does such a tiny update work?Studies suggest big pre-trained models have a low intrinsic dimension: the change needed for a new task lives in a small subspace, and pre-training itself lowers that dimension. That is also why low-rank pre-training hasn't scaled yet: full-rank pre-training seems to be what makes low-rank finetuning possible.

Serving many LoRA models

Merged: one full matrix per customer. Separate: one shared base plus a small A, B pair each, and switching customers means loading only the adapter. Adapters can be shared and reused (some even mixed from public adapter hubs).

QLoRALoRA's adapters are already tiny (37.7 MB for GPT-3 at rank 2 on two matrices vs 350 GB of weights), so the bigger win is shrinking the frozen base. QLoRA stores it in 4-bit NF4 and dequantizes to BF16 for computation, letting a 65B model finetune on one 48 GB GPU. Its Guanaco 65B (41 GB) scored 1022 Elo vs ChatGPT's 966 in a May 2023 GPT-4-judged comparison (GPT-4 itself: 1348). NF4 conversion slows training.

Model merging and multi-task finetuning

Model merging combines several models into one, often finetuned from the same base. Unlike an ensemble, which runs every model and combines the answers, a merged model runs once. Uses: better quality, multi-task models without training on everything at once (simultaneous finetuning needs lots of data; sequential finetuning tends to forget earlier tasks), fitting more skills on a device, and federated learning.

Finetuning tactics

Choosing a base model

Progression path: test your code on the cheapest model, test your data on a mid-sized one, then run the best model you can afford, and map the price/performance frontier. Distillation path: train the strongest affordable model on a small dataset, use it to generate more data, then train a cheaper model on that.

Method and framework

Start with LoRA; try full finetuning later. Full finetuning usually needs thousands of examples; with a few hundred, LoRA may do as well. Many adapters can share one base. Use a finetuning API for convenience, or frameworks (LLaMA-Factory, unsloth, PEFT, Axolotl, LitGPT) and distributed trainers (DeepSpeed and similar) for control.

Learning rate and the loss curve

illustrative

Batch size

Too small (fewer than about 8) makes training unstable; larger is faster but needs memory. Gradient accumulation lets small batches act like big ones.

Epochs

Millions of examples may need 1–2 epochs; thousands may need 4–10. Both losses falling: train longer. Validation loss rising: overfitting, stop.

Prompt loss weight

How much the prompt tokens count in the loss versus the response. Often 10% by default: learn mostly from responses.
