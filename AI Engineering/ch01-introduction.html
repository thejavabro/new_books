Introduction to Building AI Applications with Foundation Models

Scale turned language models into foundation models, and foundation models turned AI into a tool anyone can build with. This chapter explains how we got here, what people build, how to plan an AI product and how AI engineering differs from classic ML engineering.

1

Language models work in tokens

A language model stores statistics about language: how likely a piece of text is to come next, given what came before. Its unit is the token, which can be a character, a word or a piece of a word such as -ing. Tokens let a model split words into meaningful parts, keep the vocabulary smaller than a word list, and still handle made-up words. For GPT-4 an average token is roughly three quarters of a word, so 100 tokens is about 75 words.

A toy tokenizer

type anything

Illustrative only: real tokenizers (such as byte-pair encoding) learn their pieces from data. This one just peels off common suffixes and prefixes.

There are two families. A masked model fills a gap anywhere in the text using words on both sides (useful for classification and embeddings, like BERT). An autoregressive model predicts only the next token from what came before, which is why it can generate text one token at a time. In the rest of the book, "language model" means the autoregressive kind.

A completion machineGive the model a prompt and it continues it. Phrase a task as a completion ("Is this email spam? Answer:") and the same machine becomes a classifier, a translator or a coder. The continuation is a probability-based prediction, not a guaranteed answer.
2

Self-supervision is why they could grow so big

Most ML models need supervision: people label examples (this photo is a cat) and the model learns from the labels. Labels are slow and expensive, especially when they need experts. Language models dodge the problem: any text already contains its own labels, because each next token is the answer for the text before it. That is self-supervision, and since text is everywhere, it let models scale to huge datasets.

The book's example: at 5 cents a label, a million images costs $50,000, before quality checks. Expert labels cost far more.

Self-supervision is not unsupervised learning: there are labels, they are just taken from the data itself. A model's size is counted in parameters; GPT-1 (2018) had 117 million and was called large. Bigger models need more data to fill their capacity, which only self-supervision could supply.

3

From large language models to foundation models

Text is only one way we take in the world. Models that also take images, audio or video are multimodal, and a generative one is a large multimodal model. The book uses foundation model for both LLMs and LMMs: the word signals that they are the base on which many applications are built, and that they are general purpose rather than built for one task. One model can translate, summarise, classify, write code and describe images.

4

Why AI engineering took off

AI engineering is building applications on top of foundation models. Classic ML engineering develops models; AI engineering mostly uses models someone else trained. Three things came together:

Factor 1

General-purpose capability

Models can do more tasks, and do old ones better, so many more applications become possible. Anything that involves communication can be partly automated.

Factor 2

Investment

After ChatGPT, money poured in from investors and enterprises, while the cost of using a given level of capability kept falling.

Factor 3

Low barrier to entry

Model as a service means one API call gets you a capable model; AI can even write the code. You no longer need to train anything to start.

Why "AI engineering" and not "ML engineering" or an "-Ops" term? The work differs from classic ML, and it is more about tweaking (engineering) models than operating them. In a small survey the author ran, most practitioners preferred the name.

What people build

Categorising uses is hard because one application can sit in several buckets. The book groups what it found across hundreds of open source projects and many company interviews into eight categories. Enterprises are quicker to deploy internal-facing applications (knowledge management, internal tools) than customer-facing ones, because the risks are lower.

6

Should you build it, and what role does AI play?

If you are building for a living, step back first. Why build it? The book ranks motives by risk: if you don't, competitors with AI could make you obsolete; if you don't, you'll miss chances to boost profit and productivity; or you're unsure yet but don't want to be left behind. Then ask whether you have to build it yourself. Next, pin down the role AI plays. Flip the switches to see what each choice demands.

Decide the role of humans too: AI might draft replies a human agent edits, answer simple requests and hand off harder ones, or answer everything. Involving people in AI decisions is human-in-the-loop. Microsoft's Crawl–Walk–Run framework raises automation step by step:

7

Set expectations, plan for the last mile

Decide how success is measured before building: business metrics (what share of support messages should a chatbot handle?), a usefulness threshold (how good it must be to ship), plus quality, latency (time to first token, time per output token), cost and fairness metrics. Then plan milestones from where an off-the-shelf model already gets you.

Quick demos are misleading

Illustrative curve. The book cites UltraChat's authors ("0 to 60 is easy, 60 to 100 is exceedingly hard") and a team that reached about 80% of the experience it wanted in a month, then needed four more months to pass 95%.

Maintenance never stopsModels get cheaper, faster and longer-context, which is good but means constant cost-benefit checks. Swapping APIs is easier as providers converge, yet each model has quirks that force prompt changes. Rules on data, compute exports and intellectual property can shift under you, sometimes fatally.
8

Three layers of the AI stack

Every AI application stack has three layers. Teams usually start at the top and move down only when they need to. The 2023 explosion of tooling happened mostly in the top layer, while the bottom layer stayed comparatively stable: many principles of building ML systems still apply.

9

How AI engineering differs from ML engineering

Three big shifts: you use a model someone else trained, so the work is adapting and evaluating rather than modelling; models are bigger, so compute, latency and inference optimisation matter more; and outputs are open-ended, which makes evaluation far harder. Adaptation comes in two flavours: prompt-based techniques that change the input only, and finetuning that changes the weights.

ResponsibilityTraditional MLFoundation models

Because a working model exists on day one, you can build a product first, invest in data once it shows promise, and train or finetune last. This rewards fast iteration and brings AI engineering close to full-stack development; many AI engineers now come from web backgrounds.

Words that get mixed upPre-training trains from random weights. Finetuning continues training an already-trained model. Post-training is finetuning done by the model maker after pre-training. Prompt engineering is not training: no weights change.
