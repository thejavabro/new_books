Understanding Foundation Models

You can use a model without building one, but the choices its makers took (data, architecture, size, alignment) and the way it picks each token explain most of its strengths and its odd behaviour.

1

A model is only as good as its data

If a language is missing from the training data, the model can't use it. Teams can't afford to collect data for everything they want, so they "use what they have": mostly web crawls such as Common Crawl, which is full of clickbait, misinformation and toxicity, and is heavily English. Teams filter it with heuristics, but the mix still shapes what the model is good at.

Share of speakers vs share of Common Crawl

ratio = world share ÷ crawl share

Data from the book (Lai et al., 2023). A ratio above 1 means the language is under-represented. English, with 18% of speakers and 46% of the crawl, sits at 0.4. Unsurprisingly, models such as GPT-4 score best in English on benchmarks like MMLU and do much worse in under-represented languages.

Tokenizers trained mostly on English need many more tokens for some languages. For Burmese the book finds roughly ten times as many tokens, so ten times the time and cost for the same content.

Domain-specific models

General models do well on everyday domains but struggle where the data was never public: drug discovery, cancer screening, internal company knowledge. Specialised datasets give models like AlphaFold (protein structures), NVIDIA's BioNeMo and Google's Med-PaLM 2. Training on everything isn't the answer either: more data costs more and a smaller, cleaner dataset can beat a big noisy one.

2

The transformer and its attention mechanism

Before transformers, seq2seq models used RNNs: an encoder read the input token by token into one final hidden state, and a decoder generated from that summary alone. Two problems: answering from a summary is like answering questions about a book from its blurb, and processing is sequential, so it is slow. Attention fixes both: at each step the model can look directly at any earlier token and weigh how relevant it is, and input tokens can be processed in parallel.

Pick the token being generated

illustrative weights

Each token gets three vectors made by multiplying its embedding with learned matrices: a query (what am I looking for?), a key (what do I contain?) and a value (what do I pass on?). The dot product of the current query with each key, scaled and passed through softmax, gives the weights used to mix the values. Several heads do this in parallel so the model can attend to different things at once.

score_i  = (q · k_i) / sqrt(d)
weight_i = softmax(score)_i
output   = Σ weight_i · v_i
Two phases of inferencePrefill: process all input tokens in parallel and store their keys and values.
Decode: generate one token at a time, each attending to everything before it.
Longer contexts mean more keys and values to compute and store, which is one reason long context is expensive (chapter 9).

Where the parameters live

A transformer is an embedding module, a stack of blocks (each an attention module with four weight matrices plus an MLP module) and an output layer. Rough count: blocks × (4d² attention + 3·d·ff MLP) + 2 × vocab × d. It ignores small terms and tricks like grouped-query attention, but lands near 6.7B and 13B for the presets.

Alternatives exist. RWKV is a parallelisable RNN; state space models (S4, H3, Mamba) handle long sequences efficiently; Jamba mixes transformer and Mamba layers. The transformer still dominates.

3

Model size, data and compute

Three numbers signal scale: parameters (learning capacity), training tokens (how much it learned from) and FLOPs (training cost). Parameter counts can mislead. A sparse mixture-of-experts model like Mixtral 8x7B has 46.7B parameters (experts share some), but only two experts run per token, so it costs about as much as a 12.9B model to run. And a big model trained on too little data can lose to a smaller one.

Uses training FLOPs ≈ 6 × parameters × tokens and the H100's quoted 60 teraFLOP/s from the book's worked example (GPT-3-175B at 3.14×10²³ FLOPs would take about 236 days on 256 H100s at full use). Double the model size and you should double the tokens.

Production isn't only quality

Chinchilla optimises quality for a training budget. Real products also care about inference cost, so teams often train smaller models on far more tokens than "optimal" (Llama 3 used 15 trillion).

Diminishing returns

The cost of reaching a given quality keeps falling, but each improvement costs more. Dropping cross-entropy from about 3.4 to 2.8 nats needs ten times the data. Small metric gains can still mean big differences for users.

Bottlenecks ahead

Human text on the internet may run out within years; more of it is AI-generated, raising fears of model collapse; proprietary data gets locked down. Electricity is the other limit: data centres already use a noticeable share of global power.

Hyperparameters at scaleA parameter is learned; a hyperparameter (layers, learning rate, batch size) is chosen. Big models can be trained only once, so teams study how hyperparameters behave on small models and extrapolate (scaling extrapolation). Emergent abilities, which appear only at scale, make this harder.
4

Post-training: from completion machine to assistant

Pre-training on raw web text gives a model that completes text, with all the web's manners. Post-training fixes two problems: it should converse, not just complete, and it should give answers people prefer. It uses a tiny share of compute (about 2% for InstructGPT) but changes behaviour a lot.

Training a reward model from comparisons

Scoring a response from 1 to 10 is hard and inconsistent. Picking the better of two is easier. The reward model learns to give the winner a higher score: its loss shrinks as the gap between winner and loser scores grows.

What the data costs

  • Demonstrations (prompt, ideal response) need skilled labelers. About 13,000 pairs for InstructGPT; at around $10 a pair that is $130,000 before design and quality checks.
  • Comparisons are cheaper: Llama 2's team reported about $3.50 each.
  • Alternatives: volunteers (LAION's OpenAssistant), heuristics on web conversations, and increasingly AI-generated data (chapter 8).
  • DPO skips the separate reward model and RL loop and is gaining ground; some companies use only a reward model to pick the best of several outputs.
5

Sampling: how the next token is chosen

For each step the model outputs a logit per vocabulary token. Softmax turns logits into probabilities. Always taking the top token (greedy) is dull and repetitive; sampling from the distribution is more varied. Three knobs shape the choice. Try them.

Prompt: "My favourite colour is …"

Temperature

Logits are divided by T before softmax. Below 1 sharpens the distribution, above 1 flattens it. With logits [1, 2], B gets 73% at T = 1 and 88% at T = 0.5. "T = 0" in practice means just take the top token.

Top-k

Keep only the k largest logits, softmax over those. Saves computation and cuts off the long tail of unlikely tokens.

Top-p (nucleus)

Keep the smallest set of tokens whose probabilities add up to p. The set grows when the model is unsure and shrinks when it is confident. Min-p is a cousin: drop tokens below a minimum probability.

Logprobs: a debugging windowMany APIs return log probabilities. They show how confident the model was, help classification and evaluation, and reveal when a correct label sits just below a wrong one. Stopping conditions (max tokens, stop tokens) control length, but stopping too early can break a JSON object.
6

Test time compute: generate more, pick the best

Instead of one response, generate several and choose: show users options, pick the one with the highest average logprob, score them with a reward model or verifier, apply a heuristic (shortest, valid SQL), or take the majority answer. More diverse samples help. It costs more: two outputs cost roughly twice as much. Verifiers gave OpenAI a large boost on math problems, but returns stopped at around 400 samples in their experiment.

Assumes independent samples and a perfect picker. Real pickers are imperfect, which is why gains level off.

7

Getting structured outputs

Many tasks need a fixed format: semantic parsing (text to SQL or regex), classification into valid labels, or JSON that downstream code will read. Approaches, from lightest to heaviest: prompting (ask, maybe have a second model validate), post-processing (fix common mistakes like a missing bracket), test time compute (retry until valid), constrained sampling (only allow tokens that keep the output valid) and finetuning on examples in the format, possibly with a classifier head that guarantees a valid class.

Constrained sampling, step by step

Constrained sampling needs a grammar for each format and can slow generation; some argue the effort is better spent making models follow instructions. LinkedIn chose YAML over JSON for its outputs because it needed fewer tokens.

8

The probabilistic nature of AI

Sampling makes models probabilistic. That is great for creativity and a headache for reliability. Two symptoms: inconsistency (same or nearly the same input, very different outputs) and hallucination (confident output not grounded in fact).

Grade the same essay five times

Fixing temperature, top-p and the random seed, or caching answers, makes repeated runs consistent, though different hardware can still change results. Slightly different inputs giving different answers is harder; careful prompts and memory help.

Hypothesis 1: self-delusion (snowballing)

Hypothesis 2: knowledge mismatchDuring SFT, labelers write answers using knowledge the model may not have. The model learns to produce answers that sound like they rest on knowledge it lacks, which teaches it to make things up. Proposed fixes: verification, and teaching models to say "I don't know". Interestingly, InstructGPT hallucinated more after RLHF than after SFT alone.
What you can doPrompts such as "answer as truthfully as possible, and say you don't know if unsure" help a little, as do shorter answers. Detection and measurement are covered in chapter 4; grounding with retrieval in chapter 6.
