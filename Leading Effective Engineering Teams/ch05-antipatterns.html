An antipattern field guide

An antipattern is a common response to a recurring problem that looks like a fix but isn't. The book sorts effectiveness antipatterns by where they start, because that decides who can fix them. Individual ones are fixed by the person, with help. Practice ones need the team to change course, led by its leader. Structural ones need the team's shape changed. Leadership ones usually need someone more senior to step in. Pick any antipattern to open its card.

        2

        Too deep, too wide, or T-shaped

        The specialist owns one module so completely that nobody else dares touch it, and the team dreads their vacation. The generalist helps everywhere but masters nothing, and hands hard tasks to specialists. The target is a T-shaped skill set: depth in one area, breadth to collaborate across the rest. The book's rule of thumb for experts: each should have backups who know 60 to 80% of what they know. Have the specialist hand changes to others and review instead.

        3

        The hoarder and the giant pull request

        The hoarder works quietly all sprint, then submits one enormous PR. Maybe they fear judgment, maybe they're used to working alone. Either way it signals low trust in both directions, starves the feedback loop and buries reviewers. Slide from one giant PR to many small ones.

        One hoarder's turnaroundThe author coached a talented engineer whose solo Chromium module was hard for anyone to integrate. "Code is read more often than it is written" started the change. Then: explain things simply, mentor a junior who hit a bug he'd already solved, and own a failed risky bet without defensiveness. He became a mentor and leader.
        4

        The relentless guide and the trivial tweaker

        Two quieter individual traps. The relentless guide helps before being asked, turns reviews into rewrites, and spoon-feeds answers, so others stop learning and the guide burns out (teams can even split into followers and skeptics). The trivial tweaker keeps refactoring stable code for no user benefit, eating review time and risking regressions. Both get fixed by redirecting energy, not by scolding.

        For the guideAsk people to try first; pair across experience levels; hold knowledge-sharing sessions; protect the guide's own time; give them something unfamiliar to struggle with.
        For the tweakerGive them something to build from scratch; ask "what changes for users?" before refactoring; agree criteria for code changes; celebrate substantial work.
        5

        Last-minute heroics

        Bugs pile up until the week before release, then everyone rallies and saves the day. It's celebrated, and then it happens again. Repeated heroics signal a process problem: little time for feedback, hidden debt from quick fixes, lower quality, and a team that relies on heroes. Compare where the fixing happens.

        6

        Pull request irregularities

        Four ways the review process goes wrong: rubber-stamping (approval without reading), self-merging, long-running back-and-forth PRs, and a flood of last-minute PRs before a deadline. Fixes: real reviews, approval by someone else, expectations for review speed, and checkpoints such as stand-ups. Diagnose this queue.

Long-tenured engineers settle at about four comments per hundred lines. Breakdowns mostly come from misunderstandings, such as why a change was needed.

        7

        Protracted refactoring and retrospective negligence

        A refactor starts with good intentions and becomes a project of its own: scope creeps, engineers get pulled in, releases slip. Find the cause (perfectionism needs a defined scope; missing domain knowledge needs the right person), then timebox it and review to closure. Retrospectives decay too: skipped, shortened, unstructured, dominated by a few voices, no follow-up, or stuck on symptoms. Use a structure like Start / Stop / Continue and dig with five whys. Step through one.

        StopMerging untested hotfixes on release day

        8

        Isolated clusters and knowledge bottlenecks

        Isolated clusters form when subgroups only review and help each other: comfortable, but friends rubber-stamp each other and knowledge fragments. Toggle reviewer rotation.

        Knowledge bottlenecks concentrate critical knowledge in a few people, often the specialist and the guide. The bus factor is how many people you could lose before the project stalls; you want it high. Click cells to mark who can work on each module and watch the bus factor change. In the author's Chrome team, documentation, shared ownership, tech talks, rotation and pairing new engineers with veterans scaled the team's capability without new headcount.

        9

        Leadership: micromanagement and the glass barrier

        Micromanagement wears three costumes: the perfectionist who keeps adding wish-list items, the prescriber who dictates how instead of what, and the gatekeeper who withholds information "to protect the team". The costs: less innovation, lower morale, slower progress, stunted growth and uninformed decisions. The alternative is autonomy plus a glass barrier: shield the team from noise but let them see what matters. Move the dial.

        10

        Scope mismanagement and planning overkill

        In one published case, a document imaging system planned for 18 to 24 months took four years, partly because around 50 users fed requirements to the vendor and nobody managed scope. Simulate a quarter of change requests with and without a change evaluation process (baseline comparison, prioritization, scope-freeze periods, escalation when needed).

        Planning overkill is the opposite failure: weeks spent designing a hyper-configurable discount engine when the stakeholder wanted a standard discount. Overanalysis, endless design iterations, documentation for all of it, and late, rigid delivery. Prefer a realistic scope, iterative refinement and progressive elaboration. As Kent Beck put it, an iteration's worth of data beats months of speculation.

        11

        Skeptical, passive and unappreciative leaders

        A skeptical leader turns every hiccup into a minefield and every blog post into a design review. "Trust, but verify" is healthy; suspicion is not. Pick the better way to raise a concern.

        Passive (absentee) leadershipComplacent, timid, indecisive: says "great job" when it isn't, won't try the automation the team proposes. Research calls absentee leaders the most common kind of incompetent leader, and the hardest to spot. Senior leaders fix it with clear expectations, open channels, information, an innovation culture and accountability.
        UnderappreciationGood habits go unnoticed: a precise commit, focused work, a self-started cleanup. People feel invisible and stop going the extra mile. Recognize regularly, promptly and publicly, even small things.
