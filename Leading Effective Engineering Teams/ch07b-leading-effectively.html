Becoming an Effective Leader: Leading Effectively

The second half of chapter 7: choosing a leadership style for the situation, strategizing, communicating relentlessly, and the attitudes (trust, conviction, data, adaptability) that let a team become effectively efficient.

1

Three styles, mixed by situation

Transformational leaders set an ambitious vision, challenge the status quo and push people past self-imposed limits. Democratic (participative) leaders involve the team in decisions, share information and get buy-in. Servant leaders put people's needs and growth first. The author's own style is closest to servant leadership: empathy, humility, stewardship and commitment to development, like a lead who helps the team break the backlog into tasks, estimate, and clear roadblocks during sprint planning. Most leaders mix styles by situation. Pick a moment.

2

A new manager who tried to do it all

On a Chrome performance team, a brilliant engineer moved into management and kept coding, reviewing, planning and managing, until an intense sprint showed it couldn't last. Five changes fixed it. Turn them on.

3

Startup or enterprise: the environment shapes the style

Small, flexible projects suit a democratic approach and make good learning grounds. Complex, urgent ones may need a more decisive leader. Two composite stories from the book.

4

Strategize: visualize, map, think, prioritize

Without strategy, leaders become reactive. Visualize the future through environmental scanning, scenario planning, risk assessment and diverse perspectives. Rate some risks for a team adopting generative AI for developer productivity, a focus area the book uses as an example.

Turn the vision into a roadmap of initiatives and SMART milestones: keep it simple and visual, build in flexibility, update it, and build it with stakeholders rather than handing it down. Protect time for immersive strategic thinking: retreats, notifications off, quiet spaces. Then prioritize ruthlessly: three to five strategic bets, and the courage to say no to the rest. You have 10 units of team capacity. Choose your bets.

5

Play the part: communicate, structure, make it safe

Leadership isn't something you do 75% of the time. Start with relentless communication. Tick what you covered with your team this month.

Structure for innovation: flatten needless hierarchy; let people with domain expertise vet ideas (one large company's principle that expertise carries decision rights); favour speed and minimal viable process; protect innovation time and ideation sessions. And keep it psychologically safe. Step through how one lead handled a quiet engineer raising tech debt in a retro.

6

Spot potential, give feedback that helps

Talent development is personal: mentorship, sponsorship, coaching, custom training and stretch work, and celebrating small gains. Feedback runs through one-on-ones, peer reviews and retros. Good feedback is specific and actionable, balanced, tailored and followed up. Which would you send?

Keep the technical edgeRegular time for coding or new tech, conferences and courses, hands-on workshops, following industry trends.
Grow as a leaderLeadership programs, a mentor of your own, reflection and feedback, strategic thinking, communication, reading on leadership.
7

Master the attitude: trust with guardrails

Engineers don't sabotage their own work; they want to do it well, even if their route is sometimes wrong. So grant autonomy (feature ownership, flexible schedules, decision rights in their domain) inside guardrails. Choose guardrails and an autonomy level.

Model the behaviors you want (a growth mindset, inclusiveness, integrity, punctuality) consistently. And decide with conviction: know when to stop adding features and ship, and when to change the plan and face the resistance.

8

Data-driven and adaptive leadership

Data guards against opinion, bias and groupthink: velocity, cycle time, defect density, code coverage, customer satisfaction, shared on dashboards and in retros. In one composite story, a director of a smart-city IoT team saw velocity drop 20% in a quarter because of technical debt and used the data to win a dedicated debt sprint. Try it.

Change is constant, so the book closes with the 4 A's of adaptive leadership. A manager sees that a product his team already supports could serve a bank client's global risk-management need. Step through.

Effectiveness becomes efficiencyAs a well-led team matures, efficiency follows: people get more skilled, processes get streamlined (without over-optimizing), and strategy puts resources where they count. Keep room for creativity and adaptation. The book's last word: effectiveness is a mindset you expand by embodying it in strategy, action and attitude.
