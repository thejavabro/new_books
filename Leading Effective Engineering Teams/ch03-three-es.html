The 3 E's Model of Effective Engineering

What works for five engineers breaks at fifty. The 3 E's model says how a leader's job shifts as the team grows: first enable, then empower, then expand.

1

Enable, empower, expand

Teams grow and change, so the definition of effective has to survive growth. The author's model has three stages, in order. Enable: define what effectiveness means for your domain and share it. Empower: remove blockers so people can act on it. Expand: make teams self-sufficient so the leader can repeat the pattern at a larger scale. Tap a step.

2

Enable: define effectiveness for your context

No blanket definition works. Team goals should serve organizational goals (with OKRs, every team OKR should link to at least one company OKR). The steps: identify goals, pick relevant metrics, set targets, then define effectiveness as hitting the right outcomes. Involve stakeholders, back it with data, keep it simple, and review it. Even one company can have clashing definitions: a large newspaper's ad team wanted more third-party ads while engineering wanted a fast site. Pick an organization type.

3

Enable: initialize, and three enablers

Once defined, effectiveness has to reach everyone: communicate it clearly, train on it, measure progress, give timely feedback, and recognize people who meet it. The point for engineers is value to customers, not the newest technology. A 2020 Gartner survey of software teams adds three enablers, with these reported gains in effectiveness.

Teams that help write their own standards (UX, architecture, database, integration) and evolve them; versatile teams rather than pure specialists; leaders who clear obstacles and handle coordination with project managers and governance partners.

4

Empower: feed opportunities, starve problems

The author's core principle for empowerment. Feed opportunities: match people to work that fits their strengths, train them, form cross-functional pairs. Starve problems: audit processes for bottlenecks, settle conflicts fast, make sure staffing and tools are adequate. Continuous delivery, Kanban WIP limits and delegation do both. Sort each move.

The slow-page exampleA team fixes a slow page by indexing tables and reworking one query. Problem solved. Feeding the opportunity means going further: apply the same optimization to other queries on other pages before users hit them.
5

Empower: start with yourself

Effectiveness is not longer hours. When the author's team grew, his inbox filled with decisions waiting on him: he had become the bottleneck. He delegated routine decisions to leads (using RACI matrices), built trust and transparency, and dropped command-and-control for agile habits. His job shifted from firefighting to strategy. Peter Drucker's The Effective Executive gives the habits; the first is know where your time goes. Set up a typical week.

6

Empower: team effectiveness models

Models give you a vocabulary for debugging a team. Lencioni's five dysfunctions form a pyramid where each failure causes the one above. A familiar software example: developers and testers treating bugs as a contest, producing trivial bug reports and shallow fixes. Tap a layer to see cause and cure.

Tuckman's model describes the stages a team goes through. It isn't strictly linear: adding people can knock a performing team back into storming. Advance the team, then try adding a new hire.

Neither model is a quick fix. Pick one and apply it consistently.

7

Empower: multiply effectiveness

A data team lead at a fast-growing startup kept pace by changing tools (a data build tool to remove repetitive work, a more scalable warehouse), investing in documentation, shielding his team from meetings, and finding a director-level sponsor. Liz Wiseman's Multipliers names the leader behaviour behind that: some leaders amplify their people, others unknowingly shrink them.

8

Empower: find high-leverage activities

Andy Grove's High Output Management defines leverage as output per unit of activity. Personal leverage comes from your own tools and skills; organizational leverage comes from structures that make a whole team better, and it's the one people overlook. When an accessibility effort found screen readers behaving differently on desktop and mobile, the fix wasn't one bug: it was a testing framework and library that made every later release faster and cleaner.

9

Expand: when your domain grows

Expanding means growing leaders so every team can run without you. A respected manager whose scope grew kept reviewing code and attending every planning meeting, and became a bottleneck. Her fix: trust and delegate, streamline updates, set boundaries, mentor a second line of leaders, and reflect regularly. Drag your scope and watch the job change.

10

Always be deciding, leaving, scaling

Borrowed from Software Engineering at Google: three habits for staying effective as you lead more. Deciding: find the blinders, name the trade-offs, decide and iterate (avoid analysis paralysis). Here is a build-or-buy decision for a CRM system.

Leaving means the team no longer needs you for every decision. Divide the problem space, delegate pieces to future leaders, then adjust from a distance. Test this team: take people away and see whether work can still flow. (The book frames this with the "hit by a bus" metaphor; the aim is that losing any one person, including you, doesn't stop the team.)

Scaling protects your fixed pool of time, attention and energy. Be proactive (a few hours a day on important but not urgent work), manage your energy, and accept you won't finish everything: of a to-do list, roughly the bottom 20% can go, the middle 60% is mixed, and the top 20% is what only you can do. Triage this list.

Seen at scaleA large online retailer pushes "high-velocity" decisions with disagree-and-commit (always deciding), keeps teams small enough to feed with two pizzas so people can move between them (always leaving), and is famous for relentless scale and automation (always scaling).
