Effective Management: Google's Research

Google tried to prove managers don't matter and found the opposite. Two research projects turned into checklists: what good managers do, and what makes teams work.

1

Google set out to prove managers don't matter

Some strong engineering teams have always wondered whether they need managers at all. Early Google even tried a flat structure with no middle management. As it grew, that stopped working. Tap through the timeline.

2

Project Oxygen: ten behaviors of great managers

The People Analytics team surveyed employees about their managers, ran double-anonymized interviews with managers ("How often do you discuss careers with your reports?"), and compared the best and worst managers across engineering, business and administrative functions. Eight behaviors emerged; later work made it ten. The book's advice is to use them as a checklist. Rate yourself honestly (1 = rarely, 7 = consistently) and see where to focus.

3

Coach, empower, include

The first three behaviors are about people. Good coaches give timely, specific feedback, ask good questions, tailor to the person and genuinely care. Empowering means stretch assignments, light-touch check-ins (no daily updates on a five-day task, but "tell me right away if you're stuck") and cheering the team on to outsiders. Inclusion means welcoming newcomers with a buddy, building rapport, modelling civility and supporting people in bad times as well as good.

One Simple ThingA well-being practice: each person names one easy, non-work goal, such as working out three lunchtimes a week or a fully disconnected week of vacation this quarter, and the manager helps protect it.
4

Communicate: a one-on-one that covers the bases

Good communicators encourage open discussion, respond quickly, share context from leadership, stay honest and calm, and listen without judgment. The one-on-one is where this happens person to person. Build an agenda from the pieces below and see what it covers. A good meeting touches every column, not just status.

5

Support careers with GROW

Career support means clear expectations, fair evaluations, explaining how pay links to performance, and pointing out paths other than promotion: lateral moves, special projects, cross-functional work. Google structures career conversations with the GROW model. Step through an example conversation (an illustrative one).

6

Vision: from values down to goals

Great managers explain the why, align the team's vision with the company's, involve the team in shaping it, and connect strategy to everyday work. Google suggests a cascade from deeply held values to this quarter's goals. Tap each level for an example from an imaginary developer-tools team.

7

Aristotle for managers: psychological safety

Amy Edmondson defines it as a shared belief that the team is safe for interpersonal risk. It is not the same as cohesion: a cosy team can be less willing to disagree. Research also suggests silence is more often situational (the environment) than personality. And safety doesn't mean lowering the bar: both safety and standards must be high. Move the sliders. (The zone names come from Edmondson's own two-by-two; the book makes the same point in words.)

One habit carries a lot of weight: replace blame with curiosity. A deploy just broke checkout for an hour. Which opening line would you use?

Other moves: treat conflict as a collaborator, speak human to human, anticipate reactions, ask for feedback on your own delivery, and measure safety with anonymous surveys.

8

Dependability, structure and clarity

Dependability shows in small things: one person always late to the meeting teaches everyone lateness is fine. Dependable people have genuine intentions, accountability, sound thinking and consistent contribution; managers foster it by modelling it, defining roles and keeping communication open. Structure and clarity come from OKRs (each team OKR tied to at least one company OKR, reviewed a few times a quarter) and a RACI matrix. Click cells to cycle R, A, C, I. Each deliverable needs exactly one A and at least one R.

R Responsible: does the workA Accountable: owns itC Consulted: reviews, advisesI Informed: kept in the loop
9

Meaning, impact, and measuring the five

Meaning is personal: craft, financial security, family, changing society. Managers help people find their own story through recognition and support. Impact comes from connecting each task to company objectives, co-creating a team vision, sharing customer stories, and linking performance to outcomes. Be ready to answer, for anyone on the team: "why my role?" Google's gTeams exercise turns the five dynamics into a 10-minute survey plus a team conversation. Try a mini version.

Teams that adopted a simple norm, such as opening meetings with casual chat about weekend plans, improved 6% on psychological safety and 10% on structure and clarity.
