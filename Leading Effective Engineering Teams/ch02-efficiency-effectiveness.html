Efficiency Versus Effectiveness Versus Productivity

Three words that get used interchangeably mean different things. A team can ship fast, ship clean and still miss the point. The aim is to do the right things right.

1

Doing things right, doing the right thing, output over input

Efficiency is doing things right: minimal waste, maximum output for the resources. Effectiveness is doing the right thing: the result serves users and the business. Productivity is output divided by input, a subset of efficiency and the rawest of the three. A team can ship clean code on time (efficient), ship lots of it (productive) and still build something nobody needed (not effective). What pushes the three up or down

The same forces from chapter 1 show up here. Each one can help one measure and hurt another, which is why you have to balance them rather than maximize any one.

5

Output is proof of work; outcome is proof of value

An output shows that the team did something: a release, a refactor, a document. An outcome shows that it changed something for the better. Outputs are quantitative and easy to report: throughput, velocity, quality, capacity, code health. Outcomes are partly subjective and harder: business value, investment, user adoption. Outcomes pick the outputs worth trackingA large healthcare software group had reasonable sprint velocity yet kept missing completion dates, with stories stuck "in progress". It started from desired outcomes and switched to flow metrics: work in progress, cycle time and throughput. Cycle time fell by 42%.
6

Watermelon metrics: green outside, red inside

Measuring only outputs is tempting because tools like GitHub or Jira count them for you. It goes wrong in four ways: contributions are hard to attribute, teams overproduce low-value work (feature bloat), deadlines get set without the team and rush quality (a famous government health site launch is the cautionary tale), and people burn out. The result is the watermelon effect. Tap each green tile to cut it open.

7

Do the right things right

The goal is to be effectively efficient. Individual contributors can drift into pure efficiency, polishing their own speed or writing terse code that hurts readability. Others overindex on technical brilliance: one star engineer built an advanced feature the team had said wasn't in the year's plan. Pairing her with a product manager and having her present to non-technical stakeholders fixed it. Tap a quadrant.

The 20-minute rule

A senior engineering manager's habit for when you're stuck: spend the first 20 minutes researching on your own, then ask the team chat or a person, and share what you already found.

8

When effectiveness and efficiency pull apart

Sometimes the more effective choice costs efficiency: a familiar stack versus a promising new one, comprehensive testing versus a faster partial pass. Four factors tip the balance. Set the situation and see which way it leans.

A well-known early-stage rental startup, in debt and with a handful of bookings a day, built only what hosts and guests valued most (profiles with photos, listings, booking) and parked tempting extras such as advanced filters and personalized recommendations. It launched fast, learned from early users, and grew feature by feature.

9

Redefine productivity for knowledge work

Traditional productivity measures were built for repetitive work like data entry. Software work is problem solving, and much of what makes engineers productive (meetings, learning, discussion) doesn't show up in throughput. GitHub, wanting a fuller picture, asked questions like the ones below. Then use goal frameworks to choose your own metrics.

Goal, question, metric

SMARTFor individuals: specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, time-bound.
GQMFor teams: goals, the questions that reveal progress, and the metrics that answer them.
OKRsObjectives with measurable, time-bound key results, aligned up to company strategy. Google uses them for ambitious goals.
Why not just "do your best"?Goal-setting research finds that specific, hard goals beat vague encouragement. "Do your best" has no external reference point, so people interpret it however they like and performance varies widely.
