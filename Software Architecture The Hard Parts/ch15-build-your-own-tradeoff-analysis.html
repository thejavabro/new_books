Three steps, repeated until it makes sense

The book's solutions for Sysops Squad are examples, not templates. What carries over is the method. In outline:

Generic answers rarely survive contact with a real system, so you build your own analysis, iterate on it, and expect your first draft to be wrong. The authors arrived at their three forces (communication, consistency, coordination) the same way: by sketching hundreds of examples until a pattern held.

2

Build a static coupling picture

No tool can draw your architecture's coupling for you, because every architecture is different. For each service, list what it needs to start and run. Leave out services it merely talks to during a workflow; that is dynamic coupling. Tick what your service depends on.

Teams that already build environments by automation can have the same scripts emit this inventory as a by-product.

3

Rate each option alone, then line them up

The saga ratings in Chapter 12 were produced one pattern at a time and only then put side by side. That is when patterns show up. Plot any two characteristics of the eight sagas against each other.

4

Qualitative, but comparing like with like

Almost every table in the book is qualitative. Two architectures are never similar enough for honest numbers, but looking at many examples supports a fair "better" or "worse". Get good at that, because real quantitative comparisons are rare. What you must get right is what you compare. Borrow the strategy consultants' MECE test: options should be mutually exclusive (no overlap) and collectively exhaustive (no obvious gaps). Pick a set of candidates for "high-volume messaging".

5

The out-of-context trap

An architect weighs a shared service against a shared library for common code. Generic research gives a tidy matrix, and the library wins on count. Now add the actual context. (Scores here are my illustration.)

Fewer options, better decisionsFinding the right context usually removes most of the choices. Count the criteria that matter here, not all criteria that exist.
6

Model the cases your domain actually has

One payment service, or one per payment type? Chapter 7's integrators and disintegrators help in general. Modeling concrete scenarios shows where the real trade-off is.

7

Prefer a bottom line to a pile of evidence

Having researched everything, you want to show everything. Don't. Boil it down to the one or two choices a stakeholder can actually weigh. Example: a credit-card application handled by an orchestrator, called either synchronously or through a queue. Tap the domain scenarios that matter to your stakeholders, and the page drafts the question you would put to them.

8

Avoid snake oil and evangelism

Enthusiasm inflates the good parts and hides the bad. Architects cannot afford that. When someone pitches a tool or approach as all upside, ask for the downside. Tap each pitch to find the trade-off it leaves out.

A worked example: an auction system broadcasts bids on one topic. A new consumer, bid history, is needed. The evangelist says "pub/sub, obviously; it's extensible". Model it both ways.

The same applies when you are pushed into being the opposing evangelist. Do not argue against a fan's favorite on principle; turn the debate back into a trade-off, agree what evidence would settle it, and measure.

Sysops Squad: how it ended

Sysops SquadBack in the room where the business sponsors once nearly shut down the support-contract line, the numbers are good: tickets get assigned and closed, customers are happier, the business line survives. The architects credit one thing above any pattern: the application developers and the database team stopped working apart and made decisions together, writing down the trade-offs each time.
