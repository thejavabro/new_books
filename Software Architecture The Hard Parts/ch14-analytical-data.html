Managing Analytical Data

Operational data runs the business; analytical data steers it. Three generations of analytics architecture, ending with the data mesh and its data product quantum.

1

Analytical data has a different job

Most of the book is about operational data: the transactions that keep the business running. Analytical data answers questions about the business: trends, forecasts, reports. The two need different shapes, and the way architects have handled the split has followed architecture fashion. Monoliths kept everything together; then came the warehouse, then the lake, and now the mesh. Pick an era to see where data flows and who has to understand it.

2

Facts and dimensions

Warehouses typically reshape data into a star schema: a central table of facts (measurable numbers) surrounded by dimensions (the things you slice those numbers by). It is denormalized on purpose, so queries need fewer joins and aggregations run fast. Sort these Sysops Squad items: tap once for fact, twice for dimension.

The trouble is what the transformation does to knowledge. The schema is shaped for queries, not for any domain, so the people who understand tickets live in one place and the people who build reports in another. Every report needs both.

3

Why warehouses and lakes disappointed

Integration brittlenessTransform-on-load couples the warehouse to every upstream schema. A change in one domain breaks the pipeline.
Domain knowledge splitDomain experts build systems; separate analysts build reports. Neither has the full picture.
ComplexityA second schema plus ingestion and transform machinery, run as a project outside normal operations.
Value vs effortMany warehouses never paid back what they cost to build and maintain.
Synchronization bottleneckIndependent data streams have to converge in one place and one team.
Lake: hard discoveryRaw dumps lose the relationships that gave the data meaning; consumers must rediscover them.
Lake: privacyCombining raw data sets can reveal personal information no single source exposed.
Both: technical partitioningData cut away from its domain, while the rest of architecture moved toward domain partitioning.

The data product quantum

The architectural unit is the data product quantum (DPQ). It sits beside a service, owned by the same team, with its own code and data, and serves that domain's analytical data to the rest of the organization. It is a separate deployable, but a cooperative one: it talks to its service asynchronously with eventual consistency, has a tight contract with that service, and a looser one with outside consumers. Tap a DPQ.

How it fits the quantum modelBecause analytics is a required feature, the DPQ belongs to its service's static coupling. Dynamically, it should use a pattern that is both asynchronous and eventually consistent: in Chapter 12's terms, a Parallel or Anthology saga, so analytics never slows down operations.

Data mesh suits

  • Microservices and other well-isolated architectures
  • Modern engineering practice
  • Strong decoupling of analytical from operational data
  • Evolving analytics through careful, loose contracts

It requires

  • Contract coordination with every DPQ
  • Asynchronous messaging and acceptance of eventual consistency, hard where analytics must always match operations
6

Sysops Squad: the Expert Supply DPQ

Sysops SquadAfter trying a warehouse, the team moves to a mesh. A Tickets DPQ serves the long-term history of tickets; other DPQs supply daily snapshots about experts. A new aggregate, Expert Supply, predicts whether there will be enough experts with the right skills in each area. A federated governance group of product owners plus security, legal and compliance people decides what every DPQ must standardize.

Trend analysis is ruined by half-days. The fitness function: watch message timestamps; with normal volume, any gap longer than a minute means the feed broke, and the whole day is marked exempt. Tap days to inject an outage.

ADR

Expert Supply sources deliver a whole day or nothing

Decision

Each source feed provides complete daily snapshots, or no data for that day so data scientists can exclude it. Keep source-to-DPQ contracts loose to avoid brittleness.

Consequences

Too many exempt days will erode trend accuracy. Fitness functions: the timestamp-gap check above, and a consumer-driven contract between the Tickets DPQ and Expert Supply.
