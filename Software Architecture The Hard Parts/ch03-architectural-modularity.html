Architectural Modularity

Modularity is not a goal in itself. Breaking a monolith apart only pays off when it buys specific characteristics the business needs, and you should be able to say which.

1

"Nothing else works" is not a business case

The Sysops Squad sponsors are furious: endless patches, endless breakage. The architects want to break the monolith apart, but when the lead architect asks what assurance do you have that this will help?, they have none. Frustration is not a justification. To get funding you must know what modularity actually buys, match those benefits to the real problems, and document the trade-offs.

Business and technology both change constantly: mergers bring sudden new users, competitors force fast pivots, and cloud, containers and DevOps keep reshaping the platform. Architecture is meant to be the stable foundation, yet it has to keep adapting. Here is the analogy the authors suggest using with executives.

2

The modularity drivers

Don't break a system apart without clear business drivers. The two main ones are speed-to-market and competitive advantage. Speed-to-market comes from agility, a composite of maintainability, testability and deployability. Competitive advantage adds scalability and availability on top. Tap a box to trace its path.

Modular does not have to mean distributedMaintainability, testability and deployability can also be had from a modular monolith (components grouped by domain) or a microkernel (functionality in plug-ins). Scalability and fault tolerance at the domain or function level are where distribution earns its keep.
3

Maintainability: how far does one change reach?

Maintainability is the ease of adding, changing or removing features and of applying patches and upgrades. Useful proxies: component coupling (especially incoming), cohesion, cyclomatic complexity, size, and whether components are partitioned by technical layer or by domain. Take one small requirement: add an expiry date to wish list items. Compare the change scope.

4

Testability and deployability shrink as services talk

Smaller deployment units mean smaller, more complete test suites and less risky, less ceremonial deploys. But the benefit evaporates as services call each other. Add communication links, then change a service and see how much has to be retested and redeployed together.

Tap a service to change it.
The big ball of distributed mudAs Matt Stine put it, if your microservices must be deployed as a complete set in a specific order, put them back in a monolith and save yourself the pain. Deployability is ease, frequency and risk together; releasing every two weeks or less often batches changes and raises risk.
5

Scalability is not elasticity

Scalability: staying responsive as load grows gradually over months. Elasticity: staying responsive through a sudden spike, like concert tickets going on sale and users jumping from 20 to 3,000 in seconds. Elasticity depends on a small mean time to startup (MTTS), which needs small services. Scalability depends more on modularity, elasticity more on granularity.

Availability and fault tolerance

Here fault tolerance means some parts stay responsive while others fail. If payment processing runs out of memory, customers should still be able to search and place orders. Load-balancing several copies of a monolith is costly and doesn't help when the fault is a bug, because every copy has it. Break something and see.

Sysops Squad: build the business case

The architects take each complaint and match it to a driver. Do the same.

Which driver addresses it?

ADR

Migrate the Sysops Squad application to a distributed architecture

Context

One monolith handles registration, tickets, reporting, billing and admin, with problems in scalability, availability and maintainability.

Decision

Move to a distributed architecture: isolate core ticketing from failures, scale ticket creation, move reporting load off the shared database, and ship features and fixes faster with fewer bugs.

Consequences

New features slow down while developers work on the migration; extra cost; release engineers juggle many deployment units until the pipeline catches up; and the monolithic database must be broken apart too.
